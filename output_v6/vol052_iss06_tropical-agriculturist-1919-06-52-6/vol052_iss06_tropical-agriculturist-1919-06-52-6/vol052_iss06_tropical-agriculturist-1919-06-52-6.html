SUPPLIES OF SEED PADDY.

In view of the shortage of rice in the Colony at the present time and the consequent high prices ruling for paddy, it is essential that some thought should be given to the reservation of stocks of seed paddy for the next sowing season.

In certain parts of Uva, there was actually a shortage of seed for sowing for the last crop, and the Chief Headmen had to take steps to secure seed for loan to the village cultivators. The villagers owing to scarcity and high prices had been obliged to consume the whole of their stocks, and had no provision for seed for the next sowing.

What has occurred in one locality may equally well occur in others, and therefore it seems essential that steps should be taken at an early date to see that adequate reserves are kept in stock in every village. Holders of paddy may be tempted, owing to the high prices prevailing, to sell the whole of their stocks or they may, particularly in the poorer districts, be tempted to consume their whole supplies.

Every village should therefore take stock, and undertake that adequate reserves are in sight for the next sowing season.

The importance of this question has been impressed upon all members of Co-operative Credit Societies assembled during the past two months for general annual meetings, and a circular printed at the end of this article, has been distributed in the

304[JUNE, 1919.

Vernaculars by authority of Government to all Food Production Committees for circulation amongst agriculturists.

All Agricultural Instructors have been informed of the importance of impressing upon cultivators, the absolute necessity of having adequate stocks in hand.

One Co-operative Credit Society in the Northern Province has made arrangements for the collection and storage of seed paddy for distribution to its members, when the next sowing season arrives. Other Societies may follow this example particularly in those areas where only one crop can be raised per annum.

There are evidences in every district of the Colony that increased attention is being given to paddy cultivation. The high prices have induced many cultivators to pay attention to lands which have been idle for some time. The efforts of Revenue Officers and their Headmen, as also the efforts of Food Production Committees, are beginning to result in greater attention to cultivation. It is true that there have been many disappointments, and influenza has been responsible for many fields not having received attention. But progress has been made.

There is no doubt, however, that preparations must now be begun for the next North-East or Maha season. A general campaign throughout the whole country for greater sowing and planting during the next season should be started without delay and provision made that there will be adequate stocks of seed on hand. Food Production Committees can do much in this direction. Much has been accomplished in the past, but greater efforts in the future are essential.

Agriculturists throughout every district must realize that upon their efforts depend the welfare of the Colony. All and everyone should plant something and every assistance possible will be given to cultivators by Agricultural Officers to further the production of greater quantities of foodstuffs in the Colony.

Food Production Circular No. 1.

SEED PADDY FOR NEXT SEASON.

It is essential that the attention of all cultivators should be directed at the present moment to the absolute necessity of saving sufficient seed paddy for the next main sowing season. There is a danger that with high prices for paddy, cultivators may be tempted to eat or to dispose of the whole of their stocks, taking no thought of the next season.

It is the object of this Circular to impress upon all, the absolute necessity of a sufficient stock of grain, being reserved for the next sowing season. Every Village Committee should see that sufficient stocks are visible. Every landowner, middleman, and cultivator should feel that he has a duty to see that there is seed grain available for sowing all his lands. Now is the time to take stock and to decide upon what quantity must be reserved.

It is not advisable in the present state of our knowledge to move seed paddy from one district to another, except on an experimental scale. Soil and climate have such influences upon varieties of paddy, that it is more than probable that there are large numbers of names of paddies in different districts relating to one real botanical variety. Trials to move seed paddy from one district to another have not been wholly successful. There have been some complete failures.

It therefore seems essential that every Headman's Division should reserve its own seed supply of those varieties that are usually grown in the Division.

In case it becomes necessary to secure seed paddy from another district, the Department of Agriculture should be consulted.

THE SWEET POTATO.

Of the several foodcrops recommended to be extensively grown to meet a possible shortage of food, sweet potato is about the most well-known and most popular crop. It is already fairly extensively grown in the villages, but not so on estate coolie gardens. The object of this article is to encourage its cultivation on an extended scale both in estate gardens and villages.

Sweet potato is accepted to be a nutritious food. It is easily cultivated. It is a quick-grower, yielding large crops—10 to 12,000 lb. per acre. In Ceylon it can be cultivated anywhere. It is a pretty hard crop, less subject to disease than many a crop. Being appreciated by all classes, there is a ready sale. At the present time a pound is sold at five cents in the Kandy market. It is eaten in a variety of ways. It is boiled and sold in the streets. Is cooked as a curry in several forms. Sliced and dried, it can be preserved for months together. It even has taken the place of English potato.

There are several varieties, and recently several excellent varieties have been introduced from Mauritius. The most extensively grown varieties are the white and red-coloured potatoes. The cluster sweet potato, also introduced not very long ago is quickly replacing the common varieties. Those who are trying sweet potatoes as a new crop should obtain cuttings of the Mauritius varieties or the cluster variety.

The best soil for the crop is a light sandy or loose loam. Clay, stiff and wet soils are unsuitable. Very rich soils are inclined to produce a large quantity of vines and leaf.

The land for sweet potatoes must be worked fairly deep. The deeper it is worked the larger the out-turn. Though hardly any special manuring is necessary, a free application of well-rotted cattle manure will be very beneficial. If sweet potatoes are grown on land heavily manured for the previous crop, it is all the better.

Propagation is usually by cuttings. These should be 9 to 10 inches long and buried rather deep in the soil. The best time to plant is with the early rains. If cuttings are not available, plant a few tubers of a medium size and of a short compact shape. These will send up shoots from which cuttings can be taken to start a plantation.

Cuttings are better planted on ridges, raised about 9 inches high and about a foot broad. The ridges should be at distances of 2 feet apart. Cuttings should be put at a distances of 10 to 12 inches, and the soil well firmed around the cuttings.

The cuttings will strike root easily in about a week and after that the growth will be vigorous. If planting has to be done in dry weather, it will be found advantageous to dip the cuttings in a thick paste of fresh cow-dung and soil, and after planting, it will be necessary to water for a day or two.

When the plants have begun to grow, very little attention is necessary. The ridges should be kept free of weeds.

The crop is ready for harvesting when the leaves begin to turn yellow. There are early and late varieties. Some take 3 months others 4, and some varieties even 6 months. Before raising the crop, cut away all the vines and lift the potatoes by digging them out.

The crop can be kept for some time by adopting the following method of preserving: Put down a thick layer of dry sand and place a layer of tubers, then run in sand until all the crevices are filled in and the tubers are covered. Keep another layer and repeat the process until as often as tubers are kept. Cover the whole with some straw or dry material.

W. MOLEGODE, S.A.I.

TOMATO GROWING.

The Tomato is one of the most popular vegetables with all classes and sells readily; fetching at times very good prices—20 to 25 cents per lb. It is a quick-yielding vegetable—the fruit beginning to ripen in about 10 weeks. It can be very extensively grown in Ceylon being suited to all elevations. Excellent tomatoes have been grown in the Residency Gardens at Puttalam by Mrs. W. E. WAIT—perhaps one of the worst places in the Island, where conditions for vegetable growing are far from favourable. Badulla is famous for tomatoes, and the largest specimens I have seen, were produced here. A large quantity is also grown in Dumbara and Hewaheta. At the recent village shows in Matale district there were exhibited some very good fruit locally grown. But such a popular vegetable that is always in demand is not grown sufficiently extensively in Ceylon, nor do the village cultivators pay attention to improving the varieties commonly grown.

VARIETIES.

Cultivators should aim at producing fruit of medium size, well shaped and with a smooth skin. There are a large number of varieties to select from, but I have found Brunning's "Large Red" and "Chalk's—Early Jewel" to be suitable and popular.

SOIL.

Almost any kind of soil, provided it is well drained, can be made to grow tomatoes. Low wet soils should be avoided, and where it is available a loose mellow soil with a fair proportion of sand should be selected as being about the most suited soil.

PREPARATION OF LAND AND MANURE.

The ground should be well prepared, dug fairly deep and all weeds and vegetable matter turned under the surface and the soil reduced to as fine a tilth as possible. If the soil is rather stiff, it can be improved by adding some sand. Well-rotted farm-yard manure with some lime added to it, will give good results.

PROPAGATION AND NURSERY BEDS.

Propagation is by seed. \frac{1}{2}-lb. of seed will give more than sufficient number of seedlings to plant an acre. Raise seedlings in nursery beds, or if in small quantity, in boxes. The soil for the nurseries should be made very fine, loose and mellow for a depth of about 5 to 6 inches. A dressing of well-rotted cattle or horse manure mixed with lime should be thoroughly incorporated into the soil.

The seed should be sown thinly in drills, 3 inches apart and watered and shaded carefully. Heavy watering and a crowded seed-bed will effect the seedling and kill many of them.

PLANTING

Tomatoes should be planted in rows or preferably on ridges, 4 feet apart and the distance in the rows should be about 3 feet. Only good healthy seedlings should be transplanted, and the soil made firm around the plants. After transplanting, water thoroughly, if in dry weather, shade for 2 or 3 days. After the plants have stuck root, remove the shade.

AFTER CULTIVATION

Tomatoes are either run on trellis placed 3 or 4 feet above ground or are tied to poles about 6 feet high firmly fixed close to the plant. A good thing is to erect an arbour over the space left between the rows and train the plants on to them. Nip out the side branches or offshoots until the plants have well set on the trellis, or have reached the top of the poles. In the latter case when the plants have reached the top, nip off the last bud and allow offshoots to grow.

When carrying a heavy crop, it will be well to mulch around the roots. Weed and hoe the soil frequently and water when necessary.

HARVESTING.

Those who are accustomed to visit the Kandy market on Friday morning would have observed that practically all the tomatoes that come in are too green. Tomatoes picked green never ripen sufficiently or satisfactorily, to be fit for eating. Nor should fruit be allowed too long on the trees to ripen because transport of fully-ripe tomatoes result in the greater number of them being spoilt. The proper time to pick is when the fruit have reached full maturity and begin to change from green colour to the white stage of ripeness. In plucking the larger fruit take them off with a bit of stalk attached.

SEED.

Leave the best fruit for seed. The seed is obtained by washing it out in a basin. Spread out the seed to dry and keep in a cool, dry place in an airtight bottle until required.

DISEASES.

The sleepy disease or the droop or wilt disease is very common in tomato. The roots are first attacked and then the leaves begin to droop and the plants die. As soon as the disease appears, all diseased plants should be removed and burnt. Plenty of lime should be applied to the soil, and do not attempt to grow tomatoes in the same soil for some years.

Tomatoes if grown two or three years in the same land, soon deteriorate and becomes easily liable to disease.

As a substitute for wheaten flour, plantain flour is probably one that will be made the greatest use of as it is the cheapest to produce. We have also a number of other starchy foods that can be used, some of which will be described in detail.

Plantain flour can be made from any kind of plantain, although it is generally recommended to use those plantains such as the "Red fig" which at the present time are scarcely used at all in Trinidad. On some large estates quantities of this plantain can be seen. From a bunch of silk figs the hands were cut off and weighed; they yielded 26.19 per cent. of flour.

When cutting the plantains, it is better to cut them before they are quite full than to leave them till they are beginning to ripen, as if too full, they will, after slicing, ripen in the sun, be very difficult to dry and produce a sweet flour. The process of pealing and slicing is a simple one, and can be done most economically by boy labour, the boys becoming quite expert and performing the work quickly and well after a few bunches have been done. Steel knives should not be used on the green fruit as they blacken them unless immersed under water the whole time. A nickel or fruit knife is the best tool to use.

When slicing the plantains the thinner they can be cut the better, as it is essential for them to dry quickly. In the dry season no trouble is experienced, and they will dry thoroughly in a couple of days if spread on wooden trays in the blazing sun. In the rainy season it is a different matter and it is best to choose a fine morning for the work, and if a shower comes on remove them to a dry warm room. A drying room with hot air going through it would be necessary on a large scale, but when doing a few bunches for the home, this is not needed as they can always be dried in trays on a warm grate. As soon as the chips are dry they are ready for milling. On a small scale they may be pounded, grated, or better still ground in an ordinary corn-mill which can be bought from about $2. upward. After buying the plantains and paying for the labour, the flour can be produced for about three cents a pound; therefore for those who grow their own plantains and use their own labour, the cost is practically nil.

The uses of plantain flour are many and varied: It would be as well here to mention that as a food substitute plantain flour is most valuable, it being very digestible; so much so, in fact, that well-known medical authorities have recommended its use for the feeding of children and invalids; we need therefore have no fear of bad consequences to our health by using plantain flour.

Plantain bread is best made from a mixture of wheat flour and plantain flour, but in what proportion is a question for the consumer to judge for himself. The addition of a certain proportion of wheat flour is necessary to give the bread the proper consistency.

SELECTION OF SEED.

Soil, Climate, and the length of the growing season vary so much in the different countries and in portions of the same country, that it is very important that corn should be bred up in each neighbourhood. If we bring seed of the best corn from another country and plant it directly in our fields, we shall not get a maximum yield the first year because the seed was not acclimatized, nor was it "at home" in our soil. If, however, we select the best ears from the best individual stalks of those grown the first year and plant kernels from them in our field the next, we shall get a better crop of corn than we did the first year because the seed used in the second planting is more accustomed to our soil and climate; if we keep selecting the seed ears from the best plants each year, after a while we shall have produced a variety of corn that is suited to the Philippines and will produce more per hectare than any other kind. In a like manner if we select our seed ears from the varieties of corn at present growing in the Islands and use them for our next seed, we shall get a larger yield than if we had not selected the seed.

Any planter who produces a strain of corn that is well adapted to these Islands will be able to sell good seed at a price profitable to his neighbour as well as to himself; and in so doing, he will be a benefactor to his locality, for he will be increasing the production of corn therein.

The main object in selecting seed corn is to produce more and better shelled corn per hectare than has ever before been produced. The most desirable type of corn for commercial growing in the Philippines is one that is a good yielder and very flinty—the more so the better—as this is not easily attacked by weevils, the principal enemy of the corn-grower in this country, and makes more "rice" and less flour.

The seed (kernel).—A kernel of corn contains a very small germ which when planted, will make a stalk of corn. If the seed we use is not the best, in a short time the strain will "run out" and we shall harvest a small yield. The stalks will be scrubby and yellow with only undeveloped ears on them. Then, too, unless we select good ears for seed, disease and insect enemies will develop to such an extent that corn-growing will become unprofitable.

A good stalk of corn.—Since the stalk is the individual, it is therefore necessary to select seed ears from stalks that are well developed, and this can be done only by selecting from standing stalks in the field at ripening time. Thus we are able to select seed corn from stalks that have produced heavily under average field conditions and in competition with other stalks in the same field.

A good stalk of corn should be without suckers, thick at the base, with well-developed roots, and gradually tapering towards the top. It should be free from "smut" or other diseases and should have from ten to sixteen well-formed blades, depending on the variety, and two good ears each attached by an ear stalk or "shank" about 8 centimeters in length. The ears should be well covered by the husk, to guard against weevils, and should point downwards; the lower one borne about one meter from the ground.

A good ear.—The ear should be symmetrical and nearly cylindrical in shape, well rounded at each end. The rows of kernels should be straight and compact, commencing close to the "shank" and extending clear over the end of the cob to the tip. The cob should be neither too large nor too small for the variety. The kernels should fit snugly, and be uniform in size, nearly flat on the sides fairly long, and slightly tapering on both edges ("wedge-shaped").

Selection in the plot.—As soon as the plants begin tasseling, the plot should be carefully inspected and all barren and poor stalks detasseled so that sterility will not be transmitted. Just before harvesting, the plot should be re-inspected to mark the desirable stalks as described above for the purpose of seed selection. When ready for harvest, the good ears, as indicated above, are to be saved for seed.

HARVEST AND CARE OF SEED CORN.

After we have selected our seed corn, the next thing to do is to preserve it until it is ready to be sold or planted. It is very important that all corn which is to be used for seed should be preserved in the best manner possible. Though seed corn may be thoroughly dried, it will absorb moisture again when it comes in contact with a damp atmosphere. When first harvested, seed corn very often contains from 20 to 30 per cent of moisture, and unless it is well dried, it may be ruined in a single day through heating, or, in damp places, molding.

Whenever practicable the seed corn should be allowed to mature and cure on the stalk thoroughly. Then the seed ears are removed from the stalks, with the husks on, and placed in a dry, well-ventilated place where weevils, ants and mice or rats cannot get at them. Where it is not possible to properly dry the seed corn on the stalk, the seed ears should first be spread out in the sun during the middle of the day until properly dried before they are finally stored up.

Storing.—Once dry, the ears should be tied by the husks into bundles of ten or any convenient number. Care should be taken, however, to leave as much as possible of the husk covering each seed ear. These bundles should then be hung on the rafters of the planter's home where they will not get damp, preferably in the kitchen. The smoke from the open stove drives the weevils away. Obviously only a few ears can be kept in this way. Where a large quantity of seed is to be saved, this can be stored advantageously by the halayhay method.

This method of storing seed corn consists in hanging up the unhusked ears systematically by their own stems, tips downward, on a bamboo frame or rack built in the open as follows:—

A number of strong posts, about 5 centimeters in length are set perpendicularly in the ground in a straight row, about 1.5 meters apart from each other. Then bamboo slats about 4 centimeters wide are nailed across horizontally, joining all the posts. The slats are placed one above the other in such a way as to allow the tips of the ears hung on the upper slats to overlap the "shanks" (stems) of those hung on the slats immediately below. The first slat from the ground should be high enough so as to protect the corn from roaming animals. Whenever convenient, slats should be similarly fastened on the opposite side of the posts in order to economise space by thus doubling the capacity of the halayhay.

The base of the posts should be buried deeply, not less than 70 centimeters, so as to make the rack strong. It is oftentimes necessary to provide the rack or halayhay with either guy-wires or braces to prevent it from being blown down by strong winds. The halayhay should be built near a building or some other windbreak, but not under the shade; it should preferably face from east to west, so as to admit as much sunshine as possible.

Corn for storing by the halayhay method should be gathered thus: (a) select the best, well-matured ears (without opening or in any way mutilating the husk) from the best individual stalks; (b) gather the ears so selected by cutting the stalk with a knife or (bolo) about 4 centimeters just below, and about 15 centimeters above the base (node) of the shank. The piece of stalk thus left on the shank forms with this a sort of hook whereby the ear may be hung up. Immediately after harvesting, the ears should be hooked over the bamboo frame, commencing with the lowest slat, and building upwards.

It is obvious that the method of selecting seed corn on the husk is faulty, in that there is possibility of getting ears of imperfect type. However, if proper care is taken in the selection, by picking the largest, best-proportioned, well-covered, well-matured and best-filled ears, as may be determined by the touch and judged from external indications, the number of undesirable ears may be reduced to a minimum. Before planting time, the seed ears should be tested by planting five kernels from the different parts of each in a germination box to determine their viability. The corn should be husked when needed for testing, at which time the final selection is made and the undesirable ears are discarded.

Weevils.—In the Philippines, as in all hot countries, stored grain is always damaged by weevils. These weevils are small insects, having snouts which they use to bore into the kernel of corn for the starch and germ contained therein. Therefore in storing corn, it is very important that farmers should guard against the attack of weevils. These insects can, to a great extent, be held in check by selecting those with husks that fit tightly over the ears and by planting varieties with hard kernels (flint corn). The simplest and most effective method of eradicating them in stored corn, however, is by fumigating with carbon bisulphide, a colourless liquid with a strong, disagreeable odour, which kills the insects. It evaporates very quickly, but is a powerful poison and highly inflammable.

How to apply carbon bisulphide.—The seed corn should be placed in a tight barrel or deep box, which is provided with a hole in the side near the bottom. The hole should be kept plugged during fumigation after which the plug is removed for the gas to escape. About 30 grams of carbon bisulphide, in a saucer or on a piece of cotton, is then placed on top of the corn, and the box or barrel kept tightly covered for six hours.

The carbon bisulphide rapidly volatilizes or turns to gas, and being heavier than air, descends and permeates the mass of corn, killing all insects or vermin present.

ONIONS AND ONION-GROWING.

As the time approaches for preparing for an onion crop, it will not be out of place to give directions for sowing the seed and for the after-cultivation of this most valuable vegetable. There is no reason why onions should be imported in such quantities as we see daily arriving by steamer from the Southern States. The climate here is perfectly adapted to them, and, if only planted on suitable soil and given the necessary attention, heavy crops are an almost certain result. At one time it used to be said that onions could not be profitably grown as a field crop in the Blenheim district, near Laidley. To disprove this, the writer determined to experiment on the sandy loam of the scrub land on Sandy Creek. The seed was sown in April: the variety, Brown Spanish. The land to which they were to be transplanted was well worked and then rolled to make a hard, compact bed. Transplanting as carried on throughout July, and the season having proved favourable, the result was a heavy crop of magnificent bulbs, which gave a splendid return for the labour expended. Now, if such a result can be obtained by one farmer, it follows that others can do the same. As there are thousands of acres of similar land under cultivation both there and in many other portions of the State, it only requires determination on the part of the farmers to enable them to successfully displace the importations from the South.

On looking over an old diary of farm operations, it appears that the writer's crops averaged 6 tons per acre, and sold at £25 per ton. Are there many crops which will give a like return? The present price of onions in the market is about £24 per ton. Now, suppose a crop to yield 8 tons per acre (and we know that double that return has been made), the cash return for a medium crop will amount to £192. Certainly there is a considerable amount of labour involved in planting out an acre or two of onions, but that labour is amply compensated for by the nett return.

A consideration of the following Notes, based on practical experience, may, therefore, be of some service: Let us first consider

THE SOIL.

The most suitable soil for onions is a rich sandy loam, such as that of the Blenheim scrubs-free, friable, and easy to work, a soil that will not cake, and not lying so low as to retain the super-abundant moisture after heavy rains. In such a case the land should be well drained. An eastern or south-eastern aspect has been proved to be better than if the land sloped to the west, as the onion does not require intense heat to bring it to perfection.

Before sowing the seed, it is important that the seed beds should be clear of weeds and of their fallen seeds. By sowing in April or May there is not much to fear from weeds; still, it is advisable that the land, both of the seed beds and of the area proposed to be planted out, should be turned up and exposed to the weather for some time previous to sowing. As soon as the weeds appear, give the land a good scuffling, and if this be done two or three times between March and April there will be no trouble afterwards. If the soil be not virgin scrub, or if it has borne crops for many years in succession without manuring, it should be thoroughly well manured with stable-dung, ashes, bone-dust, etc. as the onion demands plenty and the best of nourishment. New scrub land is rich enough in natural fertilisers not to require any addition of manure.

PREPARING THE SOIL.

In planting out onions, a very serious mistake is often made, and that is, the soil is carefully worked, reduced to a fine tilth, and the plants are set out in a soil which is loosened to a depth of perhaps 8 inches. From land prepared in this manner no good results need be expected. The onion requires a firm bed; otherwise the plant, instead of making a large

316[JUNE, 1919.]

well-shaped bulb, will run to neck, and have more the appearance of a leek than of an onion. Therefore, the land before being planted must be well solidified by rolling.

THE SEED BED.

Onions may be sown broadcast, or they may be drilled in, or they may be sown in a seed bed and afterwards planted out in the same manner as cabbages. The best way is drill them in. In this case, about 2 lb. of seed per acre will be required. The seed must be dropped at a distance of about 2 inches apart in the drill, and the drills should be from 12 inches to 15 inches distant from each other. The plants will afterwards require thinning-out with the hoe. When sown in seed bed, planting out must be resorted to—a tedious process, but one that pays well for doing well.

On rich soil the plants may be 6 inches apart. The drills should be slightly raised, and the roots of the plant firmly embedded in it—allow the bulb to, as it were, squat on, not under the surface. As the plant grows, the soil must be kept perfectly clear of weeds, and, where the working of the ground has thrown the soil against the bulbs, it must be drawn down, so that only the root is in the ground. Where this has not been attended to, the remedy for the resulting want of bulb formation is to wring the necks of the plants, or, at least, to bend them down with a twist. This will have the effect of inducing the formation of bulbs.

When sowing the seed, care should be taken that they are not covered to more than their own depth. If sown deep, many seed fail to germinate, and most of those that do appear will make an abnormal growth of neck, causing much labour in drawing away the soil from the incipient bulbs. The writer has never sown onions broadcast, and therefore offers no opinion on the value of the method. Of course more seed would be required per acre, and, if weeds are troublesome, a good deal of hand work would be necessary.

Now, about the seed : There are few seed so annoyingly deceptive as onion seed. So difficult was it to get good seed in the State even at 10s. per lb. in the good old days, that growers imported it from Spain. The largest growers at Oxley, Messrs. MARTINDALE & NOSWORTHY, were most successful with imported seed, but the writer had a very bad experience in this business. Twenty pounds weight of onion seed was sent from a friend in Germany. Instead of packing it in hermetically-sealed bottles, he stowed it in calico bags in the body of an immense wooden Swiss cuckoo clock. When the clock was opened the bags of seed were examined and looked perfect in colour and shape ; but, alas ! When subjected to pressure, no oily fatness was perceived ; and when at last it was given a chance and sown thickly in drills, five acres returned the magnificent yield of 72 lb. which, at the rate of £28 per ton, amounted to 18s. Certainly, the land, after a week's waiting for the seed to germinate, was utilised otherwise, but not 1 cwt. of onion was harvested.

Make sure, therefore, of the seed. After sowing, it should germinate in less than a week.

In former days large onions were always aimed at, but now the public taste is in favour of medium-sized bulbs, so that closer planting may be adopted.

Onions may be known to be ripe by the drying up of the tops. As soon as this happens, take them up by hand and leave them on the ground between the rows to dry. As soon as they are dry, carry them carefully with as little bruising as possible to the barn.

As before stated, the Brown Spanish has proved most successful in this State, but the gentlemen above-mentioned grew what they called the large White Portugal onion. It certainly was a splendid bulb, and fetched very high prices in the markets of Brisbane, Maryborough, and Rockhampton.—

The cultivation of manioc (Manihot utilisima Pohl), from whose starchy root tapioca is produced, has only recently been developed in the Dutch East Indies, although, already in 1854, official initiatives were taken. The aim of the Government was to provide the native with an "auxiliary foodstuff" with the object of guarding against the lack of ordinary foods, especially rice, in time of famine. The population required several years in order to appreciate the utility of this crop, which now is fairly well diffused. PLANT PESTS & DISEASES.

BROWN BAST.

(Department of Agriculture, Ceylon.—Leaflet No. 12.)

BROWN BAST is a disease which attacks the cortex of the rubber tree Hevea), practically always on tapped trees. Instances of its occurrence on untapped trees have been recorded, but they appear to be very rare.

The first symptom of the disease is that the tree becomes "dry," the tapping cut ceasing to yield latex. Sometimes only part of the cut goes dry, and in such cases the latex often coagulates, when it first meets the dry part and blocks the cut, so that the latex which exudes later, runs down the stem instead of flowing down the cut to the vertical channel.

Examination of the affected cut shows that the part which is not yielding latex is grayish-yellow, and dotted with pale-brown, or brownish-yellow spots and streaks. It may also be somewhat sodden and watery, and exude a watery fluid when pricked. In well-defined cases, a continuous brownish line is found running along the cut very near the cambium. Behind this line the cortex still yields latex, and if it is pricked, latex will issue from this inner layer; hence the fact that a tree yields latex when pricked is not evidence that it is not attacked by Brown Bast. This latex, however, lies too deep, as a rule, to be obtained by ordinary tapping. Brown Bast should be suspected, if the cooly has to tap deeper than usual to obtain latex.

When the cortex beneath the tapping cut is scraped or pared away, it is found to be yellowish-gray, with grayish streaks and patches, and, here and there, red-brown streaks spots. No latex exudes until the scraping reaches to about two millimetres from the cambium.

The disease may begin at or below the tapping cut, or at the collar. In Ceylon it appears that it most usually begins below the tapping cut, and that the cooly cuts into the diseased patch as tapping proceeds downwards.

If left untreated, the disease may take either of two courses: In the one case all the diseased tissue external to the continuous brown line dries up and is cut off as a thick, usually hard, scale, leaving only a thin layer of laticiferous cortex, one or two millimetres thick, overlying the cambium. When the scale is prized off, the underlying layer appears to be healthy, but on scraping it, one finds that it is speckled internally. Subsequently, as it grows in thickness, it develops nodules, and the tree becomes untappable. The dead scales are at first usually very hard and brittle, but they become soft and friable later.

In the other case no dead scale is formed, but the nodules are produced in or just beneath the diseased cortex.

The final result of an attack of Brown Bast is the production of nodules. It does not, however, follow that all nodules are the result of Brown Bast.

334[JUNE, 1919.

Nodules are formed round abnormal regions in the cortex. One of the causes of such abnormal regions is the disease known as Brown Bast. Hence nodules succeed an attack of Brown Bast. They are a consequence, or a secondary feature, of Brown Bast, although, if it were not for their development, the disease would not be so serious.

Brown Bast may begin at the collar, or in the lower part of the stem, and extend downwards along the tap root for a length of two feet or more, and also along the lateral roots. It is often confined to the part of the circumference which is being tapped, and usually does not cross the vertical channel.

There are no indications of the disease in its earliest stages. Sometimes the latex becomes very thick and coagulates on the cut. The first indication is usually the stoppage of the flow of latex; but this should not be accepted as a sign of Brown Bast, unless the cortex is discoloured. After scales have formed, they may crack longitudinally, but, as a rule, they do not fall off.

Three methods of treatment have been adopted for Brown Bast: They all have the same aims, viz., the removal, or destruction, of the diseased cortex to such a depth that any diseased tissue is destroyed and the formation of nodules prevented.

STRIPPING.

This method is officially recommended in Malaya. The whole of the affected cortex is stripped off, down to the wood.

Ascertain by light scraping how far the disease extends. With a sharp knife isolate this area with vertical and horizontal cuts, which extend to the cambium, including a margin about one inch wide of healthy tissue.

Shave the isolated area to about half the thickness of the cortex, collecting and burning all shavings. Carefully lever up the lower edge with a knife, taking care to press on the cambium as little as possible, and then take hold of the free edge and peel off the whole of the shaved cortex.

The stripped surface must not be touched, nor exposed to direct sunlight, nor to rain. Hence the operator should work under the shelter of a screen, and the stripped surface must be protected from sunlight and rain by cadjan or other screens.

Various methods of screening the stripped surface are in use: Whatever screen is used, it must not touch the stripped surface. Cadjan screens, or grass screens braced with bamboo, about three feet by two feet, are convenient. Two of these should be leant overlapping against the tree, with the lower edge on the ground about a foot from the trunk and tied to the stem by a cord round the top. To prevent rain water running down the stem over the stripped surface, a piece of cloth, dipped in paraffin wax, is placed round the junction of the screen with the tree, where it should adhere by means of the wax. It is necessary, for good renewal, to admit light, but not direct sunlight. Hence a third screen is laid against the tree, beneath the other two, and is withdrawn daily, to admit light, for a few hours.

White wax cloth aprons protect the stripped surface and admit light but are expensive.

JUNÉ, 1919.]335

In about ten days, the stripped area will be covered with a layer of renewed bark, and the screen may then be removed.

Stripping cannot be done when the tree is wintering, and it should not be done during dry weather or heavy rains.

SCRAPING.

The diseased cortex is shaved until latex begins to exude, and is then painted with Brunolinum Plantarium. As the scraping will, in general, leave some of the diseased tissue remaining, the Brunolinum should be used as strong as possible. The strength which can be used without causing wounds must be determined by trial; estates apparently differ in this respect. Twenty per cent. should be tried first. If this causes wounds when applied immediately after scraping, application on the day after scraping should be tried. If the twenty per cent. solution does not cause wounds, the strength should be increased. Fifty per cent. solution has been used without injury.

When the cortex dies back after scraping and application of Brunolinum, it does not necessarily follow that the death of the cortex is due to the Brunolinum. One or two trees should be treated by scraping only, to see what happens when the Brunolinum is not applied. It must also be borne in mind that it will not be possible in all cases to make a diseased tree "as good as new."

TARRING.

The following method has been reported to give favourable results in Java:—

The extent of the diseased patch is ascertained by high scraping, and it is then isolated by deep horizontal and vertical channels, including a margin of an inch or two of healthy bark. The patch is then shaved to half the thickness of the cortex, and painted with hot coal tar, heated until it begins to bubble.

If the disease extends into the renewing cortex, this should have the outer brown layer only removed, and then tarred. To avoid wounds, cold tar should be used for renewing bark.

In cases where a thick brown scale has formed, the scale should be prized off. If the stripping method is used, the underlying layer is stripped in the usual way; but if scraping methods are being used, this layer should be treated with Brunolinum, fifty per cent. if possible, without scraping.

In old cases, where nodules have already formed, the nodules should be cut out in the usual manner, and the wounds painted with twenty per cent. Brunolinum Plantarium, or tarred. In such cases wounds will inevitably be caused, where the nodules have become united to the wood.

The whole of the area affected with Brown Bast must be treated, including the tap root and the lateral roots. Where the roots have been treated, the holes must be left open and drained so that water does not lodge against the treated patches. If they can be spared, trees which are badly affected on the roots, should be cut out.

TEA TORTRIX.

PRELIMINARY REPORT ON THE SPRAYING EXPERIMENTS.

The endeavour of the Tortrix Investigation has been to find a practically useful wash which will kill the Tortrix and make the tea bushes distasteful to the insect; the proposed scheme of spraying is part of the remedial and preventive measures suggested in Bulletin 40; the main suggestion was to have a series of Wind Belts or Tortrix Flight Breaks of leguminous trees protecting those ridges exposed to the South-West Monsoon, which is responsible for the wholesale distribution of the pest. These flight breaks may be either Dadaps, Grevilleas, Acacias or Albizzias according to the growing capacity of the plants in the particular districts; or two adjacent rows of tea allowed to grow into seed-bearers. These flight breaks were the main scheme of prevention, for they will check the distribution of the pest, insure the plants to some extent from infection from his neighbour and will prove a local breeding ground for the pest. Let the pest come and establish itself in the flight breaks, and it will be simple to kill it out cheaply with sprays.

When these flight breaks are in use, tea must be protected from attack emanating from the flight breaks, for there is a big chance that after overcrowding in the flight breaks, the insect will attack the adjacent tea.

The investigation has been established in Maskeliya for five months experimenting, to prove the efficiency of a particular chemical which may be based on tea with no harmful effect to the marketable product or on the flushing of the bush, but which will kill the insect and make the bush distasteful to it. As the investigation was satisfied by preliminary experiments and previous experience of the chemical with a similar tortrix, that the wash would kill the insect, the Maskeliya experiments were instituted to prove beyond doubt that the application of the chemical had no deleterious effect upon the flushing capacity of the bush or upon the made tea.

Before explaining these experiments, I would point out how useful such a wash will be in the general scheme of prevention and remedy. R. J. H. HOPE, R.E.

It is recognised throughout the whole world that Great Britain possesses in its Tropical plantations a most valuable asset : The greater part of the planted rubber area lies in British Possessions, and it must also be observed that a fair proportion of the area under rubber in the Dutch Colonies is owned by British investors and controlled by companies registered in this country. It so happens that Hevea Brasiliensis can only be grown in hot, moist climates ; unfortunately extensions have taken place over very wide areas on which there are now very few forms of vegetation other than this ideal plant for cultivation. The existence of huge stretches of the same species renders the individual plants liable to the attacks of dangerous fungi. A fungus once it has adapted itself to its host, will always be certain of finding plenty of food from the same species, and a continuous life is therefore assured to any fungus, if no attempt is made to destroy it.

DISEASES ALREADY KNOWN.

During the last few years without exception every island or territory on which Hevea Brasiliensis is cultivated, has reported the existence of parasitic fungi capable of doing enormous damage in a very short time. The fungus which has probably caused most trouble throughout the area is Phytophthora faberi.—Maubl., and possibly another species of the same genus. In different countries the effect of Phytophthora has been variously described, the names : "Black Thread," "Stripe Canker," "Bark Rot," "Bark Canker," "Black Line Canker" and others, having been applied to affections of the bark specifically attributed to one of more species of this devastating fungus. The bark of rubber trees is also affected with a disease commonly described as "Brown Bast," which unfortunately, was at one time referred to as "Patch Canker." There is also another most serious disease known as the "Pink Disease," which particularly affects the angles formed by branches and also the bark on other parts of the tree.

Bark diseases of a somewhat similar type have been known to affect cacao plants throughout the Tropics, and, guided by the results of experiments with bark diseases on cacao, many planters believe that the only effective remedy for such diseases is the free use of the knife. Now it so happens that the cacao tree may have its stem hacked about without the fruit crop—the source of revenue—being materially affected. With rubber trees the very opposite is the case. The bark is the mother of rubber. If it has to be destroyed by the process of excision, a future source of rubber is lost to the estate. It is admitted and, in the interests of the industry, recommended, that in severe cases the diseased bark should be cut away and burnt. But, on the other hand, knowing that the bark is the one tissue to preserve, one must aim at finding some re-agent which will kill the fungus without destroying the whole of the bark from which rubber is obtained. OF FUNGUS DISEASES.

A. C. TUNSTALL, B.Sc.

Just before the war, many gardens were making arrangements for adding spraying to the general routine of tea culture and the machinery, in many cases, was indented for. Unfortunately it was then impossible to get the necessary apparatus at reasonable prices. Conditions are now improving, and it is to be hoped that it will soon be possible to obtain satisfactory machines. It is probable therefore that many planters will be turning their attention to blight treatment in the near future. In the past few years, we have added considerably to our store of information regarding the fungus blights of tea and, in the light of this knowledge, it is possible to modify the schemes proposed three or four years ago with advantage.

THE VALUE OF SPRAYING.

To begin with, it should be pointed out that spraying is only one of the ways of treating fungus blights. Unfortunately it seems to be impossible to get out of doing it, but it should be avoided as much as possible, as, under the present conditions, it is costly in both money and labour. To attempt spraying on a large scale, without considerable knowledge of the life-histories of the fungus diseases concerned, is likely to lead to disappointment and waste of money. Planters are recommended to consult this department in regard to any schemes they are carrying out. Our experiments have shown that the spray fluids in common use, although beneficial to healthy tea, do not cause a sufficient increase in crop to warrant their application to such tea. The spraying of tea attacked by leaf or stem diseases however is a distinctly profitable undertaking, if it is carried out with due regard to the life-histories of the fungi concerned.

THE GENERAL TREATMENT OF OUTBREAKS OF FUNGUS
DISEASE.

The parasitic fungi of tea mostly live inside the tissues of the plant, and it is only at those periods of their life-history when they are exposed, as during the early stages of infection and in the fruiting period, that fungicidal fluids have any great influence on them. Spraying alone therefore cannot be expected to cure many fungus diseases which grow inside the tissues. It can be used to protect the plant from attack by coating the vulnerable portions with a fungicidal covering, but to some extent on account of the climatic conditions and largely owing to the rapidity of growth of the tea plant, this protection is only afforded for a very short time. It is mainly useful in killing the fruiting stages of the fungus and thus preventing the distribution of live spores. At the same time, such spores as are germinating on the surface of the plant organs are killed and fresh infections are thus prevented. Spraying therefore must be looked on as an aid to blight treatment, not as a panacea for all fungus diseases. By spraying, we disinfect the surface of the plants. Attempting to treat, by spraying alone, a fungus disease, which lives for the greater part of its life inside the tissues of the host plant, is just about as sensible as trying to disinfect a human habitation by spraying alone, without paying attention to bedding, clothing, etc.

344[JUNE, 1919]

THE TREATMENT OF OUTBREAKS OF DISEASE.

When an outbreak of disease occurs, every effort should be made to collect and destroy all diseased material, taking care that in so doing the disease is not distributed over a large area by careless handling of such material. The collection and destruction of diseased material should, in the case of leaf and stem diseases, be followed immediately by the application of a fungicidal spray, to disinfect the plants, and thus prevent new infections. After this treatment, a careful watch should be kept for fresh outbreaks, and the same procedure followed on their discovery. If this is done as soon as the outbreak is discovered, the disease can often be eradicated, or at any rate kept in check with little difficulty.

The climatic conditions in the tea districts are such that, if once a disease get out of hand in the growing season, it is almost impossible to check it until the cold weather. It is therefore most important to have everything connected with blight treatment ready for immediate use. Too often the spray materials are out of stock and the machines in a dirty condition, frequently with parts missing. In consequence, the work cannot be put in hand in time, and it is often necessary to spend a great deal more labour and money.

The diseases of tea may be classified as follows :—

  1. 1. Those which infect the whole plant generally.
  2. 2. Those which cause infection more or less local.

The latter may be sub-divided according to the part they most commonly attack, e. g., into leaf, stem or root disease. The above will be dealt with separately.

DISEASES WHICH CAUSE GENERAL INFECTION OF THE PLANTS.

The only certain cure for diseases which cause general infection of the plant is to remove and destroy all the infected plants. Thyradaria tarda is the only fungus of this kind which gives trouble in North-East India. The disease requires special conditions for infection to take place. Apparently it can only attack shoots which are dying back from other causes, such as the ends of pruned shoots. Considerable benefit would be obtained by spraying the shoots with a fungicide after pruning; but whether it would pay to do so is another matter. In most cases the number of bushes which die out from this disease is so small that the cost of spraying would be out of proportion to their value.

LEAF DISEASES,

Until recently very little was known of the life-histories of the commoner leaf diseases of tea, but for some time, the matter has been under investigation in this department and, although the results are not ready for publication in pamphlet form, in making the following recommendations, the information gained in these investigations has been made use of.

The commonest leaf diseases are Blister, Brown and Copper Blights. These all grow inside the tissues, so they cannot be treated satisfactorily by spraying alone. Blister Blight and Copper Blight are caused by fungi which apparently confine their attentions to tea, and infection from the jungle is not to be feared. Brown Blight on the other hand is found to be caused by one of the commonest parasitic fungi in the Tropics. Both Blister blight and

JUNE, 1919.]345

Copper Blight are diseases which attack healthy, vigorous tea, while Brown Blight is generally found on tea which is out of condition.

It is found that Brown Blight is generally present on healthy tea, but, unless the general health of the plant is below par, the fungus does not develop. The fungus can only attack young leaf, but it does not make itself obvious until the leaf has hardened. When tea has been badly attacked by Brown Blight the fungus not only attacks the leaves, but also the young stems, and will spend the cold weather in the latter, developing an abundance of spores in the early rains. These infect the young growth, and another severe attack follows should there be any drought or other adverse conditions. It is therefore desirable to clean out tea, which has been attacked by Brown Blight in the cold weather, and burn or bury in trenches all the prunings and fallen leaves. It would also be advantageous to spray the tea as soon as the first shoots develop. This protects them from infection and ensures a healthy first flush. Another spraying, after the first flush has been plucked, may be made if there are signs of a return of the disease. It is very little use trying to check a severe attack in the rainy season, except in the case of nurseries. In this case all the diseased shoots must be removed and the young leaf sprayed frequently. The older leaves do not matter, as they are immune.

In the case of Blister Blight the fungus has no resting stage, but is present in districts like Darjeeling throughout the year. The gardens should be searched thoroughly for the white blisters which, whenever found, should be removed and the infected bushes sprayed. If it develops into a bad attack, it is practically impossible to deal with it. The difficulty in Darjeeling is that the gardens are so close together that co-operation in dealing with this disease is essential to success. The fungus takes ten or eleven days from the time of infection to develop its fruits, so that it is necessary whenever an outbreak has been discovered and treated, to keep a sharp lookout for that time at least, and remove and spray wherever the blisters appear. In Darjeeling, where this disease is very bad, all nurseries and collar-pruned tea should be sprayed at frequent intervals until the shoots have grown satisfactorily.

Copper Blight is very easily confused with Brown Blight. It however frequently causes the leaves to bend over in a characteristic manner. It is usually found on vigorous healthy tea. Fortunately it requires special climatic conditions for infection. Usually attacks occur about the second flush. The fungus can survive the cold weather in dead leaves so that, where this disease is prevalent, it is most important to remove or cover deeply all dead or diseased leaves. Spraying as the first and second flushes develop is beneficial as the fungus is only capable of infecting young leaves.

STEM DISEASES.

The commonest stem disease is Red Rust. An attack of Red Rust is the result of the ill-health of the plant. It is therefore imperative to find out what is really wrong. The dead or badly-diseased shoots must be removed and the plant sprayed immediately after. If the badly-diseased portions are not removed, it takes much longer for the plants to recover and the shoots die back much further. The spraying helps the bush to recover, but the improvement is only temporary, unless the causative conditions are set right. The spraying is very useful, however, as it may save plants from dying right out.

Another common stem disease is Thread Blight: This is very easy to cure. In most cases letting in the sunlight by stick pruning is all that is

346[JUNE, 1919.

necessary. It sometimes happens, however, that the disease survives this treatment. In that case spraying should be resorted to after pruning. Solutions double the strength recommended for use in the rainy season may be used with advantage.

ROOT DISEASES.

The treatment of root diseases is quite simple. In most cases no chemicals are required. Merely dig up and burn all the diseased bushes as soon as they are found. All the little pieces of dead roots would be carefully removed from the soil. The simplest way to do this is to throw it through a piece of coarse wire-netting fixed to a strong frame. Further treatment, if necessary, can be carried out later according to the nature of the disease.

SUMMARY OF WORK REQUIRED.

The work required, in addition to the customary routine, for the general treatment of fungus diseases, may be summarised as follows :—

In cold weather :

  1. 1.—Remove and burn or bury, so deep that the first few rounds of hoeing will not turn them up, all leaves and twigs showing signs of disease.
  2. 2.—Spray with a fungicide all bushes whence diseased portions have been removed.

In the growing season :

  1. 1.—Keep a careful lookout for outbreaks of any diseases, which experience has taught, are liable to become serious.
  2. 2.—Remove and destroy all leaves or stem attacked by those diseases at once.
  3. 3.—Spray all bushes from which diseased portions have been removed, together with their immediate neighbours.

APPARATUS NECESSARY.

Every garden should be equipped with a godown with sprayers, water-carrying apparatus, spare parts, field repair outfit and chemicals ready for immediate use. Where conditions permit, it is a great advantage to have a special cart fitted with all the necessary appliances ready to proceed anywhere in the garden. This should be in charge of a special man or staff of men, whose duty it would be to keep the machinery, etc., in order and look for outbreaks of blight.

WHAT TO SPRAY WITH.

There are many spray fluids to choose from, but most of them require careful preparation. It seems practically impossible to get such solutions as Bordeaux Mixture and Lime Sulphur solutions made up satisfactorily on the garden. It is cheaper in the long run to get ready-made fluids in concentrated form, though even ready-mixed preparations are not used properly if any trouble is required in diluting them. After a great deal of observation, the writer has concluded that Lime Sulphur solution is very satisfactory for most purposes, but in cases where planters prefer copper compounds, such as Bordeaux Mixture, Burgundy Mixture ready mixed in powder form is better than the Bordeaux Paste. Both the above are available in Calcutta.

CAUSTIC SPRAY FLUIDS.

Many gardens have been using caustic spray fluids for heavy-pruned tea on a large scale. It should be pointed out that these fluids are recommended for special purposes, such as the removal of mosses and lichens. They do very little good and possibly some harm to really healthy tea. In places like Darjeeling, where the mosses and lichens are very thick, the general use of these fluids is recommended, but even then they should be only just strong enough to do the work required of them.—PLANTERS' CHRONICLE, Vol. XIV., No. 16.

B. ARCE.

(Plant Inspector, Philippines),

Almost every year many rice-farmers suffer great loss in their crops from the damage inflicted by the cut-worms. Sometimes the farmers cannot plant rice at the right time, especially those with no irrigation system, due to the fact that the damage is done when the rice seedlings in the seed-bed are vigorously growing and are about to be transplanted.

There are two species of cut-worms, that are commonly found in these Islands, attacking rice seedlings of lowland and upland rice plants; they are known to the common farmers as "uod sa punlang palay" in Tagalog, "uod sa humay or Tagustus" in Visayan, "Iggues ti bonobon" in Ilocano and "ulad king punlang paie" in Pampanga. These worms, which are nocturnal in habit, voraciously attack cabbages, tomatoes, tobacco and other plants beside rice. In the day-time they hide under loose soil, weeds or dirt.

Their life cycles vary. That of the Prodenia litura fabr is from 33 to 50 days; and that of the Spodoptera mauritia is from 31 to 66 days in captivity through the following stages:—

It has been noted, the breeding adults or moths in large cages, under observation, when fed with molasses, sweet fragrant sliced fruits and flowers with sweet nectar, lived from 5 to 24 days.

The moths of these two insects differ in appearance. The Prodenia litura is a pale-brown with whitish stripes crossing the wings and two heavy white patches set transversely on the centre and near the costal margin of the forewings. Its size is about 8 to 20 millimeters when resting.

The Spodoptera mauritia is a dark-brown with a pair of whitish stripes and black spots set side by side transversely on the centre of the forewings. The size is about the same as that of the Prodenia litura.

Relative to the control measures for those insect pests, the following recommendations are given and the farmer may select the one that he considers most practicable for use on his crop and in fields.

SPRAYING.

Rice plants which are attacked by the rice cut-worms may be sprayed with a solution of lead arsenate in the proportion of one kilo of lead arsenate to one hundred liters of water. The arsenate should first be well dissolved in a small amount of water and then more water to make one hundred liters. Spraying can be done at any time of the day, but it is better to do it before evening. The plants should be liberally sprayed. A spray-pump with a good nozzle is recommended. The solution must be stirred during spraying, so that the arsenate of lead will not settle to the bottom. This method can be used for any kind of cut-worms and biting insects attacking rice plants. Every precaution should be taken to prevent poisoning human beings or domestic animals. Lead arsenate is a deadly poison.

Sweeping with a net.—This is practical and is one of the best ways of controlling the pest, because it is inexpensive, simple and effective. It is done as follows:—

A coarse cloth bag fastened to a ring, the lower portion of which is flattened and fitted with projecting teeth about ten centimeters long and about five centimeters apart, like a rake, is used as a net to sweep the infested field. The worms brushed off from the leaves into this bag or net are then destroyed. The sweeping must be done between 6 and 9 p.m.

Submerging method.—This is applied only where there is plenty of water. After the dikes (pilapils) are repaired, the water is allowed to run in until the seed-bed is submerged. The water will force the worms to rise to the surface and float. They can be easily collected or skimmed off by means of a sieve or net. The surplus water should be drained off afterwards. Where the seedlings have grown much taller than the dikes and complete submersion is impossible, a combination of this method and the one described in the preceding paragraph will be found very effective. Care should be taken not to overflow the dikes to keep the worms from being washed over into other fields. The worms should always be collected and destroyed.—PHILIPPINE FARMER, Vol. V. No. 2.

SOILS AND MANURES.

THE COMPOSITION OF THE SOIL.

SIMPLE FACT REGARDING ITS CHEMISTRY.

Considering what an important part the soil plays in the life of the farm, it is surprising how few know much about its structure. There is no reason why this should be neglected, for the soil presents a most interesting study, and one which should be at all times profitable. It may be advanced that few have the opportunities owing to the pressing nature of their work to undertake studies of this kind, which is true enough as far as it goes, but if it results in an improved knowledge and better yields, and, consequently more money, it will be admitted that nobody can afford to neglect such investigations.

If any soil be examined that has not been disturbed by tillage operations, it will be seen to be composed of layers which are always in the same order, no matter what soil it is or where it may be. The best place in which to make an examination of a soil, is in a railway cutting, or a donga, or in any place where there is a deep opening between two portions. An examination of such a section will reveal a grass or vegetable layer about three inches deep at the top, which is generally called turf. Below this is a layer of the soil proper ranging from a few inches to a few feet or more in depth.

Below this again, in some cases, is what is called the subsoil. There are cases in which there is no subsoil at all, but on the other hand the subsoil may extend downwards to a great depth. The subsoil can be distinguished from the soil and turf by the fact of its being lighter in colour. Sometimes, in the lower portions of the subsoil, or where the subsoil is absent, immediately below the soil proper, we find rubble or broken rock mixed up with the earth. Underlying the whole is the solid rock from which the overlying soil has been formed.

These are the structural divisions of a soil. They do not all exist in every soil, but when they do occur they are always in the same order. Sometimes while the different divisions can be seen, it is impossible to find any actual line of separation between them.

Next we come to the composition of a soil, or the ingredients of which it is composed. There are five of these : viz., stones, sand, clay, lime and humus. It is on the preparation of these constituent parts that the farmer bases his opinion of the value of a soil, so far as its mechanical condition is concerned, for agricultural purposes. It forms, in fact, the basis of classification of soils for the purposes of classification and description.

VARIETIES OF SOILS.

There are endless varieties of soils, but every soil can be included in one or other of the following seven classes : (1) Sandy, (2) Loamy, (3) Clayey, (4) Marly, (5) Calcareous (i.e. chalky), (6) Gravelly, (7) Humus.

350[JUNE, 1919.

The description of a soil depends upon the relative proportions of its five proximate constituents. For instance, if humus predominates, we call it a humus or peaty soil ; if sand is a large constituent we term it a sandy soil. It is scarcely necessary to point out that it is impossible to give hard-and-fast percentages of constituents for each class of soil, but we can decide upon approximate proportions which will assist us in classifying soils that come under our observation.

If the planter in the course of his wanderings will test his knowledge by estimating the character of the soil, he will find the information gained valuable, and the practice in differentiating between them very helpful.

The following percentages will be useful as representing the limits of each class : Sandy soil should contain over 90 per cent. of sand. Sandy loam should contain from 80 to 90 per cent. of sand. Loamy soil contains from 70 to 80 per cent. of sand. Clayey loam contains from 30 to 50 per cent. of clay. Clayey soil contains over 50 per cent. of clay. Marly soil contains from 5 to 20 per cent. of lime. Calcareous soil contains over 20 per cent. of lime. Humus soil contains over 5 per cent. of organic matter. These facts are worth storing up, so that when a certain treatment is recommended for a certain soil, a farmer may see at once what it is composed of.

CLAYS.

Clay comprises the sticky, plastic part of the soil. When there is too much of it, the soil is heavy. Every fertile soil must contain lime, even if only in a small proportion. Limes make stiff soils, such as clayey ones, more crumbly and friable, and is therefore added to improve the soil.

HUMUS.

Humus is the animal and vegetable matter, which, decaying, have accumulated in the soil and mixed with the mineral constituents. All fertile soils contain some humus. The terms organic and inorganic are often used in relation to the soils, and there must be many times when they puzzle the farmer striving to understand what he is reading about. The meaning of such terms can be impressed upon the mind by a very simple experiment :—

Suppose one were to take a quantity of dry soil on a shovel and heat it over a fire to a red heat, part of the soil would burn away, while a portion would remain in the shovel unaffected by the heat. The part which burns away would be the organic part, and the portion remaining, the inorganic.

The proportion burnt away in any soil would depend upon the amount of humus, or decayed organic matter present in the soil. In a peaty soil there is sometimes as much as 90 per cent. of humus, so that if a sample of such soil were burned by far the greater part of it would disappear into the air, leaving a relatively small amount of inorganic or mineral matter.

The part that disappears in the burning consists of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen.

These facts are but the A B C of soil chemistry, but the knowledge of them may prevent many mistakes being made, and induce better farming practice.—SOUTH AFRICAN SUGAR JOURNAL, Vol. 3, No. 3.

THE THEORY OF FERTILIZERS.

The amount of food that may be realised from any region, depends upon two obvious factors—the area of soil and its fertility. In a new or pioneer country, land is plentiful, and the need of an increasing food supply for a growing population is met at first by enlarging the area cultivated; but sooner or later, there comes a time when further enlargement is impossible and food must be imported or else the yield of the soil must be increased by artificial means. The world has already reached the stage where in all regions of dense population the latter is imperative—a fact which should have the widest and most thorough appreciation.

Various means of increasing productivity are practised, but the most important gain is attained by adding certain products directly to the soil. These substances are the so-called fertilizers. A glance at the nature of soils and the chemical reactions that take place in them to produce plant-growth will make clear the part that fertilizers play in accomplishing their important function.

To begin with, soil does two things: It forms a mechanical medium for supporting and protecting the growing plant, and it supplies the plant with some of the chemical material to be built into its structure. The rest of its substance the plant obtains from the air, from moisture and from the bacteria present in the soil, although the last may be regarded as part of the soil itself.

The chemical material contributed by the soil, focuses our interest here—the calcium, the phosphorus, the sulphur, the nitrogen, the potassium, the sodium, the magnesium, the iron, and some others. These substances are not present in their elemental or simple condition, but as compounds available for transfer into the body of plants.

These elements are likewise present in the solid rocks that everywhere underlie the soil, but in combinations wholly unfitted for sustaining plant-growth. They must first be rendered suitable by the general process of natural rock decay called weathering, which mechanically reduces the solid rocks to the consistency of soil and chemically prepares their components for the sustenance of life. The soil then becomes the laboratory in which the gases of the atmosphere, with water, and certain constituents of decayed rock are combined into the organic products, or plants, upon which human life ultimately depends.

In the normal course of events, plants spring up, live their course, and die, giving back to the atmosphere and soil the elements employed in their life cycle. *Thus it is that the rank growth of the jungle is unremitting. But where plants are removed by artificial means, the balance is upset and the soil is depleted in respect of some of its essential compounds. But soil is not static; the processes of weathering are continuous, and if the plant-removal be stopped for a sufficiently long period, the deficiency will be naturally remedied by the transformation of a further quantity of needed compounds into an available or soluble condition. This result, however, cannot be waited for; in populous sections the soil must be cultivated continuously; the elements removed too rapidly must be replaced artificially, that is, by the use of fertilizers. This is an absolute, basic necessity. Fertilisers, indeed, play a more fundamental part than that of merely increasing yields and profits; no soil can be cultivated continuously without their use.

352[JUNE, 1919.

Fertilizers, moreover, have an application beyond that of making possible continuous and intensive cultivation; by their use, many soils can be rendered fertile which are deficient in one or more essential constituents by virtue of peculiar geologic history. Thus fertilizers extend the possibilities of food production to great areas, unfitted by nature for productive yield.

These considerations of the social value of fertilizers are aside from the economic advantage of their use as leading to greater profits to the acre and greater yields to the unit of labour expended. Yet this economic advantage is the great motive force which, under existing conditions at least, must be counted upon to carry fertilizer utilization to its proper realization.

By long experience, it has been found that those elements of which the soil is most quickly depleted are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium; and therefore compounds of these elements are the chief ingredients of the commercial fertilizers. Calcium, in the form of lime or crushed limestone, is added with favourable results to some soils; but this is more to counteract or neutralize a high acidity or "sourness" than to contribute a needed constituent, and since, moreover, limestone is so widely distributed that an organized industry is not necessary to supply it, this substance will not be considered in further detail in this paper. Sulphur in the elemental condition, has recently come to the front as a fertilizing material of some promise, but its use, as well as that of sulphuric acid on alkali lands, may be said to be still in an experimental stage. The fertilizer industry, therefore, as it exists at present, is concerned with the preparation of suitable compounds of nitrogen, phosphorus and potash to form commercial fertilizers, which are distributed for immediate addition to the soil. The securing of an adequate supply of raw materials, and the development of a wider and more intelligent use of fertilizers, are two problems of the utmost importance, especially at the present moment when the World War endangers certain of the supplies, and at the same time necessitates an increased yield in food.—WEALTH OF INDIA, Vol. VIII. ERADICATION OF WEEDS BY SPRAYS
AND MANURES.

WINIFRED E. BRENCHLEY, D. Sc.

(Rothamsted Experimental Station.)

The old-established ways of weed destruction by appropriate methods of cultivation were sufficient and effective in the days when labour was plentiful and the land was less heavily cropped. Of late years, however, with the progress of intensive cultivation, coupled with a constantly increasing shortage of labour, the problem has become critical, and many attempts have been made to supplement the mechanical means of eradication by more rapid chemical methods. A considerable measure of success has attended the venture, though the natural conservatism of farmers has hitherto prevented a general adoption of the new methods. This may be owing partly to the failure of many people to realise that it is possible to destroy the weeds in this way without causing injury to the crop.

JUNE, 1919.]353

The chief reason that chemical weed-killers can work successfully when applied to a growing crop lies in the fundamental difference in the habits of the majority of weeds and of such crops as cereals. The leaves of many weeds are broad and outstnding, and are often wrinkled, so that if moisture falls on them, it is retained for a considerable time, often till it is dried up by evaporation. Cereals, on the contrary, have long, narrow, smooth leaves which grow upright and are often covered with a waxy coating or "bloom," so that the leaves do not remain wet for any length of time, as moisture cannot adhere to them. If, therefore, corrosive or poisonous substances are sprayed on to the plant, the cereals throw them off and are not injured, whereas the weeds retain them and suffer the full extent of the possible injury. This same characteristic, however, renders it impossible to treat with chemicals such broad-leaved crops as clover, trefoil, sainfoin, and serradella, as their leaves retain the injurious substance in the same way as weed leaves. With root crops, the constant cultivation that is carried on is usually sufficient to keep weeds in check, so that on arable land, as a rule, it is only cereals that are dealt with by chemical means, though copper sulphate has been used to eradicate charlock from mangolds without injury to the crop.*

These methods are not so useful on grass land, because, as the herbage consists of a mat of plants with a large total leaf surface, the chemicals are held up even by the grasses, and the amount of spray necessary to kill the weeds is sufficient to cause serious injury to the herbage itself, especially to such broad-leaved plants as clovers, which are a valuable constituent of the crop. In the majority of cases, it is not possible to apply the spray in such a way that it reaches the weeds without affecting the rest of the herbage, and in consequence the method is of but limited use on grass land.

The success of the chemical eradication of weeds depends to some extent upon the weather. It is essential that the substance employed should remain upon the leaves long enough to come into action, and, therefore, if heavy rain falls immediately after an application of liquid spray, much of the benefit is lost. Consequently it is sometimes necessary to repeat the operation if the weather breaks too soon after a dressing of the destructive agent. Dry substances would easily be blown off from dry leaves, so when these are used, it is usual to carry out the work when the leaves are wet with dew or rain, as then the stuff adheres firmly when the leaves dry.

The chemical substances used as weed-killers may be divided into two groups:—

  1. 1. Chemicals that merely destroy the weeds, and have no direct beneficial action upon the growth of the crops. These substances are usually applied in the liquid form as sprays.
  2. 2. Compounds that not only destroy the weeds, but also exercise a manurial action, thus directly benefiting the crop at a later date. These substances are usually very finely ground manures and are applied as dry powders when the leaves are damp.

1. Sprays.—Most of these are corrosive in nature and destroy the delicate plant tissues, either killing the weeds outright or so crippling them that they cease to be active competitors of the crop. The chemicals are applied in solution, the strength varying according to circumstances. The most commonly used sprays are copper sulphate, iron sulphate, and sulphuric acid, but other substances are occasionally employed. A summary of many of the earlier spraying experiments was drawn up by H. C. LONG in 1910,† and the results of these have largely been confirmed by those set forth in the present paper.

Copper Sulphate.—Copper sulphate in solution is the most familiar of the weed-sprays used on a farm, and is generally associated with the destruction of charlock (Brassica sinapis,) though it is effective with other weeds. The rough crinkled leaf of charlock renders it very susceptible to injury and, given suitable weather, spraying nearly always makes a considerable reduction. The spray is best applied when the leaves have three or four well-developed leaves, though later applications are often effective. The writer saw a farm at Godling, Notts. in which charlock had been successfully sprayed when in full flower, but this delay is not to be recommended. Various experiments have shown that a 4 or 5 per cent. solution (4 to 5 lb. copper sulphate in 10 gals. of water) is the most effective strength, about 40 to 50 gal., containing 16 to 20 lb. copper sulphate per acre being required. In all cases the quantity of liquid should be sufficient to wet the weed thoroughly. In some cases a 3 per cent. solution is strong enough, but only under specially favourable conditions.*

Copper sulphate will eradicate spurrey if it be used strong enough '50 gals. per acre of a 5 per cent. solution is effective, but a 3 per cent. solution only destroys about half the crop, allowing the other half to recover and ripen its seeds.† In the latter case the shoots at first become brown and shrivel at the tips, so that growth is checked for a time, but later on recovery sets in with many plants. Arsenite of soda, potassium chloride and sodium bisulphate have all been used as weed-killers, but each has some special disadvantage, which prevents it from coming into common use. Arsenite of soda is exceedingly poisonous to man and beast, and there is a great risk of poisoning, when the crops are used, especially if it be applied to grass land which is either fed off or cut for hay. Besides this, it is strongly held by the soil, being retained for an indefinite period in the top few inches, and if several successive applications are made, enough arsenite of soda may accumulate to hinder the germination and growth of crops sown on arable land and to exercise a harmful action on the herbage of grass land.

Potassium chloride is ruled out at the present time on the score of expense, but when prices were moderate a 20-30 per cent. solution was occasionally used for destroying charlock, as the solution shrivelled the plants up completely. The potash salt has no bad effect on the cereals, but rather works beneficially by acting as a manure in addition to destroying the weeds.

Sodium bisulphate resembles sulphuric acid in its action, and is readily and cheaply obtained in places. In parts of France, farmers can get truck loads from the powder works at the rate of 9½d. per 200 lb. free on rail.* 80 gals. per acre of 45 per cent. solution are recommended for use, but the stuff is most unpleasant to handle, though it needs less precautions than sulphuric acid.

To sum up, therefore: copper sulphate and iron sulphate are effective and simple sprays whose value has been proved by years of constant usage. Nickel sulphate, though practically unknown in this respect, offers possibilities and might repay more extended trial. Under special circumstances, sulphuric acid has much to recommend it, but the majority of other sprays are of less general value, though several of them meet the case under particular local conditions.

2. Manures.—The method of improving grass land by judicious artificial manuring has been gaining much ground of recent years. Incidentally this secures the reduction of the less useful plants or weeds, but this reduction is chiefly brought about by the increase in the competition of the more valuable grasses, and not by a direct poisonous action on the weeds.

During the last few years, however, attempts have been made to destroy weeds on arable land by the application of finely-ground manures, especially cyanamide and kainit, and on grass land by the use of lime, gaslime and salt, and a fair measure of success is considered to have rewarded the effort.

Calcium Cyanamide (Lime nitrogen).—Since this was introduced as a commercial manure, it has been used a good deal in Germany as a means of destroying charlock and wild radish on arable land. It is claimed that dressings of 90-135 lb. per acre are quite effective for this purpose, provided that the cyanamide is applied when the plants are small and the leaves are damp†; thistles and poppies are injured as well as the charlock. In some cases the crops turn rather yellow for a few days after application, but they rapidly recover and make use of the extra nitrogen supplied, giving increased yields. NITROGEN FIXATION BY ROOT NODULE BACTERIA.

It is now more than thirty years ago since the nitrogen-fixing power of the bacteria present in the root-nodules of leguminous plants was firmly demonstrated. During this time much of practical importance has been discovered. The bacteria have been isolated and cultivated in artificial media. Their mode of entry into the root and their power to stir up the cortical cells to abnormal growth are known; yet even now much remains to be discovered. Of the many outstanding problems, perhaps the most important is that—often asked and never satisfactorily answered—which concerns the power of the nodule-bacteria to associate themselves with non-leguminous plants and to bestow upon them powers of nitrogen-fixation.

The discovery that sundry other plants enter habitually into association with micro-organisms and form in consequence nodule-like growths on their root system, led naturally to the suggestion that these symbiotic unions, like those occurring in leguminous plants, conferred on the plants which entertained them like powers with those enjoyed by Leguminosæ.

Among non-leguminous nodule-producing plants are species of such genera as Alnus, Ceanothus, Cycas, Elæagnus, Myrica and Podocarpus, and sundry investigators have claimed to show that nitrogen fixation occurs in them, and have inferred that it is due to the symbiotic association. Other observers have gone further, and having discovered that certain plants habitually associate themselves with certain fungi, claim that these unions also confer on the contracting parties, the power of drawing upon the free nitrogen of the air for their supply of nitrogenous food. It is, however, doubtful whether the evidence advanced in any of these cases is as conclusive as it is in that of the Leguminosæ. Indeed, and in the case of the non-leguminous nodule-bearing plants, the most recent evidence tends to throw doubt on the claim that they are nitrogen-fixers, or at all events justifies the demand that more rigorous proof shall be forthcoming before this view is finally accepted. Thus in his last published work* the late THOMAS J. BURRILL shows that the non-leguminous nodules differ in certain respects from those of the Leguminosæ in that they are not caused by Pseudomonas radicicola, the bacterium present in the root nodules of leguminous plants.

This conclusion is in disagreement with that reached by PROF. BOTTOMLEY and his co-worker who claim in the cases of Cycas, Alnus and Elæagnus to have isolated Pseudomonas radicicola from their nodules.

In the light of the more recent work of PROF. BURRILL, this conclusion must now be regarded as doubtful, and these plants must be rejected, at all events provisionally, from the category of nitrogen fixers.

The case of Alnus glutinosa is somewhat different : for notwithstanding the fact that PROF. It would be well worth the while of botanists to take up this question once more and to ascertain first, whether fixation of nitrogen does occur in Alnus and other non-leguminous nodule-bearing plants; and second, what is the intensity of fixation as compared with that of leguminous plants. For apart from the purely scientific aspect of this subject, there is this most important practical aspect : that if some non-leguminous plants are in fact able to fix atmospheric nitrogen, it should not be impossible to train others to do the same beneficent work. This, indeed, was the main object of PROF. BURRELL'S work. Having made the most careful study of the habits of the nitrogen-fixing bacteria, he endeavoured to induce this organism to enter into a union with non-leguminous plants as, for example, the Tomato. His experiments were not, however, crowned with success, and so far as they may be regarded as final, we have to be content with the conclusion that symbiosis between the nitrogen-fixing organism and non-leguminous plants is impossible. This conclusion is in general harmony with the facts discovered by PROF. BURRELL with respect to what may be called the catholicity of infectiveness of the nodule organism.

Although Pseudomonas radicicola must be regarded as one species, it forms a series of biological races, each of which is capable of infecting a restricted number of leguminous genera. From BURRELL'S researches, it appears that some eleven races of P. radicicola may be isolated. Of these, one race confines its activities to species of Trifolium, and can transfer itself from one of these species to another, for example, from Mammoth Red Clover to Alsike, Crimson Egyptian, White or Cow-clover.

Another race is more catholic and may infect species of Sweet Clover (Molilotus), Medicago (Alfalfa, etc.), or Trigonella. A third race is common to no fewer than eight genera of legumes : Vigna, Cassia, Arachis, Lespedeza, Mucuna, Desmodium, Acacia and Genista. A fourth race is competent to infect Pisum, Vicia, Lens (Lentil) and Lathyrus, whereas a fifth race confines its attention to the Soy Bean. Similarly, Phaseolus enjoys the exclusive use of a sixth race of Pseudomonas, whereas the Lupine and one other genus share the favours of yet another race of the nodule organism. The Hog Pea Nut (Amorpha canescens), the trailing Wild Bean (Strophostyles helvola), and the common Locust (Robinia Pseudacacia) have each a race of their own.

From these facts, it must be concluded that the conditions which lay a plant open to infection are highly specialised—that the gate by which infecting organisms enter the plant is straight and the way narrow. The problem that awaits solution is this : Can man succeed in doing what Nature has apparently failed to accomplish, i.e., train plants and nitrogen-fixing bacteria so to accommodate themselves to one another as to admit of a symbiotic union, as a result of which every cultivated plant may, "without sweat or endeavour" in the shape of added nitrogen-fertilizer, make use of the atmospheric nitrogen as a source of food supply ?—GARDENER'S CHRONICLE.—Vol. LXV. THE CONSERVATION OF OUR CEREAL RESERVES.

The dangers to which grain stored under ordinary conditions is exposed may be classified under four heads: (1) The attacks of rats and mice; (2) those of insects and mites, (3) those of moulds and bacteria; and (4) the process known as "heating." The amount of damage due to rats and mice is, no doubt, enormous, but might be avoided by any rational system of storage, and is a matter for legislation rather than for scientific investigation. The chief insect pests in this country, are the two grain-weevils: Calandra granaria and C. oryzae; while in India two other beetles: Rhizopertha dominica and Trogoderma khapra, are also responsible for much direct injury. Experiments on the rate of multiplication of the weevils show that at suitable temperatures they breed all the year round, but in this country normally only in the warmer months. At about 28°C. a single pair of rice-weevils increased about seven-hundredfold in four months. The accumulated excrement of the weevils attracts moisture and promotes decomposition, accompanied by the evolution of large quantities of ammonia, and in this way the destruction, commenced by the ravages of the insects is completed. The process of heating is the result of enzymic action in the wheat itself, sometimes inaccurately spoken of as respiration, though fermentation would be a better term, which increases with rise of temperature (up to about 55°C.) and moisture content (Bailey & Gurjar). In the eyes of the trade, heating appears to be a much more serious danger than weevilling. It is at present avoided by abundant ventilation, the grain being turned over as soon as the temperature becomes dangerously high, so as to cool it and carry off moisture.

As an effectual means of preventing damage from all these sources, airtight storage should be resorted to. Unfortunately, however, considerable doubt has been thrown on the efficacy of this ancient method by a widespread belief in the ability of weevils to withstand such treatment. This belief rests entirely upon inaccurate observations. Thus we find that tins which are supposed to be hermetically sealed, and look perfectly sound, are often leaky, as can easily be shown by placing them in hot water, when air bubbles out. Numerous experiments made at King's College by PROF. ARTHUR DENDY, F.R.S., and his colleague, MR. H. D. ELKINGTON, who is responsible more specially for the chemical analyses, prove conclusively that all insects present are more or less rapidly destroyed, when weevilly wheat is sealed up in airtight receptacles which it nearly fills. This method of treatment destroys the weevils in all their stages, and is also fatal at any rate to adult mites. The same treatment also prevents the growth of moulds and the process of heating. Two Dewar flasks, filled with grain having a moisture content of

364[JUNE, 1919.]

20.7 per cent., were incubated at about 28 °C. One was merely plugged with cotton-wool and the other hermetically sealed. In the former, the temperature gradually rose to 49.4 °C., while in the latter it remained almost stationary. The life of insects and moulds and the process of heating alike depend upon the supply of oxygen and where this is cut off, no damage from these sources need be feared.

It has been demonstrated experimentally, not only that weevils require an abundant supply of oxygen, but also that carbon dioxide, if present in sufficient quantity, has a directly poisonous action upon them. In pure, moist carbon dioxide, they become motionless in three minutes, and can remain in this condition for as much as four days (at room temperature) without losing the power of recovery. A mixture of carbon dioxide with 20 per cent. of oxygen is far more fatal than pure carbon dioxide. This is probably because, in the absence of oxygen, their metabolism is more or less completely suspended, so that the carbon dioxide is unable to exercise its poisonous effect. In a mixture of 56.4 per cent., nitrogen 20.36 per cent., oxygen, and 23.22 per cent. carbon dioxide, weevils became motionless in forty-three hours (at about 30 °C.), and after ninety-one hours' exposure, though 19.09 per cent. of oxygen still remained, none revived when supplied with ordinary air.

When wheat is sealed up in a normal atmosphere, carbon dioxide accumulates naturally owing to the so-called respiration of the grain, the rate of accumulation depending upon temperature and moisture conditions. At ordinary room temperature (July to October) in three months 300 grams of English wheat, having a natural moisture-content of 15.9 per cent., gave off 58.6 milligrams of carbon dioxide, sufficient to raise the percentage of that gas in the air in the receptacle (which was nearly filled with wheat) to 18.13. If insects also be present, the carbon dioxide accumulates more rapidly owing to the large amount which they themselves give off. It thus appears that in hermetically-sealed granaries completely filled with grain there should be no need for any artificial addition of carbon dioxide such as has sometimes been recommended, and, indeed, actually made, for the purpose of destroying weevils. Under proper conditions, which ought to be experimentally determined on a large scale, the grain must become self-protective as regards weevilling, mildew, and heating, to say nothing of rats and mice. Any damage which might arise while the carbon dioxide was accumulating, would probably be negligible.

The construction of airtight granaries or silos is a problem for the engineer, but there seems to be no insuperable difficulty in the way. If such granaries existed in the large wheat-growing countries, the grain might be completely sterilised as regards insect-life by storing for a suitable period before shipment, and the very serious weevilling which often takes place on board ship might be avoided. Moreover, it would be possible to equalise shipments all the year round and avoid the rush to get the grain away after harvest. Airtight storage would also, in all probability, afford by far the best means of maintaining reserves of grain to meet emergencies such as war and failure of crops.

From the distribution of Air Pressure over the Island, the South-West Monsoon current arrived on the 15th. Its approach was gradual and its arrival not marked by any great intensity of wind or rain.

For the greater part of the Island, the rainfall was above the average. The greatest excess was in the North-West, and the least in the South-East. In the Kandy and Hill country in the Southern Province, the rainfall was in deficit. The number of days on which rain was experienced was greater than usual.

Humidity was above normal Upcountry, and below elsewhere: being especially low to the North and East.

The sky was clouded to an extent greater than usual, especially inland and Upcountry. On the whole, the temperature was normal.

Wind strength was about normal: but in direction varied by blowing from a point or two nearer the South than usual.

Air pressure was slightly above normal.
