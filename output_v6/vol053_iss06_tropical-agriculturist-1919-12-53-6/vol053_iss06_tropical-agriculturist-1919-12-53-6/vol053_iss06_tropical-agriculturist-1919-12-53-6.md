## RUBBER.

---

### RUSTINESS IN SMOKED SHEET.

---

Rustiness in sheet rubber has been attributed by various investigators to a superficial formation of resin, or to incomplete coagulation, or to a deposit of some of the constituents of the serum, more especially the proteids, on the surface of the rubber. Research on this problem has recently been carried out by HELLENDORN, and, though he was unfortunately compelled by illness to relinquish his investigation before it was quite completed, the results, published in the *ARCHIEF VOOR DE RUBBERCULTUUR IN NEDERLANDSCH-INDIE*, 3rd year, No. 9, are of considerable value from the practical standpoint.

It had been noticed that rustiness was especially prevalent during wet weather. In experiments in the laboratory and on estates rustiness could always be produced at will by keeping freshly rolled sheets for some time, twenty-four to forty-eight hours, in a damp atmosphere. Rustiness was produced when ordinary sheets were hung up in ordinary kerosene tins, closed with a tight-fitting lid, in which a small quantity of water had been placed. If the lid fitted loosely, so that the upper part of the tin was more or less ventilated, the parts of the sheet nearest the cover sometimes remained free from rustiness, while the parts nearer the water at the bottom of the tin became rusty.

The degree of moisture in which the sheet hangs shortly after rolling is of the greatest importance. In wet weather, sheets taken immediately after rolling into a drying room at a temperature of 104°—140°F never became rusty, whilst air drying (at room temperature) sometimes produced rustiness.

Rustiness is increased by rolling the sheet a longer time after coagulation. An increase in rustiness was caused in several experiments by rolling the sheets twenty-four hours after coagulation, instead of directly.

HELLENDORN concludes that rustiness is not caused by a film of serum constituents, notably proteins, merely dried on the surface of the sheet, but by the decomposition of the serum constituents by a micro-organism.

It has frequently been noted that when the coagulum is kept in coagulating pans for some time after coagulation (say over night) the side of the sheet which corresponds to the upper part of the coagulum, i.e., the layer

which was exposed to the air, shows rustiness to a greater extent than the lower surface which was immersed in the serum. The explanation of this is that infection may start earlier on the upper surface, and the growth of the micro-organism may be more rapid there, as the organism is aerobic, that is it cannot develop in the absence of air. This may also explain why, in some instances, rustiness has been diminished, where the coagulum is allowed to stand over night, by sinking it beneath the surface of the liquid by means of a block of wood.

The condition of the atmosphere in which the sheet is kept is, however, of great importance, and its effect may equalise or even discount the effect of infection on the coagulum. In the kerosene tin experiments noted above, the side of the sheet which is lowest in the tin becomes more rusty than the other side, even if it was the lower side of the coagulum in the coagulating pan, because of the greater humidity to which it is exposed. In the same way, in actual estate practice, rustiness may appear on some parts of certain sheets only, because these remained moist (perhaps from being hung too close together) during the period immediately after rolling.

The organism responsible for the decomposition of the serum substances has not been isolated, owing to the enforced termination of the experiments. But artificial infection of sterilised sheets has been successfully accomplished by using a suspension of the surface slime which had formed on sheets kept in a moist atmosphere after rolling. The sheets were sterilised by steam after rolling. One of them, the control, kept for twenty-four hours in a moist atmosphere developed only a few rusty spots; the other which was infected by means of a suspension of the surface slime and similarly kept in a moist atmosphere became very rusty.

The micro-organism which is the cause of rustiness needs air for its growth and does not thrive where the air cannot penetrate. This may be shown by simple experiments. For example, when a freshly-rolled sheet is rolled up tightly and placed in a moist atmosphere, only the parts exposed to the air develop rustiness, while the surfaces which stick together, so that the air cannot penetrate, remain free from rust. Similar examples may be observed in the factory. For instance, it may be found that a rusty sheet shows a strip free from rustiness, where the sheet hung closely round the rod; while in other cases, a sheet, otherwise rust free, may show a rusty band where it lay only loosely round the stick, so that a local moist atmosphere was formed at that spot. Rustiness did not develop in air which had been deprived of oxygen.

Like all micro-organisms, that which causes rustiness has an optimum temperature, i.e., a temperature at which it develops most rapidly. This appears to be about  $104^{\circ}\text{F}$ . Sheet kept at  $104^{\circ}\text{F}$  developed rustiness to a greater extent than that kept at room temperature ( $80^{\circ}\text{F}$ ), but in the experiments cited, rustiness could not be obtained at a temperature of  $122^{\circ}\text{F}$ . There was, however, a possibility that at the latter temperature the atmosphere was not maintained sufficiently moist.

The micro-organism obtains its nourishment from the thin layer of serum substances which remain on the sheet after most of the serum has dripped off, or from the bye-products which are absorbed by the rubber. It appears to prefer a diluted serum rather than undiluted: sheet from diluted latex (rubber content, 15 per cent. or even 5.7 per cent.) developed rustiness to a greater degree than sheets from undiluted latex. Soaking the sheets in water after rolling, so that a great part of the serum substances is removed, does not prevent rustiness: on the contrary, under favourable conditions, rustiness develops on such sheets to a much greater extent. Even on sheets soaked in water for seven days rustiness could be produced.

"It is well known to planters that when sheets are soaked in water, and the very dilute serum so formed is kept for some time, a jelly-like substance develops, as if some grease or fat had been extracted from the sheets. This jelly-like substance is probably very closely related to the substance forming the film of rustiness.

"Of some practical importance is the question whether smoke forms a sufficient disinfectant to prevent the growth of the organisms causing rustiness. When the wet sheets are placed in the smoke house and ventilation is only small, a wet atmosphere may be formed of a somewhat elevated temperature which would be very favourable for the development of micro-organisms, unless the smoke acted as a preventive. From our preliminary experiments it seems that smoke does hinder the formation of rustiness under such circumstances to some extent, but does not wholly prevent it; and, especially in places where the smoke does not penetrate so easily (for instance near the sticks, or in places where two sheets come very close together) rustiness may develop. If however one takes care that the sheets air-dry rapidly, so that they are sufficiently dry when they are placed in the smoke-house, the disinfection by smoke is negligible.

"The simplest way to prevent rustiness in actual estate-practice is to take care that the surface of the sheet dries so rapidly that the micro-organisms, which only grow on the surface exposed to the air and need moist surroundings, have no time to develop. The sheets after rolling and soaking in water should be hung wide-spread, wide apart, in a current of air, even outside the factory under shelter or in a shady place, rather than in a dark, moist corner of the factory; and after dripping for a few hours the sheets should be at once taken to the smoke-house. Leaving the sheets to drip outside the smoke-house for a night should be avoided.

"Prevention of rustiness by disinfecting the surface of the finished sheet with dilute formalin or chinosol is also possible, but as it is not so efficient as to allow rapid surface-drying to be neglected, and as it requires additional cost and work, it is not to be recommended.

"The old method of soaking the finished sheets for some time in hot water, which however as far as we know is no longer in use, is to be discarded, as the quality of the rubber may be injured to a marked degree.

"EATON, GRANTHAM and DAY recommend against rustiness soaking in water, and other measures tending to remove the serum as much as possible and to avoid concentration and drying up of serum by evaporation; they regard rustiness as being caused by a film of dried-up serum-substances. That soaking in water does not prevent rustiness, and that after soaking in water rustiness develops even to an increased degree; and that rapid surface-drying is the best method to prevent rustiness, we stated above. It would seem that the measures recommended by EATON are useful against the defect called greasiness, which (as far as it is not complicated by oversmoking) is caused by hygroscopic serum substances. It may therefore be stated clearly that the drying up of *undecomposed* serum-substances causes greasiness, whilst the *decomposition* of serum-substances results in rustiness. The first defect should be avoided by soaking the finished sheets for some time in water, so that the larger part of the soluble serum-substances from the surface-layers is extracted; and the second defect should be prevented by rapidly surface-drying the sheets after taking them from the water. It may be remarked that rusty sheets, especially when they are not smoked, have a perfectly dry feel, and are never greasy or sticky; it seems that the hygroscopic serum-substances are decomposed during the processes that result in rustiness."

Ribbed sheet has been found to develop rustiness equally with diamond sheet.

# TEA.

## A BASIS FOR TEA SELECTION.

Our readers perhaps do not need to be informed that the cultivation of tea in Java is being supported by a thorough scientific investigation into all aspects of the subject. As was only to be expected from a country which has brought cinchona to the highest standard, and has been foremost in the exploitation of modern coffees the question of varieties and jats of tea has been taken up, in order to ensure that the best varieties possible shall be planted and to effect whatever improvement may be possible in the existing types; and to secure a foundation for this work, Dr. C. P. COHEN STUART has carried out a searching enquiry into all the available evidence. The results of his researches have now been published in the *Bulletin du Jardin Botanique* (Series 3, Vol. 1, fasc. 4), and cover 130 pages, illustrated with eleven plates. The *Bulletin* being written in English, the information is immediately available to all interested in tea in other countries. It fully deserves its title of "A Basis for Tea Selection."

DR. COHEN STUART opens with an account of the introduction of tea planting into India, Ceylon, and Java which is the most complete and authoritative that has yet been written. He goes fully into the reasons which induced the Indian advisers to introduce China tea, and holds that, under the circumstances and in the light of the information then available, it was the only possible course. Full references are given to documentary evidence, and this history will no doubt remain a standard work of reference on the subject.

His remarks on Ceylon, respecting the introduction of tea, are not particularly flattering, but it may be as well as to see ourselves as others see us.

"Ceylon is a tea district of a comparatively recent date, having known the tea plant only in a cultivated state (we will see in the third chapter, that the assertion, sometimes heard, that tea has been found wild in Ceylon, is incorrect). The first experiments were carried out as early as 1839, but the first plantation dates from 1867. In the latter year, there were only 10 acres (4 hectares) planted; 10 years afterwards, in 1877, 2,720 acres (1,100 hectares); in 1887, 170,000 acres (68,800 hectares). This rapid increase, the so-called "rush into tea," was caused by the coffee blight *Hemileia* making its appearance between the years 1870 and 1880 and ruining this culture; all coffee plantations were then planted with tea. The production of tea kept pace with this advance; in 1873 it came to 23 lb., in 1883 to 1,500,000 lb. These figures strikingly illustrate the tremendously rapid increase within 20 years, and it is quite obvious that, in order to supply the unequalled demand for tea seed, inferior sorts of seed were bought wholesale in the north-eastern provinces. The consequence has been that Ceylon tea is an awful medley of tea hybrids, and that Ceylon, as a seed-producing country, stands in very bad repute among Java planters, however good its manufactured tea may be."

DR. COHEN STUART, however, does not take into account the fact that many tea seed gardens in Ceylon do not date from the first rush into tea, but have been planted in recent years with recognised jats, though considering his following remarks on selection, he might contend that the position was not materially altered thereby.

"Carelessness about the variety of tea planted, carelessness about the precious wild tea tracts, carelessness about the management of seed gardens, carelessness in short, about all measures that were liable to secure an effective tea selection, such, I regret to say, has been the happy-go-lucky attitude of the British planters towards a matter that could not, indeed, yield immediate profits, but should undoubtedly have done so at one time or another. Nor did they lack good counsel, for SIR GEORGE WATT has, ever since 1882, endeavoured to re-organize the Assam seed gardens. This meritorious promoter of tea cultivation in India had the kindness to inform me in what way the seed gardens were managed in the beginning :

"When I first made a tour through the tea districts of India, I found seed production very much a matter of accident. Most seed gardens were found to contain several different plants.' That is, he explains, 'several distinct races of plant *including the China. These had been introduced with the object of being able to supply (as it was thought) whatever stock might be desired.*' A naive idea, to be sure ! 'I suggested to the owners that they should eliminate all but one and endeavour to justify the reputation of each seed-garden as producing a definite and characteristic race of its own. My recommendation was adopted by most seed growers with the result that Bazelona, Singlo, Tingri, Namsang, Kalline, etc., are well-known and specially recognised stocks of the so-called 'Assam Indigenous.'"

"However important a progress this measure was, selection proper seems never to have been practised in India, though WATT repeatedly urged the necessity of it, especially of crossing experiments with other *Camellia*-species. But nobody has taken up this suggestion. The only thing the planters have done of their own accord, is, the substitution of many China plantations by indigenous varieties, because they gradually recognized the superiority of the latter. But this superiority is not universally accepted, and, as a matter of fact, the Himalaya districts furnish a conclusive proof that, under certain conditions, China tea may be the better variety. Everywhere, however, it has left indelible traces in the shape of innumerable hybrids, even where they are most certainly inferior."

After discussing the history of tea cultivation in China, the author next endeavours to trace the original home of the tea plant. Here he finds two difficulties : one, that what has been recorded as wild tea is often not tea at all, as in the case of BENNETT'S record of wild tea in Ceylon, and the other, that so-called wild tea, which is really tea, is frequently found to occur in the neighbourhood of old caravan routes, sometimes in regular blocks, and is to be regarded rather as an abandoned cultivation. Coming now to the fundamental part of the memoir, viz., the classification of the known forms, species or varieties of the tea plant, DR. COHEN STUART has examined the greater part of the specimens which have been gathered by botanical collectors, and are now stored in the herbaria of Europe and Asia. Many of these had been collected in a "wild" state and had been recorded as different species. DR. COHEN STUART gives tables showing the differences between these supposed species and varieties, and discusses the question of their validity. We quote his closing pages on this point:—

"Two important questions, still, remain to be settled: Ought all forms of tea to be united unto one species, and if so, which name ought this species to bear?

"The first question as to the classification of the tea varieties, is, like that of the *Camellia* species, a very thorny one, as it is materially affected by our conceptions on the nature of systematic distinctions, and our decisions will turn out quite different according as we happen to belong to the class of 'lumpers' or to that of 'splitters,' as BATESON puts it. Now, although agreeing with this author that genetical analysis has a bearing on systematics and may modify our conceptions on "blood-relationship" in a degree, I certainly cannot concur with him where he wants to base our so-called natural system on genetic tests exclusively, and accordingly encourages systematists to serve science by giving names freely 'and by describing everything to which their successors may possibly want to refer, and generally by subdividing their material into as many species as they can induce any responsible society or journal to publish.' This extreme 'splitting' as well as the obligatory genetical analysis would render the whole business of classification a mockery, the exact determination of a given specimen practically impossible. On the contrary, considering systematics from a purely opportunist point of view, apart from any pretentious evolutionary theory, I think 'lumping' is the only practical means of cataloguing the existent forms of living beings without having to await the results of an intricate cross breeding procedure. How, indeed, could we henceforth class any rare or extinct form.

"As to the other category of extreme 'splitters' consisting of those enthusiastic systematists whose ambition cannot be appeased before their scrutinising eye has detected the largest number of *novæ species* amongst the poorest material available, I think naturalists never can be too strenuously cautioned against their fatal activity. This scourge of systematic botany compels future generations to manipulate their fabric and drives them nearly to despair when trying to make head or tail of those deficient diagnoses.

"Now, supported by such diagnoses as the above, we may readily constitute two well-characterised species of tea. The difficulty begins when intermediate forms have to be classed. Crucial questions! I prefer naming them tea, to cut it short.

"It is, certainly, a personal view, based on the 'vague notion' of a species meant by DARWIN, rather than on the exact definition dreamt of by some modern geneticists; and that botanist, who could scarcely believe all specimens in WATT'S herbarium to belong to one and the same species, may have appealed to that same 'vague notion' in support of his view. But I think if this naturalist had also been acquainted with the other *Camellia*-species, and if he had known the very isolated position of the *Thea*-section as compared with the other *Camellias*, he would possibly have felt an equal apprehension of subdividing this *relatively* homogenous morphological group.

"There are, besides, more practical reasons for uniting all forms of tea. The commercial seed types, 'Landsorten' they might be called, are anything but well-defined varieties; they present a stupendous mixture of the most diverging leaf shapes. It would be a hopeless task to sift them according to LINNEAN species, which are besides mere abstractions, whereas their description by purely statistical methods is not, in principle, impracticable. Moreover, everybody understands that the word 'tea' means *any* variety of tea as long as no form is expressly mentioned by name.

"The latter line of argument is supported by the subsequent curious reasoning. If a new form of *Camellia* is met with, how are we to make out whether it is a 'tea plant' or not? We may of course limit this conception by botanical distinctions. "Now, what name must we adopt for this collective species? I propose the name placed at the heading of this chapter, to wit, *Camellia theifera* (Griff.) DYER and such on the following grounds:

"This name was used for the first time by GRIFFITH, who applied it to the Assam tea plant in contradistinction from China tea ('*C. bohea*.'). As a collective name for all tea forms it was used in the first instance by THISELTON DYER (1874). According to the 'International rules,' cited in the 5th chapter, art. 43, this increased significance is equivalent to the creation of a new species, and both authors are to be quoted in the manner shown above.

"It should be observed, however, that a *strict* application of the aforesaid Rules leads us to another deduction. Indeed, *the first name intended for all tea forms jointly was Thea sinensis L.*, so that *Camellia* being the correct generic name *Camellia sinensis* should henceforth be used for the collective species embracing all the forms known in *Linnaeus'* time and those then yet undiscovered, according to art. 44. It was actually, and I must own, correctly employed by O. KUNTZE in 1881, and again in 1887 (p. 195); he afterwards rejected it (1891, p. 64,) but on arguments not available. F. MASON (1883, p. 633) also makes use of this name.

"However correct the denomination *Camellia sinensis* (L.) O. KUNTZE may be, I do not think it advisable to adopt this very unusual name. We may object. (1) that the expression '*sinensis*' does not suit large-leaved *Indian* teas; (2) that LINNÆUS in 1753 knew but one form and soon subdivided this species, so that we may take it for granted that he would not have applied the name *sinensis* on the *Indian* forms; (3) that this name cannot properly be qualified as the first denomination which comprehends *all* forms of tea, since the union was not actually effected before DYER. Neither of these arguments are, to be sure, valid in the sense of the nomenclature rules. The case is rather doubtful, but I prefer *C. theifera*, this name being more 'classic' than *C. sinensis*.

"On the contrary, we need not feel the least doubt with regard to the name *Camellia Thea*, which is quite certainly incorrect. We meet with it for the first time with LINK (1822), who used it to designate *Thea bohea L.*; BRANDIS (1874) was the first, KURZ (1874) the second to apply it in the sense of Chinese plus *Indian* tea. But THISELTON DYER had got the start of him and chosen the name *C. theifera* for the same combination; accordingly, BRANDIS mentions DYER'S synonym, not conversely. Though the term *C. theifera* was published only a few months before *C. Thea* this of course suffices to deprive the latter of its priority.

"We may summarise all questions connected with the nomenclature of tea in the following terms:

1. The generic name *Thea* is certainly incorrect, *Camellia* is the sole exact name.

2. *Thea sinensis* (L.) SIMS has therefore certainly become obsolete; *Camellia sinensis* (L.) O.K. is in accordance with the international rules, but LINNÆ'S denomination of 1753 did not include Assam tea.

3. *Camellia Thea* (Link) Brandis is certainly to be excluded, since the union of China and India tea was earlier effected under the name *C. theifera* (Griffith) DYER.

4. It is desirable to use *C. theifera* instead of *C. sinensis*."

# FOODSTUFFS.

## UPLAND RICE.

The term "upland rice" is applied to those varieties of rice which grow in ordinary well-drained fields during the rainy season.

Such rice may be grown wherever corn or sugar-cane will grow or on newly opened lands.

In the growing of this kind of rice several factors which are *primarily essential* to the production of *lowland rice* may be *dispensed with*, the most notable being the *artificial water supply* and the *carabao* and *puddling operations*.

Upland rice is suitable therefore for general extensive growing on new lands. From investigation and experiments it has been conclusively proven that upland rice will yield much more per hectare than any variety of lowland rice tested.

Average production as compared with lowland rice shows that for every 100 cavans produced in paddy, 117 cavans are produced in dry field culture of the same areas.

Figures tend to prove that the lowland rice most farmers are raising is much less productive than upland rice would be while the production of lowland rice entails more labour, time and expense and proves conclusively that our Filipino farmers have been labouring under a delusion all these years, and are wasting time, energy and money in this present method of rice production.

The introduction of improved methods of growing upland rice will bring vast areas of unirrigated land into use, while if grown in rotation with sugar and corn will become an even greater source of profit, and keep down the annual crop of weeds, which is allowed to develop regularly under present methods.

Improved lowland varieties alone have proven inadequate to supply our present needs and require carabaos and large quantities of artificial water supplies, neither of which are abundant at present.

Machinery can be used exclusively for growing upland rice, and irrigation is no more needed than in the growing of sugar-cane.

The best varieties of upland rice mature in from 120 to 135 days, or during the regular rainy season, which gives more than 30 days more time for plowing and preparing to raise the crop.

Where the ground to be planted with upland rice is thoroughly prepared, this variety grows fast enough to smother grasses and weeds and is sufficiently strong to stand up well and to a great degree resist the effect of the wind.

Many varieties of upland rice will average yields of 50 cavans per hectare on properly prepared land, while the average production of rice for the entire Philippines for the year 1918 was only 26.16 cavans.

## RICE PRODUCTION IN CALIFORNIA, U. S. A., AS COMPARED WITH RICE PRODUCTION IN THE PHILIPPINES.

The first commercial crop of rice in California was grown in 1912 in the Sacramento Valley.

The profits from this crop of 600 hectares were so large that wide publicity was given to the possibilities of rice culture and 2,500 hectares were planted in 1913.

The average yield of 3,200 lb. per acre or 64 cavans per hectare, gave so great an impetus to this industry that over 6,000 hectares were planted the following year and added to the agricultural wealth of the state over \$1,600,000 in the first four years of this industry. Such men as H. O. JACOBSON of the Bureau of Agriculture, were secured by salaries greater than this Government thought it could pay them to develop this industry, and later on another of this Bureau's best men, A. M. BURTON, then chief of the Plant Industry Division, was secured in the same way.

With such men as MESSRS. JACOBSON & BURTON, with others from California to lead the way, it is no wonder that the rice industry of that State has gone forward by leaps and bounds and that fortunes have been made in the production of rice.

All of this work has been done by the use of modern machinery and modern scientific methods of farming, using tractor motors for planting, disking, seeding and harrowing and the regular threshing machines for threshing.

Here is what the CALIFORNIA AGRONOMIST says: When not contrary to good farm management, *plowing should be done in the fall and deep plowing should be practised.* The action of the weather will reduce the clods and make disking and harrowing and the preparation of a good seed-bed, easier the next spring.

The vigorous growth of the young plants, as well as high germination, is dependent upon how well the soil has been prepared for seeding.

Only selected seed should be planted and better results are obtained by drilling the seed than by broadcasting.

Rice sown on ordinary ground should not be drilled in deeper than two inches and well covered.

Early planting is desirable and the average early varieties will generally prove most reliable.

The levees should be just high enough to hold back the water and broad enough so that all kinds of machinery may easily pass over them.

The water will not become stagnant if freshened every 10 days.

The worst weed of the rice fields of California is red rice, and its presence in the rice of commerce always lowers the value

## A DRIED-BANANA INDUSTRY.

The Imperial Department of Agriculture for the West Indies has been in communication with the Colonial Office concerning the possibilities of extending the dried-banana industry in these islands, an industry which so far exists only in Jamaica.

Last December, the Imperial Commissioner of Agriculture requested the Agricultural Superintendent, St. Lucia, to prepare small samples by means of a Chula drier employed there during the war in connexion with the food campaign.

Samples were prepared in due course and forwarded to Barbados, but they turned out to be valueless on account of the excessive drying to which they had been subjected.

Further trials were made in due course. Ripe bananas were simply peeled, not sliced, and gently heated at a temperature of about  $150^{\circ}\text{F}$ . for sixty-four hours, until they reached a firm consistency, care being taken to provide ventilation, which is very essential. Another sample was dried in the sun.

Both these samples have been pronounced to be palatable articles of diet in Barbados, and large quantities forwarded to the Imperial Institute London, for commercial valuation, have been reported on favourably too. This report is published as the conclusion to this article.

As regards the cost of producing dried bananas in St. Lucia, MR. A. J. BROOKS, the Agricultural Superintendent, has furnished the following information. In the second trial referred to above, 30 lb. of ripe bananas were used. The skins when removed, weighed  $7\frac{1}{2}$  lb., thus leaving  $22\frac{1}{2}$  lb. of fresh banana. The dried bananas weighed  $6\frac{1}{2}$  lb. On this occasion the bananas were purchased at 1*d.* per lb. The original cost was therefore 2*s.* 6*d.* If the dried product of this trial sold at 6*d.* per lb. on the spot, without further charges of packing, freight, etc., it would, in MR. BROOKS' opinion, just about pay. The dried product at 6*d.* per lb. would realize 3*s.*  $1\frac{1}{2}$ *d.*, leaving a difference of  $5\frac{1}{2}$ *d.* per lb., which sum would have to cover labour in handling, loss from decay, and cost of fuel. If large quantities were handled, the cost of drying and handling would be greatly reduced. In this connexion the report of the valuers in London that the sun-dried fruit are better than the artificially-dried is important.

There is little doubt that with a good supply of sound fruit at about 1*d.* per lb. and the sun-dried products disposed of at 6*d.* per lb., a profitable industry could be established in the West Indian islands.

The following is the report on the samples from St. Lucia received from the Imperial Institute :—

*Description of Samples.*—The samples consisted respectively of sun-dried and artificially-dried bananas.

## ARROWROOT CULTIVATION.

W. MOLEGODE, S. A. I.

There is very little arrowroot grown in Ceylon.\* Why that it should be so one cannot understand as the cultivation is a simple one and the hand preparation of the arrowroot flour can be done by village women—the process being the same as the preparation of kitul flour. The following notes are written in the hope that the cultivation of arrowroot will be taken up in our villages and made a paying industry. Sometimes locally prepared arrowroot flour of a decidedly good quality is obtainable in the bazaars.

The commercial product arrowroot is obtained from the underground starchy rhizomes, tubers or bulbs of the plants *Maranta arundinacea* and *Canna edulis*. Both these are well known in Ceylon and are to be seen growing well in most parts of the Island up to 3000 feet elevation. The Sinhalese names of the former are *Araluk* or *Hulankiriya* and the latter *Bulsarana*. [*Hulankiriya* is properly *Phrynium zeylanicum*, and *Butsarana* *Canna orientalis*.—Ed.] The tubers of both species are boiled and eaten. Though arrowroot obtained from the tubers of both the plants is so much alike, there being hardly any difference noticeable, the plants themselves differ considerably from each other. The *Maranta*, commonly called the Bermuda or the true arrowroot, produces white tubers of a moderate size with thin shiny skin free of roots. The *Canna edulis*, commonly called Queensland or Australian arrowroot, produces bulbs of a purple colour in a compact mass and is a much heavier yielder. The plants themselves differ in size and colour. The *Canna* or *Bulsarana* grows to a good height often 6-8 feet. It has large broad leaves of purple colour and becomes a bush of great size. Its flowers are like those of ordinary *Canna*. The *Maranta* is a smaller plant and does not grow higher than 3 or 4 feet and has not got such a tendency to spread as *Canna edulis* and the yield per acre is much less. It has been found out that there is little actual difference between the commercial product of the two varieties, the quantity of starch, which is the chief requirement of arrowroot, being in Bermuda or *Maranta* variety 82.24 and the Queensland or *Canna* variety 81.52.

### CULTURAL NOTES.

Both the species are cultivated alike except that *Canna edulis* has to be planted giving double the space given for *Maranta*.

*Soil.*—A light loamy well-drained soil is the best. In clayey and wet lands the rhizomes will not develop properly. As the crop is an exhausting one the richer the soil the better it would be.

*Climate.*—The plants will grow from sea level to 3000 ft. above. A

*Propagation and Cultivation.*—Propagation is by means of young shoots or small bulbs. The land should be cleaned and well dug up and planted in rows of 3 feet apart and 18 inches in the rows (for *Maranta*) at the commencement of the rainy season. In the early stages of the growth the soil may be turned and drawn towards the plants. When the plants are well established the leaves will cover the ground and prevent the growth of weeds. Very little attention is necessary after this.

*Harvesting.*—The rhizomes are fully grown in about eight to nine months in the case of *Maranta* and little earlier in the case of *Canna*. The maturity of the rhizomes is indicated by the flagging and falling down of the leaves. When this occurs the crop should be lifted. If the crop is not raised in good time the bulbs will begin to shoot and the yield of starch will consequently be lessened. Too long exposure of the bulbs will have an injurious effect on the colour of the prepared arrowroot and therefore only such a quantity as could be dealt with should be lifted up. Arrowroot, like Ginger and Turmeric, can be left in the soil for two seasons and where this is done the second year's crop is of a better quality.

#### PREPARATION.

The rhizomes after being cleared of adhering earth should be well washed and roots and the outer skin completely removed and again washed. There are various methods adopted to obtain the starch, but the following is the simplest and easiest to be adopted in the villages as it is the process of extracting kitul flour to which the upcountry villager is quite used. A good supply of pure water is essential in the preparation of arrowroot and such should be close at hand. The first thing is to pound the cleaned tubers in a wooden mortar and reduce them to a pulp. A tub covered with a calico cloth must now be provided and this filled up with clean water. The calico should be so tied as to allow the water to come through it. The pulp is put on the calico and worked round and round with the hand till only the fibrous parts of the tubers are left, and the whole of the farina is suspended in the water. The calico is now removed and time allowed for the starch to settle down. The water is then removed and a fresh supply of water is added, well stirred with the starch and passed through another calico. This process should be repeated until the water over the starch is perfectly clear, when the farina is removed and dried in the sun on clean calico. Care should be taken not to expose the starch to rain as it will get discoloured.

*Yield.*—The average yield of tubers per acre may be put down at about 10 to 12,000 lb. for *Maranta* and anything up to 20,000 for *Canna edulis*. The amount of starch is about 15% of this weight and therefore the average return of a good crop would be between 1500 to 2000 lb. of arrowroot of commerce.

## PEA NUT OR GROUNDNUT.

WM. HARRIS, F. L. S.,

*Superintendent of Public Gardens and Plantations.*

The Pea nut, known also as Groundnut, Earth nut, Monkey nut, Pindar etc., is a leguminous plant known botanically as *Arachis hypogaea* Linn.

It has been so widely disseminated throughout the tropics, sub-tropics and temperate regions of the world that its native home was for long in doubt, but all evidence on the subject now points to Brazil as its native habitat.

*Description.*—The plant is a prostrate annual herb with abruptly pinnate leaves each with two pairs of leaflets. The flowers are yellow, more or less clustered in the axils of the leaves. After the fall of the flower the very small ovary is raised upon a stalk which in time attains a length of several inches and in its growth curves downwards, so that at length the small ovary at its extremity is thrust into the soft ground where the pods are ripened. Should the ovary by some accident not be able to enter the ground it withers and does not attain perfection.

*Soil.*—A well-tilled, light, sandy soil which contains a fair proportion of lime is considered best. Low-lying, wet soils are unsuitable, and heavy, dark soils which are largely impregnated with iron produce stained nuts which are not of high grade for retailing purposes. For purely agricultural purposes the colour of the shell is of no importance, and in many cases in the United States the largest yields of nuts have been obtained from soils of a rather heavy and somewhat retentive nature, soils with a good deal of clay.

*Preparation of the Soil.*—The land should be ploughed or forked to a depth of about nine inches and thoroughly broken up, and if there is a considerable amount of vegetable matter in the land it should be ploughed or forked in. If the pea nuts succeed a crop which had been manured so much the better; indeed, it would be good practice to follow a crop of yams or sweet potatoes with one of pea nuts.

*Seed.*—One of the most important points in connection with the cultivation of pea nuts is the careful selection of seeds. Those persons who pay special attention to this point are liberally rewarded for their attention. Only seed from vigorous, productive plants should be planted; the result of planting a miscellaneous collection of seed is an indifferent crop.

It would be well for anyone starting this cultivation to import good seed from the United States.

*Planting Season.*—The time for planting is before the rainy season, May or June. A moist condition of the soil is essential for the germination of the seeds and growth of the young plants.

*Planting.*—The nuts are shelled and the seeds are usually planted about eighteen inches apart in rows three feet asunder. About two inches deep is sufficient for the seeds in moist soil, but in dry soil they should be planted three inches deep, the soil being gently pressed down with the feet after planting.

Where implemental tillage is not possible closer planting than above may be practised, say eighteen inches by eighteen inches and at this spacing over 19,000 nuts would be required to plant an acre.

*Cultivation.*—When the plants are about two weeks old it will probably be quite time to hoe the weeds and loosen the soil round the plants, and this should be constantly attended to, the object being to keep the soil mellow and loose, and clear of all weeds. Hoeing should only cease when the peanut vines have met from row to row.

*Time to Mature.*—The ordinary variety grown in the West Indies takes from four to six months to mature its pods, but some of the American varieties come to maturity in about three months.

*Harvesting.*—When the pods are ripe the plants are dug up and the nuts picked off by hand.

In America, the plants with the nuts attached which are thrown in windrows by the diggers are taken up, gently shaken and placed with the nuts all inside the heap and packed round a pole, making a narrow upright shock about two and one-half feet in diameter at the base, and of nearly uniform diameter until near the top, where it is quickly drawn in and capped with hay or grass. These shocks are about five feet in height.

The nuts are allowed to remain in the shocks until they are thoroughly cured or until they are ready for picking. In some cases the shocks are lifted and carried to suitable buildings or sheds where the picking is done, but in the majority of cases the picking is done in the field.

The nuts are thoroughly cured, and are then placed in sacks and sent to a cleaning factory where they are put through a number of processes for removing all adhering sand and dirt, blanching the shells of those which are slightly discoloured, and polishing those of high grade which are to go on the market for roasting purposes. The brightest and soundest pods should be kept separate, the dark and immature pods being kept together and empty pods rejected with the vines to make hay.

*Yield.*—It is stated that in Barbados 2,000 lb. of nuts per acre is regarded as a fair average yield, whilst returns of 4,000 lb. per acre are not unknown.

In Virginia and North Carolina the average yield is about forty bushels per acre, but some growers make an average of one hundred bushels per acre with select types of large nuts, and yields of one hundred to one hundred and forty bushels per acre are reported for the Spanish nuts.

*Uses.*—Peanuts are put to a great variety of uses. Every one is familiar with the roasted nuts for eating out of hand. Great quantities are thus consumed. In addition, the nuts are manufactured into a great variety of confections and candies, while the vines, either with or without the nuts, are valuable for stock-feeding, and the growth of the plant is important in soil-renovation.

Peanut oil is one of the best known vegetable oils, but because of its high food value, quality and keeping properties, it has found little use in the arts. It is used chiefly as a substitute for olive oil. Marseilles is the great peanut oil factory of the world, the supply of nuts being drawn largely from Africa, India and Spain.

As a forage crop, the peanut vines make hay possessing a feeding value for cattle, mules and horses equal to that of clover hay. The yield of hay when the crop is well manured and cultivated ranges from one to three tons per acre. The value of the forage is each year becoming better recognized, and more careful attention is being given to harvesting the nuts in such a way as to preserve the hay in the best condition for feeding purposes.—

## MUNGO ON RICE LAND.

For rotating with lowland rice there is perhaps nothing better and more convenient to the farmer than mungo. The seed is easily obtained and grown. Mungo is a wholesome food, well liked by all classes of people, and there is at all times a ready market for any surplus product that the farmer may have. The mungo crop may be grown without the farmer incurring any extra expense for plowing, irrigating, or otherwise preparing the land, provided he does the planting on time. The best time for planting is immediately after the rice crop is harvested. The land is then usually sufficiently moist to grow a fair crop of mungo.

The seed should be sown at the rate of about 50 liters per hectare, and the sowing done when there is just sufficient surface water on the ground to properly soak and sprout the mungo bean. A greater amount of surface water especially on heavy soils, will cause the seed to rot. After the seed is sown, a brush drag is run over the field so as to shake down to the ground all the seed which might otherwise have remained suspended in the rice straw.

If this method is followed, there will be sufficient growth of mungo to keep down the weeds and to yield enough beans to pay for the seed used and the work of planting and harvesting, plus an appreciable net profit.

The yield of beans is not the only benefit to be derived from this practice. Mungo is a leguminous crop, and, as such, it fertilizes the soil. For heavy lands the leguminous crop will not only add fertility to the soil, but it will also make the ground looser and easier to work. For extensive fields, therefore, the best systems to follow is to plant as large an area as practicable with mungo every year. If, for any reason, the entire area is not planted to mungo, care should be taken that in the following season the first planting should be on the land unplanted the first season, so that ultimately all sections of the land will have been grown to this crop.

If farmers would only realize the advantage of planting some of the legumes such as mungo after his rice each year he will be surprised at the improvement in the condition of his soil, the greater ease with which he can prepare the land for his rice crop and the added yield per hectare of his rice—all money making factors that our Filipino farmers should take advantage of, if they want to make more money in their farming operations.—PHILIPPINE FARMER, Vol. V, No. 9.

## THE MARANG.

P. J. WESTER,

*Agricultural Adviser.*

The Philippine Islands have many native fruits; some of which are more or less edible and others that may be classed fair to good. The best of all is unquestionably the Marang, a tall, handsome tree, with large, dark green leaves, native to southern and western Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago.

The fruit is as large as a child's head, roundish oblong in shape and has a soft, thick skin covered with brownish, soft spines. Inside of the skin and separating from it is the creamy white, sweet, rich, juicy, well flavored and

## COWPEA PRESERVATION.

Considerable difficulty is often experienced by local planters in keeping cowpeas and other beans for any length of time free from bean weevils, and during the past many lots of beans and peas kept in storerooms awaiting favourable weather for planting have been destroyed by these pests. It is fairly well-known that applications of carbon bisulphide will free grain from weevils, but there are several drawbacks to its being commonly used for this purpose. In the first place, carbon bisulphide is expensive, and in addition there is an element of danger always present when it is handled, on account of its being highly inflammable. Its obnoxious odour also renders it unpopular.

Two lots of cowpeas were put up in open bottles. One lot was stored in air-slaked lime at the rate of 4 parts of peas to  $1\frac{1}{2}$  parts of lime by weight, and one lot was stored without lime. The experiment was commenced in January and was carried on for six months. Cowpeas which were untreated were attacked by weevils in four weeks' time, and those put up with lime were quite free from weevil attacks in July, six months after storing. Germination tests were made in July, and the peas kept in lime gave 96 per cent. The untreated lot was badly damaged by weevils, and thrown away some considerable time before the experiment terminated.

From the above it will be seen that planters have a cheap and effective method of keeping cowpeas free from a pest which in the past has caused them a considerable amount of worry. Although cowpeas are specially mentioned in the above, it is possible that many other grains which are being kept for planting purposes can be preserved in a similar manner.—REPORT OF AGRIC. DEPT., ANTIGUA, 1917-18.

## THE CULTIVATION OF SISAL HEMP IN MYSORE.\*

A. CHATTERTON, B. Sc.,

*Director, Sandal-wood oil Factories, Mysore State.*

In this note which is specially written for the people of Mysore, it is hardly necessary to point out that aloes of various species in most parts of the State grow luxuriantly and are largely planted to form hedges. From the leaves of these aloes fibre is extracted and used locally for the manufacture of ropes and twine. The fibre is not easy to extract as the leaves yield an acrid juice and the separation of the fibre from the pulp is a tedious operation. The industry, if it can be so called, is a purely domestic one carried on merely to supply local needs. There are machines which will easily extract the fibre; but they are costly and require a large amount of power to drive them. They are hopelessly beyond the reach of the cultivators. Further, it is an unfortunate fact that, although aloes are extremely plentiful in Mysore, the species which have been introduced are those which yield but a small percentage of a weak fibre which has a comparatively small commercial value.

There is, however, one species of aloe, the botanical name of which is *Agave rigida* var. *Sisalana*, which is very largely cultivated for fibre in Yucatan, the Bahamas, Florida, what was formerly German East Africa and, to a very limited extent, in some parts of India. This aloe yields a very valuable fibre which is in great demand for the manufacture of binder twine, of which enormous quantities are employed in America every harvest. It was introduced into Mysore by the late Mr. RICKERTS in 1892 and since then has been to some extent cultivated at the Lal-Bagh in Bangalore and from those gardens fairly large numbers of young plants have been supplied to various parts of India. So far as Mysore is concerned, nothing practical has resulted from the introduction of this plant; but just outside the borders of the State in Coorg, a small plantation of sisal hemp has been worked successfully for the past five years.

In 1914, in Bulletin No. 18 published by the Industries and Commerce Committee of the Mysore Economic Conference, I drew attention to the commercial possibilities of sisal hemp in Mysore; but so far no one has been induced to take up the cultivation of this species of aloe on a commercial scale. After an interval of four and half years, during which I have had opportunities of watching the growth of sisal hemp plants, I am still of opinion that its commercial possibilities in Mysore are very great; but I have realised that nothing is likely to result from advocating the formation of planting companies to take up extensive areas of land with a view to the establishment of large plantations.

" The large succulent growth obtained in rich land is inimical both to the quantity and quality of fibre produced ; therefore somewhat poor land of a loose stony nature is always preferred, as in addition to giving better results generally it accommodates a larger number of plants to the acre. Mysore possesses plenty of such land, and if the latter can produce leaves of four feet in length it will do ; but there is a reduction in value, as well as difficulty in extracting the fibre, when the leaves are under three feet in length."

From other authorities on the cultivation of sisal hemp, I have gathered information which may be briefly summarised as follows :—Sisal hemp will grow on arid land that seems incapable of any other vegetable life ; but the best fibre is grown on a calcareous gravelly soil of medium quality. No fertilizer is ever used. Once the plant has taken root in the ground, no further attention is necessary and the cost of cultivation is negligible. The fibre is, however, difficult to extract by hand but is easily got out by suitable machinery. It takes from five to six years for the plant to grow to the proper maturity to begin cutting leaves. When the plant is growing, all its leaves point upwards ; as the plants mature, the leaves begin to open ; as they open, they begin to fall towards a horizontal position. As soon as the leaf becomes horizontal, it is perfectly mature and must be promptly cut in order to secure from it the most fibre in the best condition. If properly cared for, a plant continues to yield leaves for about fourteen years in rocky soil and eight years in fertile soil ; but if the leaves which become horizontal are not properly cut, then the plant throws up a stalk instead of leaves and this is the death of the plant. If the cutting of the leaves be neglected, the plant throws up a stalk and when the stalk appears, there is great danger that all the plants in its vicinity will also send up the stalk and die. When the plant reaches the limit of its age, this stalk grows from the centre of the plant and the plant dies. Leaves weigh from 1 to  $1\frac{1}{2}$  pounds and 1,000 leaves will yield from 50 to 75 pounds of fibre.

On suitable land, bearing 1,000 plants to the acre, the yield of fibre may be reckoned at not less than one pound per plant per annum or 1,000 pounds per acre. The fibre was worth in London about £30 per ton before the war

21------------------------------------------------

382[DECEMBER, 1919.

and is likely to remain so owing to the increased cost of cultivation and difficulties which have arisen in two of the largest areas of production. These are Yucatan in Mexico and German East Africa.

It appears to me that the principal reasons why sisal hemp cultivation has not been taken up in Mysore although the plant grows readily enough and yields a high quality of fibre are as follows :—

1. (1) Six years must elapse before any return can be expected ;
2. (2) The extraction of the fibre by hand is not possible as a commercial proposition ;
3. (3) The extraction of the fibre by machinery necessitates operation on a large scale and the investment of a large capital on machinery and plant ;
4. (4) There is no local market for the fibre and small parcels do not command a fair price when exported.

Objections of a similar nature and of equal or greater intensity retarded the development of the cultivation of both tea and coffee and the establishment of the tea industry in India is largely due to efforts on the part of the Government of India at the outset. These industries are still mainly in the hands of European planters, though there is now, both in Assam in connection with the tea industry and in the South of India in connection with coffee, a marked tendency towards the development of Indian-owned plantations.

I have already referred to the plantation which has been started in Coorg and in course of time other sisal hemp estates may be opened out. There are, however, excellent reasons why the matter should not be left to chance. Moreover, it is desirable that the cultivation of sisal hemp should be so developed that the ordinary cultivators of the country should, as far as possible, participate in the benefits to be derived from it.

The Mysore Durbar obtains a not inconsiderable percentage of its revenue from mineral royalties and from the sale of electric energy which is still mainly used on the Gold Fields. Sooner or later, this source of revenue will be extinguished with the exhaustion of the Mines and it will be a wise policy, while there is yet time, to create new sources of revenue to take its place. The electric energy may be used for other purposes, provided industries are created which will employ the power. Satisfactory progress in this direction has already been made and there seems every prospect that, as electric energy becomes available, a market for it will be found in the growing industries of the State ; but the gold royalties will gradually decrease and ultimately disappear and it will be sound policy to look well ahead and endeavour to create new sources of revenue. In the last few years, the public attitude in respect to the intervention of the State in industrial matters has completely changed and there is no reason why the Mysore Durbar should not take active measures to create new industries if it is in a position to do so.

It appears to me then that an excellent opportunity to do something in this direction is presented by the failure of private enterprise to take advantage of the natural facilities which exist for the cultivation of the sisal

hemp plant. About one-half the area of the Mysore State is uncultivated and there are obviously tens of thousands of acres of waste land which could be well utilised for the cultivation of a plant which grows best on soils which are perhaps favourably described as being on the margin of cultivation.

If the raiyat were in full possession of the information contained in this note and further if he could be induced to accept it as approximately true, there is scarcely any doubt that, provided he was not asked to find capital which he does not possess, he would readily take to the cultivation of sisal hemp. The problem then to be solved is to convince the raiyat that it is worth his while to grow sisal hemp and this can only be done by removing the difficulties which have hitherto prevented the development of the cultivation. This can be done through the agency of the Economic Conference, the District Committees and the various associations which have been established to develop the material resources of the State. Village Committees have been formed in thousands and it is possible that village communal plantations could be started which would ultimately provide these local bodies with a revenue which could be usefully employed on the various schemes of village improvements which are under contemplation.

Government can assign land for the cultivation of sisal hemp free of kist for a period of say seven years, a sufficient time to enable the plants to be brought to maturity. It can supply the plants, charging only the actual cost, and deferring the payment till the raiyat begins to derive an income from them. It can also establish nurseries and model plantations in selected centres to demonstrate its faith in the future of sisal hemp in the Mysore State, and to afford ready means whereby the raiyats can obtain young plants and instructions how to cultivate them; but it is necessary that Government should go a great deal further than this as individual raiyat will be unable to extract the fibre from his plants and, even if he did extract it by hand, he would be unable to obtain a fair market for it. Government must therefore be prepared to guarantee to take over the crop of leaves when they are mature and to provide the necessary machinery for extracting the fibre, also to undertake the marketing of the crop.

It is difficult to frame an accurate estimate of the returns to be obtained from the cultivation of sisal hemp, as I am not in a position to state what price the hemp will fetch five or six years hence and I can only make an assumption on this point. The price, in London, immediately before the war was from £28 to £30 a ton and it is almost certain that it will be considerably higher than this after the war. I think it will therefore be fair to assume that sisal hemp will realise three annas per pound or Rs. 420 per ton and as each plant will yield a pound of fibre and at 1,000 plants to the acre, the gross return will be Rs. 187-8-0 per acre. #### GENERAL CONCLUSIONS.

MESSRS. ROSE, DOWNs & THOMPSON point out that by chemical solvent extraction there would be no difficulty in reducing the oil-content of the residual meal, if desirable, to 1.5—2 per cent.

If the seed were expressed in hydraulic mills, they state that there would be no advantage in cold pressing first, in order to obtain a paler oil, followed by hot pressing to get the maximum amount of oil. Two pressings of heated seed, if necessary and economical, would be preferable.

In the consignment sent, the whole seed contained about 45 per cent. of shell to 55 per cent. of kernel. If the shell were removed before expression it could be used as fuel or possibly as a manure, although its value is not great for this purpose.

MESSRS. ROSE, DOWNs & THOMPSON also point out, with regard to the problem of machinery for expressing oil, that it would not be economical to run a plant for say about three months in each year and also that it is desirable to run such plant day and night, and not during the day only, stopping say at week-ends only. The writer has continually emphasized this point in discussing the running of oil machinery in this country.

## SHIPPING COCONUT OIL IN BULK.

"With reference to the difficulty connected with the shipping of coconut oil in bulk in tank steamers," writes MR. B. J. EATON in the *Straits Bulletin*, which is due to the solidification of the oil in cooler climates, the recommendation contained in the paper entitled 'Copra and Coconut Products,' published in the AGRICULTURAL BULLETIN Vol. VI., No. 12, November and December, 1918, shows how this difficulty could be easily overcome by the use of steam coils in or around the tanks." Following this comes the information received from MESSRS. HARPER & Co., Kuala Lumpur, on behalf of the Asiatic Petroleum Company, which should be of interest. The letter states:—

"It will be of interest to your Department to learn that regular shipments of coconut oil in bulk have been made from Java to the Pacific Coast of America in the double bottoms of cargo steamers. As stated in the article in the AGRICULTURAL BULLETIN, the difficulty of solidification in winter can easily be overcome by fitting steam coils, the installation of which, in double bottoms, presents no difficulty."

One of the greatest difficulties in connection with export of the oil is therefore easily overcome.

This method of overcoming the difficulty was pointed out in the TROPICAL LIFE as long ago as June, 1914 (p. 112,) after we had discussed the problem fully with the head of a leading West Indian shipping firm, used to handling not only vegetable oils, but also pitch in all degrees of hardness or softness.

"What the shipper has to remember," we pointed out, "is that vegetable oils, which in the Tropics remain liquid, and can easily be pumped into the tanks, become denser and more solid as they approach the temperate zone, until, when the time comes to unload the cargo, it is no longer liquid but solid, especially if, as can quite well happen, a temperature below freezing-point prevails at the unloading port at the time of the vessel's arrival. To overcome this—which, we take it, would be no trouble but an advantage on the voyage, since a solid cargo is less dangerous than a liquid one to carry—steam coils should be, and properly would be, introduced into the ship's tanks, and by means of these the oil would be heated and liquefied sufficiently to be pumped out. As to what heat would be necessary, must depend entirely on the length of piping through which the oil has to pass and the temperature outside, for if the thermometer was below freezing-point, then the oil would require to be sufficiently warmed to keep it liquid until it reached the storage tank ashore, or the transport receptacle waiting to move it inland. We leave it to experts to say the temperature required. It would have to be about 90°F., and if carefully purified oil, as for human consumption, is being handled, it might even go as high as 280 to 300°F., we believe, without fear of harming the oil; but as a rule 120 to 150°F., should be sufficient, unless the piping extends a considerable distance, and the weather was unusually cold."

As things tend to drift to-day, the shipment of coconut and other vegetable oils in bulk must be taken into account as likely to be necessary. If its movement through piping of a considerable length proved necessary, this could be arranged if here and there it ran through a heated chamber where any idea of becoming thick and solid would be at once prevented.—

TROPICAL LIFE, Vol. XV, No. 9.

# SOILS AND MANURES.

## MANURES.

Comparatively little attention has been paid to the question of manuring in this country, and with the extraordinarily rapid development of food production during the past few months it is hardly to be expected that much would be done in this direction at the commencement. In most cases new land was utilized and the fertility of such soils is generally sufficiently rich to produce one or more good harvests without resorting to manuring. The clays and loams will stand successive cropping and give comparatively good returns for a considerable period without fertilizers while the type of soil usually found on hilly and undulating country more quickly loses its fertility and on it a manuring programme becomes necessary at a much earlier date. The constant removal of crops from the land will sooner or later have a direct influence on the returns and ultimately the soil becomes exhausted. A proper system of crop rotation and thorough cultivation will go a very long way in maintaining fertility and it is perhaps unnecessary to resort to manures until such soils fail to produce average returns in yield.

The type of soil and rotation must be taken into consideration in making a selection of the manures. Light soils require the addition of natural manures to improve their physical condition as well as their chemical state and attention should first be given to building up the soil in organic matter before resorting to artificial fertilizers. This is particularly important in the tropics where very heavy rainfall is experienced and where consequently soluble artificial manures, such as nitrate of soda, are liable to be lost in the drainage water, more especially in light soils.

Improvements as regards actual plant food in a soil can be effected in two ways.

(1) The addition of substances containing plant food.

(2) The addition of substances which may act upon the insoluble compounds present in the soil and render available the plant food they contain. Manures restore the fertility of an exhausted soil and maintain the fertility of land under constant cropping. The immediate effect of a manure depends on its solubility and suitability of the manure for the use of the crop to which it is applied. Soluble manures are immediately available for plant food and should therefore only be applied when crops are on the land otherwise a large part of the fertilizer may be lost in the drainage. Manures which slowly become available may be applied when the land is being tilled. Manures are divided in two classes, natural manures and artificial manures. Organic manures have their origin from vegetable or animal matter. Most of the organic matter in soils is derived from decaying vegetation. The inorganic manures are of mineral origin. The common organic manures consist of farmyard manures (Cattle, horse, sheep, pig, hen, and pigeon dung) guano, fish refuse, sea weed, dried blood, meat guano, bones soot, and oil cakes. The artificial fertilizers are divided into three classes

nitrogenous, potash and phosphatic. The chief nitrogenous manures are sodium nitrate (Chili saltpetre,) ammonium sulphate, calcium cyanamid, and potassium nitrate. Potash manures consist of muriate of potash, the double sulphate of magnesium and potassium, sulphate of potash and kainite (crude sulphate of potassium and magnesium). The common phosphatic manures are—Mineral superphosphates, bone superphosphates and basic slag, while there are a number of miscellaneous manures such as common salt, lime, ferrous sulphate and gas lime.

*Organic Manures.*—Organic manures, more especially farmyard, improve the physical condition of the soil, making light soils heavier and heavy soils lighter in the same way as green manuring. Farmyard manure is of high value for improving the permanent fertility of a soil as it not only replaces the valuable chemical elements in quantity but also its physical properties cannot be over-estimated. Well rotted cattle manure which has been little exposed to atmospheric influence is the best form to apply. Farmyard manure is not usually available in quantities on estates and in such cases one may substitute other organic manure, for preference horse, goat or poultry manure but guano, bone meal, ground nut cake and poonac are all useful. Guano is of high manurial value but requires to be judiciously used. It is best mixed with 4 to 6 times its weight of earth and applied sparingly to the land. The mixture may be applied at the rate of 4 to 5 cwt. per acre. Guano can be obtained from a number of caves in the limestone formations throughout the Peninsula, where there are considerable stores. There are various forms in which bone may be applied, such as  $\frac{1}{2}$  inch bone, bone meal, bone dust, dissolved bone, etc. Bones are considered to have the best effect on soils deficient in lime. Bone manure is used principally as a source of phosphoric acid. The decomposition of crushed bones is slow, varying with the degree of fineness, their fertilizing properties are lasting, while dissolved bones are more rapid in action. Bones are specially beneficial to pasture land but they are generally suitable for root crops. The rate of application of bone meal is 3 cwt. to the acre while that of steamed bone flour is 2 cwt. Poonac or any of the other oil cakes are valuable organic manures and are applied at the rate of about 8 to 10 cwt. to the acre.

*Application of Organic Manures.*—The time of applying organic manures will depend on a number of circumstances such as the soil, climate and kind of crop, but in this country it may generally be taken that organic manures should be applied when the land is being prepared for a root crop. The manure should be turned under, so as to permit of rapid decomposition, but it is a mistake to bury the manure too deeply in the soil, a depth of about 4 inches can generally be recommended. When a root crop takes its place in a rotation, then an organic manure may be applied when the land is being prepared for the root crop.

*Artificial Manures.*—One application of an organic manure during a short rotation such as is likely to be adhered to in this country is sufficient and any further manuring required would be on the line of artificial fertilizers. It is unlikely that any rotation, here, will extend over three years in which case one small dressing of a natural manure at intervals of three years is ample, the rate of application being reduced when the period of rotation is less.

(1) *Nitrogenous*.—Nitrates and ammonia salts should either be applied as a top dressing when the plants are young or only shortly after sowing the seed. Nitrate of soda is readily soluble and for this reason is unsuitable for this country as the very heavy rain storms would quickly remove it from the root feeding area. Sulphate of Ammonia is more suitable here, for, although soluble, it is partially retained in the soil. It may be applied at the rate of about  $1\frac{1}{2}$  cwt. per acre as a top dressing. Sulphate of Ammonia is a more useful manure than nitrate of soda as it may be used as an ingredient or mixed manures. A cereal crop which is rather backward in the early stages of growth may be stimulated by a dressing of a nitrogenous manure which increases the colour and vigour of the foliage. Potassium nitrate is also an effective form of applying nitrogen.

(2) *Potash*.—An application of a potash manure is of special benefit to land which is deficient in humus and particularly to land which has not been dunged. Clay soils have an abundance of potash for the needs of the plant and do not require further addition of this element. Leguminous crops generally are most benefitted by potash. When applied to cereals it increases the yield to some extent and makes the crop less liable to "lodge." Potash improves the quality of the produce and the cause of spotted leaves is often due to a deficiency of this constituent. Sulphate of potash should contain 50% of potash and is considered one of the best forms for application. Sulphate and muriate of potash are about equal in action and may be applied at the rate of from 1 to 2 cwt. per acre. Kainit is also a common potash manure and may be used at the rate of 2 to 3 cwt. per acre. Potash manures are most effective when applied one or two months before sowing, there is little risk of any serious loss taking place owing to rain.

*Phosphatic*.—The Malayan soils are, as a general rule, deficient in phosphate. Phosphate manures hasten maturity, have a ripening effect, increase the yield and stimulate the root development in young plants. The common phosphatic manures are mineral superphosphate, bone superphosphate and basic slag. Superphosphates may be applied as a top dressing just before sowing seed, at the rate of about 3 cwt. per acre. Superphosphates are particularly suitable for the growth of root crops and are the best form of phosphatic manure for the lighter class of soils. Superphosphates should not be mixed with any form of lime, basic slag or nitrate of soda. Basic slag is also an important phosphatic manure and acts best on clay soils while it is particularly valuable as supplying a readily available phosphate to soils of an acid nature. Basic slag is applied at the rate of about 5 cwt. to the acre.

*Lime and its Application*.—One of the chief effects of lime on a soil is to accelerate nitrification and to decompose insoluble potassic minerals in the soil and thus enable plants to draw upon the nitrogenous and potash stores in the ground. It has a neutralizing action on organic acids and thereby helps to sweeten sour soils. Lime has a beneficial action on clay soils by breaking them up, while sandy or light soils are improved by binding the particles together. In order to obtain the full benefits of artificial fertilizers we consider it is essential to apply a small dressing of lime beforehand, this should be done several months previous to the spreading of the fertilizers on the land. There are many soils in Malaya which would, in all probability, respond well to lime treatment. Quick lime is the best form of application,

## INFLUENCE OF TILLAGE ON THE BACTERIA OF SOILS.

Tillage has a direct influence on the activity of soil bacteria. The presence of oxygen is essential for the nitrifying organisms which are responsible for the decomposition of organic matter, therefore tillage, which increases the supply of oxygen, increases the activity of the organisms. The organic matter cannot be used directly by plants, it must be first converted to some soluble form before it is available as food. The nitrogen of the organic matter is converted to nitrites and finally to nitrates which are soluble; this decomposition is due to oxidation and the action of the bacteria referred to. Many of the chemical changes brought about by organisms result in the supply of plant food; hence the importance of thorough cultivation to induce bacterial action. In the absence of air, anaerobic bacterial action is induced and this fermentation is unfavourable to plant growth.

Before it is possible to recommend any particular form of tillage, one must be familiar with the type of soil, the past history of the land, the habit and special requirements of the crop. The nature of the surface layer has a direct bearing on the depth and kind of tillage. Where the topsoil is rich in humus and the subsoil poor, it would be most unwise to invert the top layer and bring the subsoil to the surface. If deep cultivation is required under such soil conditions, then, an implement must be used which will stir the land without inverting it. With land recently brought under cultivation deep cultivation should not be practised except under most unusual circumstances. The rich surface layers should be made full use of first before any form of deep tillage is resorted to, this especially applies when growing cereal and pulse crops. The depth of cultivation may very gradually be increased as time progresses. Land which has been brought under cultivation from jungle should have tillage to a depth of about three or four inches for cereals and pulses but in some instances where you have a very friable soil even this light surface cultivation is unnecessary, provided the plants are transplanted from a nursery, but with seed broadcast a slight stirring of the surface is always essential to provide a good seed bed. In opening up grass land, the form of tillage varies with the kind of grass and spread of the root. Grass which has

Grass of the former type may be effectively eradicated by tillage to a depth of six inches, twice over, with an interval of a fortnight between the applications, and followed by frequent light stirring of the soil to remove the roots. Ploughs may be used for performing the first operation but subsequent treatment and the removal of roots is best undertaken by zigzag harrows and cultivators. With deep rooted grass, as "lalang," the common method of eradication by the changkol is too well known to require description, but it might be mentioned that generally three changkollings of the land, at intervals of about a fortnight are required, the first operation being carried out to a depth of about 15 inches but of course this varies somewhat according to class of soil. The second changkolling is around 8 inches and the final, comparatively shallow. Eradicating lalang in this way is most expensive and objectionable on account of the time, which is often most important, when the land requires to be rapidly cultivated to take advantage of a suitable planting season. With the use of modern machinery such as the tractor, "lalang" may be destroyed by frequent applications of the plough and harrow in such a way as gradually to starve the plant out of existence. This can be done at a small cost as compared with the changkol. The depth and kind of tillage must be such as will suit the particular crop grown. Plants with a shallow root system require shallow cultivation but deep rooted plants, as for instance the root crops must have deep cultivation. From observations made at the Agricultural Reserve, Petaling Hill, Kuala Lumpur, it appeared that deep cultivation for the cereals was objectionable and that the maximum returns were obtained from cultivation to a depth of about four inches. This particularly applies to new land but in later years it is desirable to increase the depth so as to bring up the lower layers in order that they may be influenced by the various agents which render plant food available and thereby replenish the surface layers which may be somewhat reduced in fertility. Poor soils and those which have been under cultivation for many years, should have deeper cultivation than rich productive ones. Cereals are shallow rooted plants and the feeding area for the roots is confined to the surface layers. Within certain limits, the deeper the tillage the more food becomes available for the crop but this can be very much overdone on productive land and especially when growing shallow rooted plants. With root crops deep tillage is essential and when grown on new land, it is of great importance to see that deep stirring does not invert the layers. This can be best overcome by ridging and it has been proved by long experience that root crops grown on furrows give superior yields to those obtained on the flat. There are many advantages derived from ridging, the most important being (1) The root feeding area is increased without drawing, to any extent, upon the sub-soil. (2) The possibilities of the soil becoming water-logged are reduced when the tubers are developed, this is specially important on flat land and particularly when the soil is heavy. (3) The possibility of the tubers rotting is reduced. (4) The yield is increased. (5) The cost of lifting is reduced.

## LIME ON FARM LANDS.

### TESTING FOR ACIDITY.

During the last two or three years there has been a great deal of interest taken in the question of applying lime to farm lands. It must not be thought that all soils are acid, for some, by reason of their origin, are well supplied with lime. These are almost invariably strong, productive soils, and stock fed on their crops are thrifty, with plenty of bone.

On the other hand, large areas of land are just as naturally poor in this constituent from the outset, and under cultivation the supply is soon so reduced that there is not enough of it to do the work which has apparently been assigned to it in crop production. Stiff clays and wet, low-lying and ill-drained soils are apt to become sour, probably due to the fact that there is little chance for the acid materials found in the soil to drain away. Such soils should be drained first and then limed.

Lime has three main actions in the soil; it neutralises the acids formed, it improves the physical condition, and it is apparently essential for the proper development of certain plants and organisms in the soil.

The acidity of a soil is due to the presence of acids or acid salts, and they may be either organic or inorganic. If this acid is to be counteracted or neutralised, there must be some material of an alkali or basic nature present to combine with the acid and form a neutral salt, and thus destroy the acids formed. This is one of the functions of lime in a soil. If the soil is naturally poor in lime, or has been depleted of its natural store of lime, the soil will probably be sour.

### WHY WELL WATER IS HARD.

The natural chemical changes that take place in cultivated soil tend to bring its soluble plant food constituents into a soluble form. These changes are essential to render the food available to the plant. But, among these food constituents, lime, at least, is rendered soluble faster than is necessary for food purposes. Naturally, unless it is again taken up in an insoluble form, it must gradually be carried downwards with the water that sinks into the soil.

For this reason the water of wells and many springs is hard or limey. For the same reason a subsoil contains more lime than a surface soil, and the latter may become so leached that it does not contain sufficient lime to

46------------------------------------------------

DECEMBER, 1919.]407

neutralise the acids formed in the decay of the organic matter when the soil becomes acid or "sour." It is well to remember that the richer the soil is in decaying vegetable matter, and the more thorough the cultivation, the faster the lime will be rendered soluble and leached away. For the same reason, the longer the land has been under cultivation, the faster the lime will be rendered soluble and the more likely it is to need lime.

But the addition of lime does a great deal more than simply neutralise the acid of a soil. It influences the tilth or texture of a soil. This is most marked and most beneficial in the case of clay and clays loams, rendering them less tenacious when wet, and more friable and mellow when dry. This it does by causing the fine particles of the clay to gather into larger units, and makes the soil act more like one made up of larger particles. The flocculation does away with the stiff, waxy, impervious nature common to clays deficient in lime, and renders them drier, warmer, better aerated, with a larger content of moisture available for plant growth. It also brings about a more favourable condition for root development; improves drainage, and permits the farmer to cultivate his clay soil earlier in the spring, with all the advantages that naturally follow.

The action of lime on sandy soils is somewhat similar to that on clay, that is, it binds, or cements, the sand particles together; but the effect on the soil is different. It renders the soil closer in texture, and thus, being less open and porous, it does not dry out so readily in seasons of drought.

#### A CHEAP TEST.

Many methods have been devised for determining the amount of acid in a soil, but none of these are suitable for field use. In most cases, it is sufficient to ascertain the fact that the soil is acid. For this purpose a fairly satisfactory test can be made with blue litmus paper, which can be purchased at almost any drug store. It is sometimes sold in sheets or in little "books," which contain about twenty-five or fifty strips of the paper, about one half inch wide and two or three inches long. This is the most convenient form in which to have the test paper. If the sheets are purchased, they may be cut into strips the size of those in the books and placed in a clean, dry, wide-mouthed, well-corked bottle to keep them from acid fumes. When this paper comes in contact with an acid it turns red.

A very simple method and a very satisfactory one of applying the test is to make a ball of damp soil, break it open and lay the paper on the broken surface, squeeze the parts together again, and allow to stand for from five to ten minutes. If on opening the ball it is found that the blue paper has turned red, the soil is acid and in need of lime. It is apparent that the hands must be free from acid and the soil damp enough to press into shape. Unless the soil is very dry, it is generally found that by getting a sample three or four inches below the surface, there is enough moisture to answer the purpose.

If the soil is moderately dry the change in colour may appear only in spots, and greater pressure or longer time may be needed to make a satisfactory test. The rapidity of change and intensity of colour developed indicate to some extent the relative degree of acidity. To overcome accidental difference in the soil, the test should be repeated several times, with samples taken from different parts.—SOUTH AFRICAN SUGAR JOURNAL. Vol. 3, No. 9.

## WASTE LIME FROM ACETYLENE MANUFACTURE.

Since the present output of lime and lime product in Britain is far below the quantity that could be economically used for agricultural purposes, it is very desirable that full use should be made of those industrial by-products which contain lime in one form or another. Of these may be mentioned the lime waste from causticising processes in soap and bleachworks, carbonate of lime from munition works, and also the waste lime from the manufacture of acetylene gas. Some idea of the production of these materials may be gathered from the fact that the pre-war consumption of calcium carbide alone equalled about 25,000 tons, which would result in the production of more than 50,000 tons of fresh lime waste per annum.

The main objection to the use of such by-products is that they are generally in a moist condition and in consequence will not bear the cost of transit for long distances. On the other hand, they are largely produced in industrial districts, and it is exactly in these localities that, owing to the continued action of acid fumes from the air, the soil frequently becomes sour, and its productive power suffers in consequence.

*Waste Lime from Calcium Carbide.*—It is common knowledge that when water is allowed to act on calcium carbide, or carbide of lime, an energetic production of acetylene gas takes place and a residue of slaked lime is left, together with a little dissolved acetylene and any impurities which the commercial carbide may have contained. In general, the amount of the impurities is not likely to rise very high, since it is to the interest of the makers to attain as great a degree of purity of product as possible, but some amount of uncertainty has attached to the possible harmful effect of traces of sulphides and sulpho-cyanides which have been detected from time to time. With the object of ascertaining whether these impurities were present in sufficient quantity to exercise any deleterious effect on plant growth, experiments were carried out last season by three institutions—Oxford University, Leeds University and Rothamsted—at the request of the Food Production Department. ## AN OUTBREAK OF BLACK ROT IN UPPER ASSAM.

A. C. TUNSTALL, B.Sc.

In the third issue of the Quarterly Journal last year a description was given of a fungus disease called Black Rot which attacks tea in Southern India and Ceylon and causes serious damage there. In July this year a serious outbreak of this disease has occurred on two or three gardens in Upper Assam. There is evidence to show that the disease was present on one of these gardens last year, and it is probable that it was also to be found on others. The dull wet weather which has been so prevalent this season apparently favours the distribution of the disease with the result that it spread over a considerable area. At first the managers of the gardens concerned thought that the disease was merely Grey Blight, but as it was spreading so rapidly one of them sent specimens to Tocklai. As soon as the disease was identified the manager was informed by wire and instructed to remove and burn all the diseased shoots. This work was put in hand at once and as soon as possible afterwards the Mycologist visited the garden taking with him a number of spraying machines. The disease apparently originated in an old seedbari which had been cut down. From this it had spread over about sixty acres of tea of which twenty were severely attacked; the remainder had groups of diseased bushes here and there. At first sight the disease resembled Grey Blight but the diseased leaves adhered to one another wherever they happened to be in contact. Although the diseased patches on the leaves were generally grey in colour, there were none of the black dots characteristic of Grey Blight. Examination with the lens showed that there was a very thin film of fungus spreading over the under-surface of the leaves. Microscopic examination showed that the fungus was wholly external, in which respect it resembles Thread Blight. No spores were found and it was obvious from the manner in which the attack had taken place, that the distribution had not been by means of spores. The bushes were large and well-grown and in most cases in contact with their neighbours. The fungus had spread from bush to bush and the distribution had been aided by the coolies, working in the tea, rubbing against the diseased bushes thus transferring the fungus from bush to bush. The manager had pruned a number of the diseased bushes and burnt the prunings. This had checked the disease but was rather drastic. It was found that the careful removal of the diseased leaves and dead shoots followed by the application of Lime Sulphur solution checked the disease satisfactorily. The badly infested area was treated all over. In order to economise labour only the diseased bushes and those in their vicinity were treated in the slightly affected areas. The two rows of tea on either side of the roads leading from the infected areas

50------------------------------------------------

DECEMBER, 1919.]411

were dusted with sulphur with the aid of dusting machines. A neighbouring garden was also infected with this disease. In this case as it was impossible to treat the whole area infected it was decided to isolate that area as much as possible and treat all outbreaks on the other sections as soon as they were noticed. The infected area will be treated in the cold weather by removing all the diseased leaves and pruning out of dead shoots. On this garden, it is interesting to note that the distribution of the disease was obviously aided by the coolies passing from section to section. After returning to Tocklai the Mycologist received specimens of this disease from another garden in Upper Assam, and it would seem probable that quite a number of gardens are infected. At present the fungus does not seem to produce any spores, but it is impossible to say that it will not do so. It is therefore important that it should be eradicated as soon as possible. It is capable of doing very considerable damage even with its present limited methods of distribution. Should they be increased by the formation of spores it would possibly be a more serious disease in Upper Assam than Blister Blight is in Darjeeling. Fortunately the gardens in Upper Assam are very carefully cultivated and it should be possible with a little care to prevent this disease getting a serious hold.

The disease is caused by a fungus called *Hypochnus theae* or a very nearly allied species. The following characteristics distinguish this disease from others, such as Grey Blight, Brown Blight, and Copper Blight.

1. 1. As a general rule there are no spots on the grey patches. There may be a few spots caused by other fungi living on the dead tissue.
2. 2. The leaves and twigs adhere to each other at points of contact.
3. 3. Examination with a lens shows a very thin film of fungus on the undersides of leaves freshly attacked.

When this disease is suspected specimens should be sent at once to Tocklai, meanwhile the diseased leaves should be plucked off, carried away in bags and destroyed. It should be arranged so that coolies will not have to pass from the diseased bushes direct to work in other disease-free areas on the garden.

At the present time there is no need for serious alarm. In spite of the exceptionally favourable weather the disease has not spread to a very great extent, and unless it produces spores, it does not seem likely to do very serious damage if a reasonable effort is made to keep it in hand. The fungus was found in damp corners on the Cachar bheels by the Mycologist some years before but as no specimens have ever been received at Tocklai from that district it may be assumed that the disease has not caused any serious damage.—QUARTERLY JOURNAL OF INDIAN TEA ASSN., PART III. 1918.

## BEAN AND PEA WEEVILS.

Stored grains and cereal products are subject to attacks by several kinds of insects in the Tropics, to such an extent as to interfere seriously with the growing of these crops. The planter does not care to grow large quantities for storage because the loss from weevils is so great.

51------------------------------------------------

412[DECEMBER, 1919.

As a safeguard against a certain amount of such loss there have been established in Antigua and St. Vincent, under Government control, drying machines in which Indian corn has been conditioned. In St. Vincent in particular, maize has been treated for the purpose of drying out excess moisture, and thereby preventing the growth of moulds and mildews, and also for the purpose of killing the insects in the grain. This, with proper arrangements for storage in insect-proof bins, constitutes a measure of protection against insects and fungi in stored grains, and it may be applied to those species which infest peas and beans, as well as the "weevil" and other pests in maize. The trouble experienced in storing seeds and grains is not confined to tropical regions. All temperate countries have the same trouble in greater or less degree.

The United States Department of Agriculture recently (September 1918) issued FARMERS' BULLETIN, No. 983, entitled Bean and Pea Weevils, by DR. E. A. BACK and MR. A. B. DUCKETT, and the Department of Agriculture, Union of South Africa, at the same time issued a similar publication, Bulletin No. 12, 1918, entitled Pea and Bean Weevils, by MR. S. F. SKAIFE, B. A.

It is significant that at a time like the present, when the demand for foodstuffs is pressing and the need for conservation great that these two great agricultural countries, one in the north temperate and the other in the south temperate zone, should be dealing with the same group of insect pests in order to help the growers and others to save as much as possible of the valuable leguminous grains.

The weevils which attack beans and peas in the West Indies appear to be of three species: the bean weevil (*Bruchus obtectus*), the cowpea weevil (*B. chinensis*), and the four-spotted bean weevil (*B. quadrimaculatus*). The injuries resulting to peas and beans in storage from the attacks of these insects is usually attributed to weevils, without distinguishing between the several species. It would appear likely, however, that the cowpea weevil is the most abundant in the West Indies. The pea weevil (*B. pisorum*) is sometimes to be found, but it probably is merely imported with peas from northern localities, and does not breed here. Several other species of the genus *Bruchus* are recorded as occurring in the West Indies, but they do not seem to be known as pests of cultivated peas and beans.

The three species mentioned above as being of importance in connexion with the seed of cultivated leguminous crops are all alike in the essential points of their life-history, habits of feeding, and method of control. They all begin their attack in the young developing pod in the field. The insects in the seed at the time of harvesting continue breeding in storage, and if left uncontrolled will, by successive attacks, completely destroy the seeds.

Bean and pea weevils, like many other insect pests, pass through several marked changes in form and habits before reaching maturity. The adult weevil that is seen crawling about among the seeds is the parent insect. Many of these fly from the storage room or house to the fields where beans and peas are growing. As the bean and pea pods develop, the mother weevil lays whitish eggs, either on the outside or within the pods. These eggs are so small that they are often not noticed for they appear as mere white specks upon the pods. From these eggs there hatch white grubs that burrow their way through the pods into the soft developing beans and peas. Because these grubs are so very tiny, the holes through which they enter the

52------------------------------------------------

DECEMBER, 1919.]413

seeds are too small to be seen, unless one searches for them with a microscope. Usually beans become infested first when they are nearly full grown. As seeds expand and harden in the final ripening process, the holes in the skin through which the grubs entered become less and less easy to find. The wound in the skin either becomes entirely healed over or remains similar in appearance to a pin prick.

"Since beans and peas mature much faster than the weevil grubs within them, it happens that the weevil grubs are comparatively small or little developed in many instances when the crop is harvested and placed in storage. Thus many seeds that appear outwardly in excellent condition, in reality have weevil grubs hidden away in their interior." (FARMERS' BULLETIN, No. 983).

In this way the beans or peas become infested, and when harvested they contain living insects which will shortly emerge as adult beetles; these will deposit eggs, and another generation will attack the beans or peas. The injury caused by the first generation of the cowpea weevil and four-spotted bean weevil is not very great, but when the attacks are allowed to continue unrestricted during the whole time these seeds are in storage they may be entirely destroyed. Although cowpeas are the preferred food, most kinds of peas and beans are attacked. The FARMERS' BULLETIN, No. 983, mentioned above, gives the following list of food plants for the cowpea weevil:—cowpea, common pea, pigeon pea, lentil, check pea, mung bean, and common white bean. In this list the mung bean is the woolly pyrol of the West Indies.

Nearly every one who has kept seed of leguminous plants for any length of time in the West Indies knows the effect of the work of these insects. From a monetary point of view the loss is greater in these districts, particularly in the temperate climate, where large areas are grown in leguminous crops for sale and shipment to the markets of cities and towns. The Bulletin (983), already mentioned, states that One Province of Canada alone suffered from ravages of the pea weevil to the extent of over \$1,000,000 in a single year. It was estimated in 1902 that the acreage of field peas in Ontario would have been 1,000,000 instead of the 532,639 actually planted, had it not been for the fear of the pea weevil.

The situation in the West Indies is more comparable to that existing in the Southern State, where cowpeas are cultivated for their great value as soil covers and soil improvers, as well as for use for fodder, and where the acreage grown under this crop would be probably very much greater, but for the serious losses resulting from the attacks of these weevils.

## CASUARINA AND MANGROVE BOTH ATTACKED BY THE SAME BORER.

According to an article in the JOURNAL OF ECONOMIC RESEARCH, Washington, D.C., Vol. XVI, No. 6, the casuarina which has been extensively planted in Florida has been seriously attacked by the mangrove borer (*Chrysobothria tranquebarica*, Gmelin). Casuarina is a tree of much value for planting in the West Indies, and deserves to be more used than it has been in the past: the mangrove and its borer occur in some of these West Indian islands. On this account the article mentioned is summarized herewith for the benefit of readers of the *Agricultural News*.

The red mangrove (*Rhizophora mangle*, L.) is a native of the West Indies and perhaps also of Florida. The mangrove borer, also a native of the West Indies, is recorded as occurring in Cuba, Porto Rico, and Guadeloupe. It was known to science as long ago as 1787, but it appears that nothing was known previously with regard to its various stages, seasonal history, habits, etc. It has been known in Florida since 1886, but it is only within the past three or four years that it has been recognized as a pest of casuarina.

The adult beetle is a small insect of a metallic greenish-bronze colour. The female measures from 13.5 to 17 mm., and the male 12.5 to 14 mm. in length. Both sexes are fond of bright sunlight and are commonly found flying from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m. They feed on the tender, succulent bark of trees they infest.

The eggs are inserted under the thin outer layer of dry bark of the mangrove. When the eggs hatch the larvæ bore into the soft bark to the wood where they remain feeding on the cambium until nearly full-grown; at this stage the larva bores into the wood and excavates the pupal cell and provides a tunnel by means of which the adult insect will make its way to the outer world as a beetle.

One year is required for the development of the mangrove borer from egg to adult. So far as is known, this insect attacks only living red mangrove and the casuarinas. It is not a borer in dead wood.

In Florida both these trees have been killed by this borer, and the killing out of the tops or the main stem and forcing growth into the branches producing a stag-head effect appears to be a characteristic form of injury.

# CO-OPERATION.

## RURAL CREDIT IN THE PHILIPPINE ISLANDS.

MR. A. W. PRAUTCH, Chief, Rural Credit Division of the Department of Agriculture and Natural Resources of the Philippine Islands has very kindly sent us a copy of an article written by him on the above subject for the Mid Pacific Magazine of Japan. It contains an interesting account of the history and the progress of the Rural Credit in the Philippine Islands and valuable information for those who are interested in the co-operative movement.

The Rural Credit Law (No. 2808) of the Philippine Islands was enacted to remove the restriction in the Corporation Law (No. 1549) requiring \$200,000\* paid up capital before a bank can be started and to provide rules and regulations which would ensure the operation of credit banks or associations and safeguard the interest of the small investors, the general public and the Government.

The associations may incorporate with a paid up capital ranging from \$100 to \$10,000. Those who pay for shares become members and the members elect a Board or Committee of five who manage the affairs of the associations. They elect new members, collect share capital, issue loan and borrow money on behalf of the association. Only one association can be incorporated in a Municipality. This, it is said, is to avoid rivalry and factions. Provision has been made in the Act to the effect that the bonded municipal treasurer shall act as treasurer *ex officio* without any extra remuneration and that the Government Auditor shall audit the accounts. This provision has inspired the simple cultivators with confidence in the bank and MR. PRAUTCH states that no other single feature has contributed more to the success of the movement than this provision to protect the funds of the association.

All payments are made by the Treasurer on vouchers passed by the Board of Directors. No individual member can own more than \$500 worth of shares and each member has only one vote irrespective of the number of his shares.

The associations enjoy the following privileges until the paid up capital of each association attained \$10,000, viz., exception of taxes or revenue charges, of stamp fees and registration fees of mortgages and other documents and the Court fees on the collection of unpaid debts.

The organising staff of officers are paid and maintained by the Government. The office-bearers of an association work gratuitously and on no pay. But on the other hand the Government furnish no financial help directly or indirectly, and the supply of forms, books, etc., have to be paid for by the associations. This is generally met by the association by a levy of a small entrance fee from each member thus aiming at self-help.

# GENERAL.

## THE RÔLE OF SCIENCE.

It has been said that the rôle of science is prophecy. In the sense of revealing natural law and its manifestations, this is another way of saying that the rôle of science is to enable prediction regarding the operations of natural phenomena under definite conditions. For science enables the relation between cause and effect to be traced, determines the response to specific influences and conditions; and since the knowledge it establishes is exact and unvarying it becomes possible to prophesy with certainty what will take place when certain conditions meet. The statement therefore expresses broadly the final function and attribute of science.

But beyond this, science is at once the source of exact knowledge and the means by which it is advanced. It supplies a background in accumulated information, and it permits a glance into the unknown which enables further advances to be made. It thus broadens the vision of problems and their nature, stimulates speculation, and suggests courses of action which may be productive. These are essentials of prophecy in respect to natural manifestations, without which there could be no progress in science.

Progress is the keynote of science. Science is never complete; it is in continual process of being added to. An answer suggests further questions and may reveal a possible means of solution. Science is therefore constantly searching, building new theories, advancing its boundaries step by step, making prophecy more sure. Investigation which is not thus characterized falls short of its purpose.

Science not only contributes substantial facts but it discloses more clearly the real nature of complex problems, making their solution more feasible; and it propounds new ones which are practicable of productive study. To propound problems and to analyze and define their character are important functions of science. These are first steps in the direction of progress, for they provide a starting point and a clearly defined purpose.

In agriculture it has acquired long years of research to lay bare the real underlying questions and suggest how they might be approached. The starting point was naturally provided mainly by general science, but it required special insight to develop out of the traditions and practices of this ancient art the scientific aspects of the underlying problems; to show, for example, what factors were actually involved in the growing of crops and the feeding of plants. The conditions prevailing in successful practice had to be studied, data accumulated extensively under a variety of circumstances, and the results of certain definite combinations recorded. Gradually a background of fact was accumulated which although imperfectly understood gave an insight into the situation and served as a basis for specific investigation.

The ultimate solution of an agricultural question may prove to be a relatively easy task after the real nature of the question is understood. This was the case with the problems of cheese making and curing, for example,

57------------------------------------------------

418[DECEMBER, 1919.]

after the actual factors involved had been made clear and separated so that they could be studied. For many years there was great confusion in discovering and developing the laws of heredity, but MENDEL taught that success depended in fixing attention, not on the organism as a whole but upon one after another of its various attributes or characters. Thus disintegrated, the problem of inheritance became simplified and susceptible of solution by the ordinary methods of experiment.

The understanding of what is fundamentally involved in a complex question of practice is still a necessary first step. It is the rôle of science in agriculture to disclose this in order that the solution may be unfolded and prophecy made possible.

\* \* \*

There is a distinction between what it might be desirable to do in the interests of science and practice and what it is feasible to accomplish at the present state of knowledge of problems and technique. Questions are frequently brought to the attention of the stations which it is not practicable to solve at present, either because there is not sufficient background to enable the problem to be understood or the methods and technique are deficient. Investigation cannot successfully progress much faster than the methods and means of study advance, and often not more rapidly than investigation in the domain of general science clears the field and enables hypothesis and theory to be advanced.

\* \* \*

It will be recognized that there are many subjects, like studies in breeding with plants and animals, the physiology of nutrition, the effects of soil treatment, the working out of intricate questions of disease, etc., which necessarily require long and persistent study and cannot be hurried. They may or may not be ultimately rewarded with success. There are others which require the systematic accumulation of data under a variety of conditions and over long periods. But in neither case does this warrant limitation to routine effort or neglect of technical features which will be ultimately essential.

\* \* \*

The process of investigation is progressive in insight, in approach and in method. It develops with the opening up of the subject by the results secured and those of other contemporary investigation. If the work is not progressive in this sense it is not a searching inquiry and critical study, but a methodical and more or less mechanical accumulation of data. If it does not advance step by step in the effort not only to contribute to the evidence but to perfect the attack, it misses the essential feature of research and proceeds on a mistaken estimate of the necessarily inherent value of data covering long periods. Experimental data are of value to and a part of investigation as they are applied to it. Already we have a broad background for investigation in most common subjects in agriculture. It is not necessary to spend time exclusively or mainly in taking data which may serve at some future time as the basis for research. The problem and the specific question are already at hand if we can discern them.

To cite a familiar example, it has long been known that land can be cropped continuously for a long time, often with surprising returns, and that fertility or productive capacity may be maintained and even improved by

58------------------------------------------------

DECEMBER, 1919.]419

combinations of cropping systems, fertilizers, and soil treatment. Whether or not this is fundamentally true is no longer a question for lengthy experiment; the general truth is now matter for demonstration. The economic phase may need some development and local adaptation, but the underlying question for research lies beyond this point. It concerns the reasons, the changes in conditions and relationships which are brought about, the reciprocal relations of various classes of factors which are in operation, the means of maintaining the proper balance in these factors, and matters of this sort. These are not solved by growing and harvesting crops in rotation through a long period, even if the greatest possible care is observed, or by the chemical analysis of the crops and of the soils at intervals of several years, or even by sampling the plats occasionally for the usual bacteriological examination. Rotation and fertilizer experiments of themselves seem at present to be contributing relatively little that is new regarding the theory of plant feeding, soil fertility, and soil management. It is where such series of experiments are being utilized to study certain definite questions of limited range that they are productive of new light.

It is the purpose and use which give life to the taking of data and make their acquisition profitable. Correlation studies, for example, proceed through long periods and result in volumes of data, but unless they are interpreted in relation to some problem they do not directly advance knowledge, and a point is soon reached where their accumulation lacks the elements of research.

Intensive and concentrated effort is the price of success in agricultural research. To be productive it requires close application with critical attention to the accuracy and the meaning of the results. It is an intensive, diligent application of the powers of inquiry. Intermittent, fragmentary effort arrives late, if at all. It is uneconomical of the station's funds, and its encouragement breeds a habit which is detrimental to highest efficiency.

It is important to avoid losing sight of the fact that productive investigation calls for a type of training which instils the idea of the essentials of research and its methods, as well as provides a store of knowledge as a foundation for it. The former is not less important than the latter to give originality, initiative, judgment, and authority. Unless these can be insured agricultural investigation can not measure up to expectations, and science will not fulfil its rôle of prophecy.—EXPT. STATION RECORD, Vol. 40, No. 8.

In his address to Section M., PROF. SOMERVILLE pointed out that during the war the area of land in the United Kingdom under grass was reduced by more than three million acres, with a corresponding increase of the area under tillage crops. Indirect manuring through feeding stock with cake has also given unsatisfactory results.

## SPONTANEOUS COMBUSTION.

So called 'spontaneous combustion' in reference to such things in ships' cargoes, as coal, cotton, hay, tobacco, and cacao is a subject of considerable importance in the tropics. The *JOURNAL OF THE ROYAL SOCIETY OF ARTS* for June 20 contains a paper on the subject, read by MR. WATSON SMITH, F. I. C., before the Society. This paper begins by explaining that spontaneous combustion arises by reason of such chemical and physical changes in bodies,

mostly consisting or containing carbon, and under the influence of atmospheric oxygen, that sufficient heat is generated to give rise ultimately to combustion. In a general way the most frequent places of spontaneous combustion occur in coals and not many years ago the German Government, after an elaborate inquiry, declared that more ships were lost through such outbreaks than through any other cause. It is noted that coal, containing throughout its mass pyrites, is most inclined to fire when in a damp state. This should cause certain precautions, such as observations of temperature at certain periods to be regularly taken.

Another substance in which fires may arise through the gradual generation of heat is quick lime. It is well known that if water be suddenly added to quick lime there is a rapid generation of heat, and this may be quite enough to set fire to dry material or inflammable substances in close proximity. Owing to its physical properties charcoal is liable to fire. It has been found by experiments that finely ground charcoal placed in a barrel attained a temperature of 75°C. after fifteen hours; after twenty hours ignition took place. This was due to the absorption of oxygen and a certain quantity of water. This circumstance might be borne in mind in connexion with the transmission of seed like cacao or rubber in damp charcoal from one part of the tropics to another. If transmitted in large quantities it should be quite possible for enough heat to be produced to kill the seeds, if not to set fire to the parcel:

The statement that wet cotton is liable to break out in combustion is improbable, but a spark getting into a bale will smoulder for a long time, and the fire eventually occurring will then be probably stated as having been due to spontaneous combustion. Greasy cotton is undoubtedly dangerous, most fatty oils absorbing oxygen rapidly with the generation of much heat. A case is recorded in which a firm received a consignment of Indian cotton damaged with river water, and it was offered for sale by auction. On the auction day, the cooper observed that a number of bales had burst several of their encircling iron bands, and the heating of the cotton was perceptible. Investigation showed that these bales contained an extraordinary proportion of cotton seeds. The germination of the seeds in the bales could not proceed, but the heat produced was enough to cause expansion of the air, and to raise the temperature very nearly high enough to set fire to the cotton.

In the case of tobacco, fermentation is frequently the cause of combustion. Also in the case of ensilage there is some danger of overheating. Care should be taken in West Indian islands where silos have been erected, to institute a proper routine for checking the temperature from time to time. In regard to such substances as dried fish, guano, and bone dust, it is recorded that on one occasion a few hundred tons of such material were landed from a ship at Glasgow, and at once placed in bulk in a store on a stone floor. In a short time the material began to heat internally, and so it was opened out and spread more thinly over the floor by a gang of men, this of course bringing the heated stuff more in contact with the oxygen of the air. While this spreading out was taking place a large quantity of the material took fire, and before extinction could be effected a portion of the enclosure was destroyed.

Amongst the acids the most dangerous are sulphuric acid and nitric acid. As ships' freights, the carboys in straw should be stored with great care, and enclosed either under deck or on deck with sand so that should the safety of the ship demand it, they can be readily thrown over the side.—*AGRIC. ## PRESERVING FRUIT.

Fruit may be preserved without sugar by the following methods :—

(1) In special glass jars with screw tops. These should be filled with fruit and water, almost to the top. The lid and screw should then be loosely put on, and the jars placed in cold water up to the neck. The water should be slowly heated to 180 deg. Fahr., and the bottles then removed, placed on a wooden table or board, and the lids screwed up firmly. It is an additional safeguard to the sterilisation, and an aid to the subsequent removal of the lid, if a little grease, such as lard, be run inside the screw.

(2) In ordinary glass jars, which also should be filled almost completely with fruit and water. Then a piece of soft wet bladder should be tied on the top, and the bottles submerged in water and heated to 180° F. Fruit thus preserved may be stewed, or made into puddings, with a small quantity of the cheap sugar which is unsuitable for the making of jam.—*WEALTH OF INDIA*, Vol. VIII, No. 9.

## SORGHUM AS A COLOURING MATERIAL.

According to M. PIEDALU, the “glumes” or husks of sweet sorghum and those of sorghum with black seed, hitherto of no use at all, yield a gum with very fine shades of color ranging from pink to bright red, salmon, scarlet, pearl gray, dark gray, dark brown and khaki, all colors which, being sun and soap proof, are highly suitable for dyeing wool, silk, leather and vegetable fibres. The discovery of this new dyeing material, it is stated, is one of great importance, and steps are to be taken to work it on a large scale.—*WEALTH OF INDIA*, Vol. VIII., No. 9.
