## Editorial.

**T**HE second article in the series dealing with present-day views on soils and manures is included in the present number. This deals with the manurial aspect of the question and in the concluding section discusses the paucity of definite data in Ceylon in regard to the manurial requirements of its crops.

There are certain reasons for such a lack of information. Manurial experiments have been found to have been the most difficult to design and many of the older methods have had to give way before modern scientific methods of the interpretation of results. In the past fifteen years, the importance of the statistical method of dealing with the results from agricultural and biological experiments has been recognised, and although this method was employed with experimental data with tropical crops in British Guiana in 1912, it is only recently that its significance has been recognised within the Tropics. Experimental results from field trials with permanent crops are extremely difficult to interpret scientifically and the methods to be adopted in laying out such experiments have not been satisfactorily defined.

130

Permanent crops have individualities. Tea bushes have varying capacities for producing flush, coconuts have very widely varying performances in respect of yield and rubber yields vary from tree to tree.

In temperate climates and with annuals it has been recognised that the average performance of the land utilized for experimental work should be first ascertained. In permanent crops in the Tropics, the importance of the initial yield records of the different plots has become recognised. In fact, in rubber if large areas are not available for experiment, it appears to be essential that the individual yields of trees require to be secured before experiments are laid down.

It is therefore little to be wondered at that many manurial experiments give inconclusive results and that progress is only gradual. It is also apparent that co-operative experiments with estates are full of difficulties and pitfalls.

It is however clear that the manurial experiments which have been carried out in Ceylon have given certain indications of value and have provided data on which to base further experimental work. Co-operative experiments have their value and under certain safeguards in respect to scientific inspection and control should be capable of use. They are employed in other countries and there is no reason to suppose that a limited number could not be so organized in Ceylon as to be of value.

The agricultural community is beginning to demand further data in regard to manures and the research work of the Agricultural Chemist should therefore be of importance. On the Experiment Station, Peradeniya, the tea manurial experiments have recently been re-designed and the Rubber Research Scheme has in operation a manurial experiment for rubber. The acquisition of a coconut estate in the North-Western Province should make it possible to profit from the results of the experiments at Chilaw and lay out a much completer series of experiments for that crop. If the paddy area at Peradeniya can be extended, as recommended by the Board of Agriculture, carefully designed experimental trials with manures for paddy are contemplated.

## Manures.

**F. A. STOCKDALE, C.B.E., M.A., F.L.S.,**

*Director of Agriculture, Ceylon.*

**I**N the previous article which appeared in the February number of the *Tropical Agriculturist* a brief review of some of the present-day conceptions of the soil were given, and it proposed to continue this with a short statement of how plants secure their food constituents from the soil and on the use of manures.

The importance of the root system of a plant cannot be over-estimated and it has been recently strongly urged by Howard in India that there has been a great neglect of the careful study and investigation of the root-systems of agricultural crops. For permanent crops, such as tea, he suggests that the improvement noticeable after the planting of shade trees may be explained by the rooting systems of such trees, for they may act beneficially as sub-soilers—breaking into the sub-soil by their deep rooting systems and thereby effecting better drainage and ventilation. He even suggests that such a study might lead to a selection of shade trees according to their root systems for planting up in particular soil types. There is undoubtedly sufficient grounds for urging agriculturists to think along this line and to consider whether scientific investigations cannot be undertaken. The soil serves to provide water and certain food materials for the plant and these are absorbed by its extensive root-system. Cultivation is designed to bring the soil into such a condition that maximum root development can take place, but general experience now shows that roots, even of cereals, penetrate under certain soil conditions to considerable depths, and the current beliefs that it was only the top six to nine inches of soil which was of importance and that the sub-soil was only of indirect interest in plant nutrition is losing ground. In bringing the sub-soil into action for the permanent crops of tropical agriculture deep-rooted shade trees are of importance and in the rotations on areas devoted to food crops such deep-rooted crops as cotton and dhall are bound to play an important part.

## Plant Food from the Soil.

The manner in which plants obtain their food, especially from the soil, has been for many generations of great interest to agriculturists and various views have been held. It has been known for a long time that plants absorb water and certain mineral salts in solution through the small root hairs of the small feeding roots. It was held that these roots were able to dissolve some of the mineral constituents by means of acid excretions and classic experiments designed to show this are demonstrated to all classes in colleges and schools taking agricultural chemistry or agricultural botany. Russel states that this idea formed the basis of the soil extraction methods long used in soil analysis and indicates that another view has gradually grown up and the plant is no longer regarded as an active preparer of its own food, but is a passive absorber of whatever food happens to be present in the soil solution. This idea first started in the American school of soil chemists and after modifications as the result of continuous research Russel \* sums up the position as follows:—

“ The soil water contains something of everything in the soil, and it is kept charged because soil micro-organisms keep on making nitrate, sulphate and bicarbonate radicles which as fast as they are formed bring the bases into solution. The soil water contains the whole of the soil's stock of nitrate, none of which is ever present in the solid state; it is always fully charged with as much phosphate as it can hold (though this is not a great deal), and it contains always potash, magnesia, lime and other bases required by the plant. In short it contains everything that the plant needs from the soil, and on our present ideas it is the source from which the plant obtains its food.

“ The action of micro-organisms continuously alters the amount of nitrate, sulphate and carbonate, which in turn affect the amount of dissolved base. Plants and micro-organisms are continuously taking something out: rain-water dilutes it; evaporation concentrates it. In the ordinary way the soil solution is very dilute, containing about 0·05 to 0·2 per cent. of dissolved matter. Chemists find it more convenient to express this in terms of the pressure exercised by the dissolved substances: this amounts to about 0·2 to 1 atmospheres in ordinary agricultural soils. The sap in the plant root is a much stronger solution; it may, indeed, be twenty times as strong. It is still a mystery how the plant foods pass from the soil solution to the root. In spite of its dilution the soil solution appears to suffice for the feeding of the plant.

“ It was at first thought that the strength of the soil solution was immaterial, plants doing as well in a weak solution as in a stronger one. The Rothamsted workers showed that this is probably not correct, and more recently Hoagland and Sharp have come to the same conclusion. Within the

“ During its growth the plant greatly alters the composition of the solution, removing most if not all of the nitrate and much of the potassium, calcium and magnesium, though it has little effect on the phosphate, this being renewed as fast as it is absorbed by the plant. “ This seems to be what the plant prefers; indeed, W. Gerike has made some remarkable experiments indicating that some of the plant foods—phosphate and potash—actually do harm if they are given to the plant late in its life.

“ The two factors that seem to be important in determining the productiveness of the soil are: (1) the rate at which the solid food stuffs in the soil can pass into the solution to make up for what the plant has taken; (2) the rate at which the solution can be supplied by the soil to the plant. The first of these depends on the mineral composition of the soil, as has been well recognised by agricultural chemists for years past. The second, the food supplying power of the soil, is a newer idea, and we have as yet little information about it. Hoagland suggests that the main factor may be the growth of the plant roots outwards. It is the new rootlets that take up the plant food and as they spread into fresh portions of the soil they absorb what they want from the soil solution. Indeed, the whole question of the feeding of plants may be simply a case of root spread, in which case the “ supplying power ” of the soil is the same as the power to encourage root development. In all probability, however, the case is more complex, involving both root development and reinstatement of the foods in the solution.”

This conception must considerably modify our agricultural practices. It closely demonstrates the necessity for and the value of cultivation in order to encourage the spread of the feeding roots and it also demonstrates the fallacy of the practice which has been common in Ceylon estates of applying large quantities of manures at long intervals rather than smaller quantities more frequently. Experience has gradually convinced planters in Ceylon that manuring in smaller quantities every year is preferable to manuring in larger quantities every two or every three years. The question is an economic one, dependent upon labour supply and upon the cost of labour and should be viewed in that light. Within limits the practice of little and often has much to recommend it, especially where nitrogenous manures are being applied. The maximum nitrification of organic manures has been shown by Joachim in Ceylon to take place between the 4th and 8th weeks and this is confirmatory of data available from other tropical countries.

## Organic *versus* Mineral Nitrogen.

This naturally brings us to the question of organic *versus* mineral nitrogen. This is undoubtedly of prime importance in Ceylon and scientific data on which to decide the matter definitely is still not available. It reminds one of the controversy which has raged in countries with temperate climates over the question of artificial fertilizers *versus* farmyard manure. It has been found that farmyard manure greatly increases the power of a soil to hold moisture and assists root development—effects which are not shown by artificial manures. It is recognised that farmyard manure has special values and that green manures have similar qualities in improving soil moisture content and tilth. All the evidence that one has been able to gather seems to indicate that organic nitrogen is of special value in Ceylon's agriculture. This is probably due to the gradual depletion of the humus in Ceylon's cultivated soils, and to the consequent inhibitory effects on maximum bacterial action. There is evidence that some of Ceylon's tea cultivations are not in that vigorous health that is expected of them. This change has to my knowledge been taking place gradually for several years and I attribute it largely to want of adequate humus in the soil and to insufficient drainage. It occurs frequently in estates that are devoid of shade—be it dadap, albizzia or grevillea—and one feels that such lands are not having their humus content maintained nor are the deeper layers of the soil being opened up and drained as they might be by means of deep-rooting shade trees.

Organic manures in the form of cakes or poonacs assist the development of the micro-organisms in the soil and there is therefore much to recommend them for use in our crops, but the unit value of nitrogen is high. They are looked upon as expensive by several estate agents but it would appear to be necessary to continue their use until such time as the humus contents of our soils may be improved by the employment of green cover crops. Then there should be no objection to the use of mineral nitrogen manures. Farmyard manure and green manures have been proved to have steady effects against variations of rainfall. Variations in crops which have been incompletely manured with artificials alone are most marked and a bad season from the climatic point of view may result in no increased yields and a dead loss of money.

## Green Manures.

How yields of crops can be maintained in Ceylon are shown by the Tea plots at the Experiment Station, Peradeniya. The plot which received a very heavy dressing of cattle manure showed no diminution of yields for a period of nearly ten years, although it received no artificial manures except the old recognised pruning mixture at pruning time every alternate year. Similarly the plots with dadaps and albizzia as shade trees lopped at regular intervals have maintained high yields and a superiority over other plots not similarly shaded but manured with artificials. The general appearance of the tea in these plots is better than that in others and the general tilth and condition of the soil is superior. The effect of a leguminous cover crop on growing tea is now being studied and careful preliminary data should be available in another year or so. If such crops can be grown successfully without detriment to yield by reason of competition with the tea for moisture, particularly in dry weather, it is possible to contemplate that the humus and nitrogen content of Ceylon's soils can be maintained by the proper agricultural treatment of such cover crops. The same practice can be adopted with vigna in rubber and with boga medeloa and possibly other crops in coconuts. It is to be conceived that such a practice should not only materially improve soils and maintain crops but should effect considerable economics in the bills for manures.

## Manures and Manure Receipts.

Ceylon uses a greater quantity of artificial manures per cultivated acre than any other tropical country, with the exception of certain areas devoted to sugar-cane cultivation in several countries. The demand for manure increases year by year and there seems to be little doubt that this demand is likely to increase until soil erosion is further stopped, humus content of soils built up and the growth of green cover crops more general.

The receipts for manure mixtures in use on estates are worthy of close study and investigation. They seem to have been built upon on the belief that a little of everything is necessary to provide for a "hit and miss" policy. If one ingredient does not suit another will! Fortunately some change is taking place towards the simplification of these receipts, but until further data is available the agricultural industries are wanting in sound advice on a most important aspect. Experiments at Peradeniya with tea have emphasized the value of the green-

manure shade trees, and others in connection with shot-hole borer investigations have indicated the value of readily available nitrogen in the repair of damage done by that borer and preliminary trials at Peradeniya are indicating the importance of similar applications towards stimulating callus growth over wounds. Experiments with rubber have so far given no indications of increased yields but nitrogen will provide a heavier leaf foliage and in those areas liable to secondary leaf-fall may result in a greater amount of leaf being left on the trees after an attack than is the case in similar areas with unmanured trees. A carefully planned series of manurial experiments is being carried out by the Rubber Research Scheme and if any definite indications are obtained from these, co-operative experiments with estates are contemplated. The trials with manuring coconuts at Chilaw have indicated that manuring gives increased crops. Lime has resulted in increased crops of nuts, and fish, steamed bone-meal, sulphate of ammonia and potash have given increases.

For paddy it has been found that increased crops are secured by applications of bone-meal and that the applications of steamed bone-meal are preferable to application of ordinary crushed bones. It is also proposed to make some trials with sulphate of ammonia—the manure most commonly used for paddy in Japan. If the paddy area at the Experiment Station, Peradeniya, is extended it is proposed to lay out a complete series of plots for testing the manurial requirements of the paddy crop.

There is no doubt that this problem of manures is one of the greatest importance to Ceylon's agricultural industries. It is one on which the Tea Research Institute should especially concentrate in the interests of the tea industry and it is hoped that as soon as a soil chemist has been secured co-operative experiments with estates will be begun under his direct supervision. The failure of co-operative manurial experiments has been referred to on several occasions, but it is significant that one fact which was obtained from the old co-operative manurial experiments on tea estates in Ceylon was that heavy dressings of lime were detrimental—a fact which has subsequently been established by experience in Ceylon and substantiated by the experiments at the Tocklai experiment station in Assam.

Data is also required regarding plant stimulants. Recent work has shown that certain substances help the plant to take up its essential foodstuffs better than it otherwise would and others stimulate certain vital processes. The effects of silicates, boric acid, aluminium, iron, etc., on plant growth in Ceylon deserve investigation.

## Notes on Visits to Some Rubber Factories and Research Laboratories in England.

**T. E. H. O'BRIEN, B.Sc., A.I.C.,**

*Chemist, Rubber Research Scheme, Ceylon.*

**I**T is thought to be of interest to record some impressions of visits to various Rubber factories and Research Institutes which were made by the writer whilst on furlough.

It is most convenient to divide the subject into two sections, visits to factories and visits to Scientific Institutions.

Visits were made to two rubber factories namely, the North British Rubber Company, and the Dunlop Rubber Company, each of which in its own sphere is in the forefront of the Rubber Industry in England.

The North British Rubber Company whose works are situated in Edinburgh specialises in Rubber footwear (of which it is the largest Manufacturer in Great Britain), and in various mechanical goods. The number of different articles manufactured in the works amounts to hundreds.

The manufacture of such diverse articles as shoes, hot water bottles, tyres and vulcanite combs was followed with the greatest interest. To one whose work is connected with the simple processes involved in preparation of raw rubber it was impressive to note the number of processes and the large amount of manipulation required in the production of such an apparently simple article as a Plimsol shoe. The North British Rubber Company have not found that they suffered from competition of the crepe soled shoe. They make a certain number of these shoes, but it was gathered that the demand for them is small compared with their output of vulcanised shoes. They also make a shoe with a vulcanised crepe sole, which has always appeared to the writer to be a more practical proposition than the raw crepe sole.

9------------------------------------------------

138

It was interesting to see the raw rubber as it arrived at the factory. Even at summer temperature the sheet and crepe was comparatively hard, and in winter it is necessary to keep the chests in a warm room to thaw out the rubber before use.

A number of the chests appeared to be in damaged condition and this was not confined to any particular type of chest. It was stated that a damaged ply-wood chest causes more damage from splinters in the rubber than other types. Splinters of wood and other impurities which work their way into the rubber from the chests are a great nuisance to the manufacturer, and for certain purposes it is found necessary to wash, recrepe, and dry the rubber. It was stated that this trouble could be prevented to a large extent if producers adopted the practice of lining chests with sheets of rubber before packing. Contamination would then be confined to the lining sheets which can be stripped off. This practice involves no extra expense to the producer and it is hoped that it will be more generally adopted.

The manufacturer is not particularly interested in the minor defects of raw rubber which cause so much anxiety to the planter, such as slight mould, rust, bubbles, etc. Nor does he care whether sheets are absolutely uniform in size and have nicely trimmed edges and so on. These are merely the criteria of the broker who has no other basis on which to judge the quality of the rubber. Up to a point, insistence on production of rubber of good appearance is probably of value, on the assumption that care with regard to appearance connotes equal care in regard to more important details of manufacture, such as uniformity of method, careful straining of the latex, keeping the rubber free of contamination with copper and from sunlight, etc.

Variability in rate of vulcanisation of different batches of rubber has lost much of its significance since the introduction of organic accelerators, which not only reduce the time of vulcanisation, but tend to equalise the time required by various batches. Furthermore the presence of suitable accelerators produces what is known as a flat cure which means that it is now not so important for the rubber to be vulcanised for exactly the correct length of time.

What is much more troublesome to the manufacturer is variability in plasticity of plantation rubber, *i.e.*, variation in the amount of milling required to bring different batches of rubber to a suitable consistency for manufacturing processes. Both in

A number of interesting testing machines of different sorts were seen. Cotton testing is an important item in the rubber factory, and machines for testing tensile strength, and other properties are installed.

The usual method of tyre testing consists of a machine in which the tyre is mounted on a motor wheel which is pressed against the rim of a pulley wheel driven by an electric motor. The load on the tyre is adjusted to that which it would be expected to stand under ordinary conditions and it is rotated at a pre-determined speed, normally about 30 miles per hour. In some tests raised cleats of metal are bolted on the rim of the pulley wheel so that the tyre receives several jolts in each revolution. This test does not measure the abrasive resistance of the tread but is essentially a test of the general strength of the tyre. Conditions are varied according to whether it is desired to measure the heat generated in the tyre, the distance run before the tyre bursts or before the tread begins to loosen, the effects of overloading, etc., etc.

What the writer looks on as the last word in refinement was an X-ray apparatus for examining golf balls at the North British Rubber Works. The X-rays show up the hard core and if this is not quite round or centrally placed the ball is rejected as 1st quality. It is comforting to think that an inaccurate putt may be due to a badly balanced ball.

The Dunlop Rubber Company is the largest of its kind in England and the new factories cover a very large area in one of the outskirts of Birmingham. Tyres are the chief product of the factory and most of the writer's visit was devoted to following the stages of manufacture of these indispensable articles. Perhaps the most impressive thing seen was the building up and vulcanising of giant pneumatic tyres which is surely a triumph of tyre manufacture. Side by side with this was the manufacture of cycle, motor cycle, and car inner tubes, which pass through an incredible number of operations before they are finally completed. The first stage consists of forcing or "spewing" the masticated and compounded rubber from a machine through a die in the form of a tube of the required size. The causes of variability of plasticity and methods for its correction will have to be investigated scientifically, by the producer of rubber, the planter can do considerably more towards the production of uniform rubber than is done at present. I refer to uniformity in method of preparation, and bulking together of large quantities of latex before coagulation.

It is likely that rubber from different fields and trees of different ages will differ in plasticity and it follows that if the latex from a 1,000 acre estate is coagulated in 15 Shanghai jars there is the likelihood that each batch will have a different plasticity. The writer would like to see Shanghai jars done away with and replaced by tanks of such size that the day's crop can be received and coagulated in one or two tanks. A small tank would be provided for latex which arrives at the factory late. This would ensure that at least each day's crop was uniform in properties, and would bring Ceylon into line with the other rubber producing countries.

In addition, bulking of latex in large tanks makes it easier to control dilution of the latex to standard rubber content, and the use of correct quantities of acid and bisulphite. Coagulation at

It is claimed by the inventors that rubber prepared in this way is superior to other forms of rubber in tensile strength, ageing properties, plasticity and uniformity of plasticity. The manufacturer also wants rubber that is free from dirt and grit and in this respect special care is required in Ceylon. Personally the writer would like to see the *sheratty* abolished and replaced by cups that are taken in to the factory each day and washed; but as this is most improbable, great care should be taken that *sheralties* are not left lying on the ground. Apart from any other consideration it would probably pay the planter to see that *shcratties* are hung up on the trees on sticks, in that his cup scrap rubber would be of better colour and quality if it had not been exposed to the sun for 2 days.

Finally, to refer again to packing of rubber: it costs the producer nothing to line his chests with sheets or crepe before packing, and it is stated by the Chief Chemist of the Dunlop Company that this is a great protection in preventing splinters of wood from working in between the sheets in the chest.

# Natural Immunity of Rubber Trees to Bark Rot.

**R. H. STOUGHTON-HARRIS, B.Sc., A.R.C.S.,**

*Mycologist, Rubber Research Scheme, Ceylon.*

**I**N the course of some experiments carried out on the control of Bark Rot by disinfectants applied to the tapping-cut, observations of the same trees on two successive years gave reason to believe that on the whole the same trees which were infected by Bark Rot one year tended to be re-infected the next year, while those not attacked remained free the following season. It was apparent that this was not strictly the case, but there appeared to be more trees attacked both years than would be expected on a chance or random distribution of infection.

A careful analysis of the actual numbers of cases bears out this conclusion and it is intended in this article to give a summary of the analytical results obtained.

A detailed description of the experiments is not necessary, and has been given elsewhere, but an outline of the design is essential for an undertaking of the tables which follow.

A plot of 1,440 trees was divided up into four blocks of eighteen rows, each row containing twenty trees. To the trees in each of the rows in the first block was applied a different disinfectant, and these treatments were repeated in the remaining three blocks, so that in all eighty trees were treated with each of eighteen disinfectants (more precisely seventeen, as one row in each block was left untreated to serve as a control). The experiment was carried out during the S. W. Monsoon period of 1925 and repeated in the same season of 1926. All trees were tapped on a  $\frac{1}{2}$ -cut every third day during 1925 and on alternate days in 1926. The practice on the estate is to change-over the

cuts to the opposite sides of the trees twice a year, so that the same panel was in tapping in both experiments, except in those cases where a new cut had to be opened in 1926.

Examinations of every tree for Bark Rot were made twice in the 1925 experiment (July and August) and once in 1926 (August). In the examinations all trees which showed more than one or two black lines along the tapping-cut were considered as diseased.

In the analysis of the figures which follows all trees which were out of tapping in either year for any cause other than Bark Rot are left out of the count, so that in each block there is a total of less than 80.

Now, assuming that all trees were equally susceptible to Bark Rot it would be expected that on the whole the distribution of infection in 1926 would be proportional to the infection in 1925. For example, suppose in any one block of four rows treated alike, there was 75 per cent. of infection; that is to say 60 trees infected and 20 uninfected out of the total of 80 (assuming all to be in tapping). Then suppose that in 1925 there were 40 trees attacked by Bark Rot (50 per cent. infection). Then if the chances of infection are equal it would be expected that of these 40, 75 per cent. would be trees that had been attacked in 1925 and 25 per cent. of "new cases." That is 30 of the 1926 cases should be on trees attacked in 1925 also, and 10 should be on trees free from Bark Rot in 1925. This exact distribution would never, of course, be found, but it is possible by statistical methods to show what deviation from the expected results may be regarded as significant.

# Garlic.

*Allium Sativum.*

**W. MOLEGODE,**

*Agricultural Instructor.*

**G**ARLIC is a crop that was until very recently cultivated in the Island in a very limited scale and its cultivation was confined to parts of Uva and Nuwara Eliya. It is pleasing to note that the growing of garlic, even for home consumption, is extending to other parts, chiefly Kandy and parts of Kurunegala, and trials made in Harispattu, at Nugawela and Idamegama school garden and by villagers in these localities have shewn that, like onions, garlic can be successfully grown in these parts of the country.

Garlic is a much better paying crop than onions and, like onions, it is an everyday requirement in our homes. Its chief use is for flavouring of curries but plays not an unimportant part in native medicines. It is used in cases of nervous affections, flatulence, hysteria, etc. It is a stimulant diuretic. The juice mixed with sugar is often given to children and in old age for coughs and asthma. Besides these it has various other uses.

**Propagation.**—Garlic is generally and more preferably grown from “sets” or “cloves.” It can be, but is seldom, propagated by seed. Unlike the onion, to which it is allied in so many ways, the garlic bulb is composed of a number of “cloves” or “sets”—sometimes 20 to 25 of these—which are bound together by thin and tough membranes. These sets or cloves are arranged round a centre stalk and are easily separated. Each of these sets or cloves is capable of growing into a plant producing bulb. For this reason sets and not the entire bulb should be planted. The distance of planting varies from 6 to 9 inches. It has been reckoned that between 300 to 500 lb. of bulbs are required to plant an acre. In planting, the small sets or cloves that are not properly developed should be rejected and only well developed ones should be used.

**Soil and Treatment.**—Garlic succeeds best in a well drained rich soil that is moderately loose in texture. As the plant grows and the bulb begins to form, it requires a loose soil to properly develop the bulb. The land should be kept well weeded. At the time of weeding the soil round the plants should be loosened so as to allow the growing bulb freedom to expand and develop. If the soil is hard the growth of the bulb will be retarded. It has been found from experience that where the soil was hard it resulted in the production of a tiny and badly developed bulb.

The ideal soil for garlic has been described as a rich moderately loose textured one sufficiently retentive of moisture and affording a fairly free passage for irrigation water to percolate to deeper layers so that it may be drained off.

Garlic is preferably cultivated on flat beds rather than on ridges. The cultivation of the soil should not be deep since the roots of the garlic plant do not extend deep into the soil and because a firm basis is needed for the roots in order that nourishment might be easily available.

Well rotted farmyard manure applied prior to planting gave very good results both at Nugawela and Idamegama.

The manure should be broadcasted and thoroughly incorporated with the soil.

Sets should be planted about an inch or two deep and the soil pressed down a little.

**Harvesting.**—Garlic takes about four months to mature. When the leaves begin to turn yellow the crop is ready to be harvested. In planting garlic due regard should be given to weather conditions and should be planted so that the crop may mature during dry weather. Crops should be lifted carefully, freed of earth and thoroughly dried in the sun. Garlic can be kept for a considerable time if hung over the fire place.

Yields of 3,000 to 5,000 pounds per acre can be obtained from garlic if properly cultivated and treated.

The current price of garlic is between 30 to 35 cents per pound.

## Conditions of Tenancy in Paddy Cultivation in Ratnapura District.

**A. MADANAYAKE,**

*Senior Agricultural Instructor, Ratnapura.*

**W**HEN a cultivator obtains a field from a land-owner "on anda" system the usual practice is that the seed-paddy, and manure (if applied) are shared equally. The cultivator does the whole work; and, at harvest, after allocating the Vel-Vidane's share (1 laha or kuruni for each pela's sowing extent) and deducting seed-paddy with 50 per cent. interest, which goes to the person who lends paddy at the sowing, the crop is divided equally between the land-owner and the cultivator. In the event of the cultivator's inability to procure the necessary buffalos, the land-owner supplies them and gets  $\frac{1}{3}$ rd or  $\frac{1}{4}$ th the crop according to mutual agreement.

In some parts of Atakalan Korale a monetary tax known as "madaran," the amount of which varies according to the fertility of the fields, is levied by land-owners.

In Kolonna Korale, conditions vary in different villages and much facilities are afforded to the cultivators by the land-owners. This is due to the fact that there are more fields than there are cultivators to work on them.

**Fencing.**—This is entirely done by cultivators and is becoming onerous in many parts of the district owing to scarcity of fencing material. The consequence of this is that strong fences, which would last more than 4-5 months, are not put up, and these, too, only to escape prosecutions in terms of the Irrigation Ordinance.

**Irrigation Rates.**—In areas where Irrigation dues were levied 1/14th of the crop of the field was taken by the land-owner to meet the cash payment to Government. The waiving of these by Government since the time of the food-crisis has had no effect on the cultivator; for, the land-owner continued to levy them from cultivators.

### Suggestions for the improvement of the present conditions.

(1) The cultivators, as far as possible, should not be allowed to change hands.

When a field is taken on lease by a cultivator on a fixed rental, another rival cultivator offers a higher rate of interest out of sheer malice, with the result that the land-owner invariably gives the field to the latter for his own gain. The damage resulting from such a change of hands is that the bunds of the fields are not properly restored and the fields are not manured.

(2) Half the expenses in connection with the preliminary works of clearing of *Elas* and Water-courses and the building of temporary anicut, where required, should be met by the land-owners.

(3) The fencing of paddy-fields should be undertaken by the land-owners.

(4) The adoption of measures for immediate removal of silt, when such cases occur, as silt deposited in fields reduce their fertility, and it takes several years for the cultivator to bring the fields to their normal condition.

# A Contribution to the Knowledge of the Determination of Soil Fertility.

**T**HE life of micro-organisms in the soil is the result of the phenomenon of assimilation in its entirety. The vital processes are characterized by the surrounding of the chemical molecules in the living being and by a constant exchange between latent and kinetic energy. The carbo-hydrates contained in the soil, as also the nitrogenous organic substances, are assimilated, and exchange takes place in the cells of the carbo-hydrate and albumen. A part of the carbon and nitrogen is used to build up new living molecules, but the greater part of the carbon, in the form of carbo-hydrates and albumen, gradually disappears in the process of oxidation.

The decomposition products, being poisonous, are rejected by the cells, or deposited in a harmless form. The quantity of carbon dioxide exhaled by the soil when there is free admission of air, shows us the living energy of the bacteria, as does also the easy decomposition of the organic substances in the soil. The heterotrophes find in the organic substances not only a source of energy for their breathing process, but also carbon and nitrogen food sources for building up new living material.

In the bio-chemical analysis of the soil, the concentration of hydrogen ions in the soil must always be kept in view. The absorbent, unsaturated soils of humid districts, which are rich in humus and colloidal clay, usually show an acid character. The following data plainly prove how both the number of the vegetative spores, of the bacteria in the soil, and also the quantity of carbon-dioxide exhaled by the soil, are influenced by the active acidity of the soil. The experiments were carried out for 24 days in soil with 20% water at 20°C.

We have found in our laboratory experiments and those on the experimental fields, that the increase in the production of carbon-dioxide with the use of stable manure is effected on one side by the native active bacteria of the stable dung, and on the other by the supply of its organic easily decomposed substances. The stable manure must be so treated that it brings about a certain fermentation in the sense of oxide reduction. The decomposing processes of the organic substances are occasioned by the intermolecular respiration. The action of the stable manure depends not only on the quantity used, but on the quality of the decomposable organic substances and on the activity of the bacteria.

Even small quantities of stable manure can effect a notable increase of the respiration processes of the micro-organisms. On our experimental fields we have carried out experiments regarding the increase of carbon-dioxide production of the soil by stable manure. ## The Economy of Sodium Silico-fluoride as a Coagulant.

IN the *Archief voor de Rubber Cultuur*, December, 1926, Mr. N. H. Van Harpen contributes an article on "The Economy of Sodium Silico-fluoride as a Coagulant," of which the following is an abstract prepared by Mr. T. E. H. O'Brien, Chemist to the Ceylon Rubber Research Scheme:—

### Introduction

Since 1922 the Rubber Growers' Association has been investigating the suitability of sodium silico-fluoride as a coagulant. In s.s.f. it was the intention to obtain a coagulant (1) easier to handle than acetic acid, being a solid coagulant (2) acting as a mould preventive (3) cheaper than the usual coagulants.

The results of vulcanisation tests of rubber prepared with s.s.f. were favourable. The opinion was expressed that the slower rate of cure was of secondary importance since the process can be regulated by adding accelerators.

From a communication on the susceptibility to mould it appears that sheets prepared with s.s.f. arrived at their destination mouldy. Consequently one of the principal arguments in favour of the use of the substance was void, namely, its mould-preventing action.

O. de Vries in a communication of the Central Rubber Station, 1925, considered the question from all sides and concluded that up to the present the use of s.s.f. as a coagulant offered no advantages, especially since formic acid had proved to be a coagulant 50 per cent. cheaper than acetic acid.

Since the English Investigators persistently recommended its use the question arose whether s.s.f. could be cheaper than formic acid. The correct concentration of the latex was also investigated, since opinions differed in this respect.

### Experimental.

The solubility of s.s.f. in water was determined at tropical temperature ( $28.5^{\circ}\text{C}$ ) and was found to be approximately 0.833 per cent. Different samples showed slight variability in solubility. The solubility at  $100^{\circ}\text{C}$ . was 2.52 per cent.

The rate of solution of s.s.f. was tested to determine how much time elapses before it reaches its maximum solubility. The maximum solubility was reached in 30 minutes. In 1 minute 62 per cent. dissolved.

### Coagulation Experiments with Sodium Silico-fluoride.

Different quantities of solid s.s.f. were added to 15 per cent. ( $1\frac{1}{2}$  lb. per gallon) latex and after stirring for a few seconds left for 24 hours. The minimum was found to lie between 0.6 and 0.8 grs. per litre of latex

 In each case the serum was cloudy.

## Experiments with Sodium Silico-fluoride Solution.

S.S.F. has the property of forming super-saturated solutions. S.S.F. acts more slowly than formic acid enabling air bubbles enclosed in the latex to rise to the surface.

Experiments were made with weaker solutions of s.s.f., one being saturated at normal temperature and the other half this concentration. In latex samples of various concentrations it was found possible to obtain a clear serum. It was concluded that 15 per cent. ( $1\frac{1}{2}$  lb. per gallon) was the most suitable concentration.

The minimum amount of s.s.f. required for coagulation of 100 cc. of 15 per cent. latex is 0.67 grams. (English equivalent 1 lb. s.s.f. to 224 lb. rubber at  $1\frac{1}{2}$  lb. per gallon).

As regards the effect of stirring when using s.s.f. solution, stirring for 15 minutes reduced the time of coagulation from 100 minutes to 28 minutes. Latex coagulated with Formic acid (1:300) coagulated in 28 minutes without stirring. Stirring for 5 minutes reduced the time of coagulation to 12 minutes.\*

In a comparison between sheets coagulated with formic acid and s.s.f., the formic acid sheets became pitted after being left unrolled for 24 hours. After 48 hours they could not be considered of standard quality. The s.s.f. sheets on the contrary were quite sound after 24 hours and only slightly pitted after 48 hours.

There is thus a considerable advantage in coagulating with s.s.f. on rainy days when the latex arrives late and normally the working up of the sheets would be delayed until a late hour at night. With s.s.f. the sheets can be finished next morning, the coagulum being no longer soft and easier to handle.

Zinc pans especially when new are corroded by s.s.f. thus increasing the amount required for coagulation. Aluminium pans would probably also be attacked.

## Forest Destruction and its Effects.

**T**HE question of the action of forests on rainfall has been debated by foresters, agriculturists, engineers, and others for a long period, the discussion probably dating back to the time at which scientific forest conservancy was first introduced. In the tropical and sub-tropical parts of the world this is not, however, the point of primary importance. The vital factor for the community at large is the determination of how far the destruction of forests in catchment areas and on the sides of hills and mountains in the drier parts of a country, affects, in the first place, the level of the water in the big rivers, a matter of extreme importance when the rivers are utilised for irrigation or power works; secondly, the decrease in the local water-supplies and in local precipitations upon which the cultivator is dependent, and, thirdly, erosion and avalanches, and the destruction they cause in the fertile valleys beneath. Sudden floods may also cause enormous damage to railways, towns, and so forth. In India, which was the first part of the British Empire to give consideration to this aspect of the forest question, the matter has been the subject of discussion and reports through the whole of the past century, a statement which will perhaps come as a surprise to many in Great Britain.

The problem of affording protection to forests for the above causes alone is by no means new. In France and Germany special laws for the protection and extension of the forests and the protection of agricultural lands by means of the forest have long been in operation; and similar laws exist in the Italian States. So far back as 1475 the subject attracted the attention of the famous Venetian Council of X., by whom a law was passed on January 7 of that year, regulating in great detail the clearance of the forests *terra firma*. The mountain forests especially were protected by judicious regulations, which were renewed from time to time down to the very year of the extinction of the old republics. Tuscany and the Pontifical Governments were equally provident.

History has since shown that the wholesale destruction of forests in Spain, Italy, Sicily, Greece, and Macedonia has resulted in a great deterioration of climate over considerable tracts, due to loss of moisture, the sterilisation of the soil, and excessive erosion. Although now well known, the chief action of the forest may be stated briefly as follows: The great factor in mountainous and hilly country is the maintenance of tree growth on parts of the area. In the case of bare slopes the rain rushes rapidly down, causing erosion, only a fraction percolating into the soil, and is carried rapidly away, giving rise to spates and perhaps to serious floods, since

39------------------------------------------------

168

the old channels of these streams or rivers are no longer able to carry the excess water of flood levels. A hot sun bursting out on to the slope after the rain quickly dries up the thin layer of moisture covering it. In the hotter parts of the globe subject to heavy rainstorms or monsoons the rushing water starts gullies which eventually become ravines, all surface soil is rapidly washed away, and in the course of years the hillside is eaten into, rubble and boulders being sent down to cover up valuable lands below. When the area is under trees, a portion of the rain, falling on the crowns, drips slowly, down on to the layer of humus beneath and sinks into it. The larger portion perhaps, falls direct on to the forest floor, where it is gradually absorbed in the soft covering which takes it up as a sponge. The water then percolates slowly downwards, filling up springs and underground reservoirs, and reaches the streams in a retarded manner. The flow in the latter is consequently more even and regulated, as also the amount of water which eventually reaches the rivers. The latter can therefore be more depended upon to maintain a normal level when it is required to utilise them for irrigation or power works. The roots of trees protect the surface by holding up the soil, and thus directly prevent denudation.

It is possible to give some concrete examples of the effects of the destruction of teak forests in India during the first half of last century, owing to the large demands for this timber from rapidly expanding markets.

The slopes on the west coast of the Bombay Presidency were once, even in the early days of British occupation, covered with magnificent, valuable, and extensive teak forests. These have long since been cut out, some disappearing for good. The denudation of the Deccan Highlands and the Eastern Ghats has resulted in excessive erosion and the gradual silting up of the rivers. When the Dutch, French, and English first built settlements on the Coromandel Coast, it was possible to take ships up the Godaveri and Kistna. The English port of Narasapur and the French one of Yunaon, both on the Godaveri, were once the chief ports on this coast. They can now be reached only at high tide by small native shallow-draught craft. Last year the present writer had arranged to go down the Godaveri from Sironcha, on the frontier of South Canara (Central Provinces) and the Hyderabad State, to Rajamundri, as he wished to carry out investigations in connexion with the effects of forest denudation on this river. It was early in March, the commencement of the hot weather season only. Inquiries elicited the fact that few rafts were now going down, owing to the extensive sandbanks already drying off in the river, and that even by dugout canoes, delays from stranding on sandbanks would be inevitable. Some hundred years ago this great river was the chief artery or high road into the interior. At Masulipatam, Dutch ships used to ride at anchor close up to the port, whereas at the present day even small native vessels have to anchor five miles out in the roads owing to the silting up. Between 1840 and 1850, Dr. Gibson, the first Conservator of Forests in Bombay, drew up a list of the rivers and creeks on the Malabar coast, where on arrival in those parts ships used to ride at anchor, all the creeks having silted up within the memory of men then alive.

Dr. Cleghorn, who afterwards became the first Conservator of Forests in Madras, directed attention to the destruction of tropical forests at the meeting of the British Association in Edinburgh in 1850. A committee was appointed to consider this matter. Dr. Cleghorn submitted its report, which was confined to India, the only country for which information was available, at the meeting of the Association at Ipswich the following year. The report summarised the position, as then known to the few in India who had given attention to the matter, pointing to the great and uncontrolled destruction which was taking place, both at the hands of timber merchants and owing to the careless habits of the native populations, who grazed their cattle at will in the forests and fired them every year in order to encourage the growth of new grass. The indigenous tribes in the hilly country also practised unchecked shifting cultivation, a practice second only to the lumberer in the destruction of fine forests. Under this method, which was a common habit in Europe in olden times, a patch of good forest is felled and the material burnt *in situ*; coarse grains are then sown on the clearing. The cultivator then sits down and awaits the harvest. Two or three crops are taken off the area; the weeds then become too strong (as he never troubles to weed) and he moves on to a fresh area. The enormous destruction of virgin forest this practice entails, when practised for centuries, has to be seen to be credited. Yet many of the tropical and sub-tropical forests in British Colonies and Dependencies are still subject to this the most pernicious and precarious form of so-called agriculture (as also to over-grazing and firing), the administrations responsible not having yet, apparently, understood the evils which attend it. The difficulties facing these Governments in prohibiting the practice or controlling it were all experienced in India, in one form or another, and overcome. The encouragement given to the growth of tea and coffee and similar crops by British administrations in the Empire, whilst eminently praiseworthy if carried out on well-considered lines, has been productive of great harm in the past, and even the present day can scarcely be said to be free from anxiety on this score. In a report written in India in 1876 with reference to coffee planting, the following criticism is made:

"The planters who come over from Ceylon are now giving a very high price for land, and the whole mischief may be effected in a very short time. It must not be supposed that coffee is at all a permanent cultivation; we have only to look at the Sampajee Ghat in Coorg, the Sispara Ghat in the Nilgiris, and parts of the Annamalais to see at once that it is very often very little better than the shifting cultivation of the natives. It pays a coffee planter to take up a tract of primeval moist forest on our mountain slopes for a few years; he gets bumper crops the third, fourth and fifth years, but denudation of the soil and erosion goes on rapidly, and it does not pay him to keep it up many years."

The hills were rapidly denuded of timber and grazing was uncontrolled. The crops are irrigated from tanks (ponds) formed by building embankments across ravines. Some of these were very old. The

It may be hoped, therefore, that the chief factors of destruction, namely, shifting cultivation, excessive grazing and the firing of forest lands, may receive that measure of considered control which the expert forestry services under the Colonial Office are fully capable of inaugurating if supported by the several administrations.—*Nature*, Vol. CXIX, No. 2,984.

### Producing Countries.

Grape fruit is grown in *South Africa* in practically all the orange-growing districts, but certain coastal strips are more favourable for the production of a good commercial article than the inland areas. Government standards of grading and packing are laid down for all grape fruit exported. Its export is not subject to any special standards of grading and packing prescribed either by producers' organisations or by the local Governments. Although grape fruit is grown in Australia, practically none is exported.

There are considerable possibilities of grape fruit production in *West Africa*, where experimental plots have recently been cultivated with considerable success, the crop being very heavy. Further local investigation is, however, necessary into the quality of the fruit, the cost of establishing production on a commercial scale, and the provision of land and labour. *We recommend that local investigation into these questions should be undertaken by the local Governments and that their inquiries should be correlated by the Executive Commission.*

The competition to which Empire grape fruit is exposed in the United Kingdom market comes from fruit of California and Florida origin. The latter is marketed under a well-known trade name, the actual indication being stamped on the skin of the fruit. This is undoubtedly the ideal way of indicating the origin of fruit, for unlike the paper wrapper adopted for South African and West Indian fruit, it cannot be removed when the fruit is exhibited for sale. Californian grape fruit production is organized by the Californian Fruit Growers' Exchange and marketed by exactly the same machinery as operates for oranges. By careful cultivation of the fruit a very high standard of quality and appearance has been secured. Very careful grading is carried out before the fruit is marketed. The success of the United States organizations supplying grape fruit to the United Kingdom market constitutes a striking illustration of the possibility of creating a market which did not exist before.

## The Fertility of the Garden.

**S**OILS, like facts, are stubborn, things; yet, fortunately for the gardener, they are not quite so stubborn, for if coaxed and bullied sufficiently they may be so altered that their fertility is increased greatly. Both coaxing and bullying are necessary instruments for the training of the soil in the way it should go; though it must be confessed that of the two the latter is the more potent, as it used to be in the education of those of us who are now elderly. Once the fertility of a garden soil has been achieved it is not difficult to maintain it; but in these days it is far less easy, in spite of the great growth of knowledge of the science of the soil, to bring, unkindly ground into a highly fertile state. Moreover, there are some soils which when brought under garden cultivation give for a year or two prodigious crops and then, as though growing tired of well-doing, regress to parsimonious yielding. Wherever this occurs the gardener may be sure that the falling away from grace is due to some defect in the essential conditions upon which a good soil depends, and generally it will be found that the defaulting condition is insufficient drainage. A wettish year, or ditches left uncleaned, may result in the winter water-logging of a soil which had previously seemed well on the way to fertility. It is for this reason that every good gardener gives first attention to drainage in laying out his garden; for he knows that what is easy to do *ab initio* is troublesome and difficult to accomplish, once the garden has been laid out. He is wise also in this, that he makes and preserves a plan of the drainage system, for he knows that within a few years the best of memories will grow dim, and long before the garden is fully established, it will assuredly happen with respect to the lie of the drainage tiles, that all the rest for which he toiled will be forgotten. In the old days it was possible, when plenty of labour was available, to ensure soil fertility by deep digging. The ground destined to become a garden was trenched three feet deep and all manner of material, which in its decay makes for soil fertility, was incorporated with the soil, so that even the unkindly turned up sub-soil was constrained to become kindly to the growth of plants. It is in some ways unfortunate that in our climate less drastically sound methods often lead to a measure of success; for it tempts those who are in a hurry to forego the toil on which alone soil fertility securely rests. There are, however, countries where, unless the soil be stirred to at least a

48------------------------------------------------

177

yard in depth, nothing will grow. On the Riviera, for example, anyone who would make a garden must, unless he would ensure failure, delve with mattock and shovel a full yard deep, for there, unless this be done, summer drought extinguishes anything recently planted, and the garden returns to natural scrub. In any case, if Pope, who was a gardener, had been thinking of soils instead of knowledge, he might have revised his famous lines and written "a little digging is a dangerous thing—dig deep or dig not . . . . . " Next, perhaps, in importance to drainage in the making of soil fertility is liming—liming on heavy, chalking for light soils—for, as everyone knows, soils have what is called a lime requirement which ranges from nil on chalky soils to a very high figure on the distinctly acid soils. This requirement is easy of ascertainment and must be satisfied if soil is to become fertile. For it is a fact that most of the plants grown in gardens—save, of course, the lime-shy contingents—thrive best when the soil borders on neutrality. It is certain that, speaking generally, the kitchen gardens of this country would be rendered more apt to become fertile if they were to receive, say triennially, a dressing of lime in quantities in accordance with their several lime requirements. They would become kindlier, the stiff ones easier to work, the light ones less skimpy as a result of the treatment and, moreover, they would all respond better to manurial treatment. With drainage satisfactory, ground dug deeply, and the lime requirement of the soil satisfied, the gardener has in his soil a well-equipped plant factory which, if supplied with a sufficiency of the right kinds of raw material, will yield a large output in the form of heavy crops. But to secure this end and to maintain production it must be remembered that large crops make large demands on the soil. And not only that, but also that the demands are of more than one kind. Although it must never remain wet, the ground is obliged, if it is to produce a heavy crop, to yield to the growing plant vast quantities of water. Moreover soil fertility consists in no small measure in balancing as it were, the water-supply and air-supply, which all plants must have. It too wet, the roots of the plants growing in the soil are asphyxiated; if too dry, they dry out. Hence the importance of depth of soil and efficient drainage. The one condition ensures a large reservoir, the other the removal of surplus water. When all these conditions have been established, the gardener can turn his attention to the problem of manuring, and this subject, as he well knows, has a dual aspect.—*The Gardeners' Chronicle*, Vol. LXXXI., No. 2091.

## Selecting Sires for the Dairy Herd

**T**HERE is no more difficult problem in farm live-stock breeding than the dairy sire, and when one of the principal aims is dual-purpose character the difficulty is increased rather than diminished. The choice of the sire is, of course, a matter of cardinal importance and of very great difficulty in all stockbreeding enterprises. There are many characteristics to be looked for in all sires according to the requirements of the breed, but in most instances all the qualities that meet the eye are as easily discernible in one sex as in the other. In the case of dairy cattle, however, milking character difficult to detect with certainty in the female is much more obscure in the male.

Beef is as easily discerned in the bull as in the cow, mutton and wool in the ram as in the ewe, bone, action and symmetry in the stallion as in the mare, fleshing and form in boar as in the sow, and it is an axiom of stockbreeding that like tends to produce like. In building up the accumulation of merits that makes for prepotency and consistency we can examine, as far as our knowledge goes, the merits of all ancestors, but in the case of dairy cattle we have gaps all along the male side, including the bull, whose value we are trying to assess. For pedigree knowledge of the bull's worth for milk we must rely almost entirely upon the merits of the cows from which he is descended.

### Milk Records.

Milk records have, of course, helped to simplify the task of choosing a bull for a dairy herd, but it has also introduced dangers that are very real. It is common knowledge, indeed general experience, that a heavy milking cow does not always produce heavy milking daughters. We may have a fifteen-hundred gallon cow, some of whose daughters become her equal or even superior in milk production, while others, perhaps full sisters, cannot be prevailed upon to give half of this amount. We may even have cows, all of whose daughters fall short of them as milkers without any adequate explanation being discoverable in the breeding on the sire's side.

As daughters of a heavy milker may fail to inherit their dam's milking character, or may inherit it in varying degrees, so we may assume that her sons will vary. Indeed, no guessing is required, for it is every-day experience that they do. In the female the lapse is detected, but we have to wait for another generation to discover it in the male. Milk records are a great assistance, but they do not provide data from which infallible deductions can be drawn.

## Blind Reliance on Records.

It has been mentioned that a real danger attaches to milk records, and this is to be found partly in a blind faith that a bull well bred for milk must produce heavy milkers, but chiefly in making the scales the supreme test, or worshipping at the shrine of exceptional records. In the breeding of pedigree-cattle breed character must remain one of the primary considerations, and in the breeding of all dairy cattle form, stamina, and constitution cannot be ignored. It happens with all breeds that some of the heaviest milkers are in themselves moderate animals, judged by every standard except milk production, and it may be claimed that in the dairy herd milk production is almost the only thing that matters. Prolonged and persistent milk production is, however, largely a matter of stamina, stamina again of conformation and conformation of consistent breeding to one standard. A blind trust in milk records must lead quickly into degeneracy, and prove a handicap rather than assistance. Because it is the object to breed heavy yielders it does not follow that the best results will always be obtained from breeding from those with the greatest yields to their credit.

## The Breeder's Problem.

The breeder's problem is how best to combine breed character, conformation, stamina, and yielding power. What particular characteristic should he build upon? Should he start with heavy yielding capacity, and try to graft the others on to it, or should he begin with the character and conformation, and work towards the yield? I do not think that there can be any question about the latter being the safer course. Character and conformation are much more difficult to regain if they are lost, and when they are lost it is only a matter of time, and generally a short time, before milking capacity goes with them. We do not require to go to poor milkers to obtain the necessary character and constitution, and heavy production does not necessarily mean that they must be absent. If we can get all the required qualifications in one animal it is likely to prove the one we are looking for, and if it transmits all its merits it certainly is. This combination, however, is so rare that it is negligible, and it is the safer plan to look first for primary merits, and take as much as we can of the others.

Success in dairy cattle breeding is founded upon constitution. Heavy milk production is a severe tax on a cow's system. No cow with a weak constitution can continue giving very heavy yields, heavy according to her size, but in the absence of cast-iron stamina she may ruin such constitution as she has. It is quite possible and very common for a cow to have milking character beyond her constitution, with the result that she and her race fade away. The use of a bull from such a dam can only end in disaster.

In the early days of milk recording one frequently heard the objection that it would destroy the capacity for good judgment, which would decay owing to disuse, but so far from this being the case milk records increase the field for the exercise of sound judgment. They supply more precise information along one particular line, so that the breeder instead of guessing at certain things has trustworthy data to go upon. He does not find matters settled for him, but only obtains facts to help him to arrive at a decision.

## Analogy with Laying Poultry.

There is a fairly close analogy between laying poultry and the dairy herd. In each case the return comes through an essentially female-function. In both cases a sire's value can be gauged only by the performance of his daughters, but in poultry breeding the returns come much more quickly, so

51------------------------------------------------

180

that breeding experience accumulates more rapidly. It has been found in poultry breeding that the big layers are seldom bred from stock with extreme egg records. The tax upon the constitution of the heavy layer is similar to that upon the constitution of the heavy milker.

Now all this might be taken to point to heavy yields in dairy cattle as a mistake. Far from it, for heavy yields must be the aim, and the only ultimate aim, in the breeding of dairy stock where dual-purpose character is not a plank of the breeders' platform. What I desire to emphasise is that the desired yields cannot be attained and retained without due regard to the constitution and stamina of which vigour is but a sign.

## Importance of the Udder.

There is another fault often seen in heavy milkers—a badly shaped udder. Indeed, many of our heaviest yielders have rather atrocious vessels. What, then, is the sense of striving after symmetry and shape of bag when the shapely vessel is outstripped in milk production by the ugly, pendulous, badly-balanced, split-up and windy-teated vessel? Here again, however, we have to look to the future rather than the present. A good level vessel is much more likely to wear well, and is less liable to the ills that udders are heir to. The sloppy, pendulous bag is more liable to injury, and the coarse teats cost much more to milk. Incidentally easy milking does not, perhaps get all the attention it deserves in these days of high wages; a hard milker that keeps a man a few minutes longer at every milking brings a considerable bill against herself in the course of a year. The shape of the udder is not a fancy point set up against utility qualities. The stockbreeder has to wage a constant war against degeneracy and to the correction of faults—faults that in moderation are not a serious blot on the commercial value of an animal, but that if allowed to develop unchecked would ruin any race of cattle. There can be no question that the sire is a very important factor in determining the shape of the udder, and a son of a cow with a bad udder is not likely to give satisfactory results even if the dam's record is exceptional.

## Breed Character.

The maintenance of breed type and character sometimes involve points that it is somewhat difficult to separate from the fancy, but one must always be careful before treating lightly any well established breed characteristics. One of the faults we have to guard against in all breed is degeneracy into mongrelism, with the loss of the prepotency built up by generations of careful breeding. Breed type is an important foundation in breeding for the future, and in maintaining its standard, and is an elementary factor that no breeder can afford to neglect. Indeed, in pedigree breeding it must be the foundation upon which all is built.

In the selection of the stock bull the breeder of dairy cattle has to study breed type, character, form, constitution, and pedigree. He must get not only a good bull, but one breed from good stock as far as he can discover. He must get the best milk records in the immediate dams he can obtain, combined with those other qualities, and even so bulls will be found that fall much short of expectations. This failure of pedigree may sometimes be apparent in the beef male when it is hidden in the dairy-bred bull. The supreme difficulty is that the breeder has to discover it in the failure of his stock. He may have suspicions when heifers have reached a year or so of age, but he has to wait until they have calved before he feels even fairly certain, and until the sire's daughters have reached second-calf stage, before he can be dogmatic. In other words, no one can speak with certainty about the dairy sire until he has reached five or six years of age.—*The Farmer & Stock-Breeder and Agricultural Gazette*, Vol. XL., No. 1894

## Correspondence of Interest.

### Manuring of Citrus.

**P** ANNIPITIYA.—The citrus fruit in other countries do not profit by heavy applications of nitrogenous manures and frequently such applications give rise to gummosis and other troubles. In Ceylon, however, soils are generally poor in humus and, therefore, applications of organic nitrogenous manures are indicated. Applications of phosphatic manures and potash usually results in increased crops, and a mixture of bone meal and sulphate of potash in the proportion of 2 to 1 is generally suitable. For small plants an application of  $\frac{1}{2}$  lb. per plant is adequate, but

for fully grown trees applications of up to 4 lbs. per tree may be made.

2. The trees should be lightly forked around before the application and then the application forked in, but deep forking is not desirable. The circle should be greater for older trees than for young ones, but in no case should be nearer than 3 feet from the stem.

3. The best time for applications of manures to fruit trees would probably be during the months of either February or September.

# Vegetative Propagation.

## Review of Some Recent Literature.

THE value of vegetative propagation for multiplying a single desirable plant on a large scale is well recognised. With perennial crops, where the creation of high yielders on a commercial scale has thus far offered difficulties, vegetative propagation at least assures the means of obtaining many plants similar to a chosen desirable specimen, without waking up in the next generation, some bad characters from among the thousand heredities bound up in one plant.

Not only for obtaining high yielders is this means of propagation used, but also for begetting other favourable qualities. By selective grafting based on the choice of the best types, including comparison of fruits and examination of their physical characters, Chance, Church and Denny of the United States Department of Agriculture showed the possibilities of obtaining plants best suited for particular purposes, e.g., the production of essential oils, acids, etc.

### Mangosteen.

Mr. P. J. Westert of the Philippines has given the mangosteen a more vigorous root system and tried to make it grow quicker and produce earlier than it does on its own roots. He inarched a seedling of mangosteen on a native *Garcinia* locally called *Bunag*. The union was successful and the plant is said to be in excellent condition. The success is especially striking as, for 25 years, repeated unsuccessful attempts have been made to graft the mangosteen on more than 20 species of *Garcinia* and related plants. The drought resistance and vigorous growth of the stock will help the mangosteen to produce several years earlier and spread over larger areas than it would on its own roots.

### Avocado, Mango and Seedless Breadfruit.

In California the method of budding of avocados has been initiated in order to

shorten the period of fruiting of seedlings. Pyerson<sup>†</sup> describes the technique of the operation which consists in the use of a germinating embryo as scion in about the same way as in budding. The seedling, he says, makes very rapid progress, and the operation is not difficult.

Budding and grafting of avocado on a large scale has been done in Hawaii. Of all the methods, whip grafting, using very young growth, proved most satisfactory. Side-tongue grafting was most successful with mangos. The propagation of the indigenous and the Society Islands seedless breadfruit from surface roots is yet in progress.

### Tea.

Dr. Keuchenius<sup>§</sup> of Java describes, in *Meded. v. h. Proefstation Voor Thee LXXXIV.*, thirteen different methods of vegetative propagation of tea. Records of results achieved by him are as follows: inarching, though tedious and expensive gave 98 per cent. successes; crown-grafting gave an average of 74 per cent. successes; rectangular patch grafting gave 87 per cent.; upright stem layering, roots entering soil on a split bamboo, gave more than 50 per cent. These figures compare very favourably indeed with the success obtained by placing cuttings of branches stripped of leaves in moist soil. Of these only 2 per cent. propagated. Experiments in Ceylon too are referred to in the editorial of the *Tropical Agriculturist* for February, 1924.

### Coffee.

Dr. Cramer<sup>\*\*</sup> in the *Ned. Ind. Rubber en Thee Tijdschrift*, 1924, describes the work done at Lampongs and at Bangelan in the grafting of coffee. In taking up this work, the aim in view was the exploitation of the possibilities of vegetative propagation of all the tropical perennial crops. The first experiments in coffee grafting aimed at protecting the Arabian coffee from the eelworm by grafting it on a resistant root system. Dr. Cramer

#### Rubber.

The Dutch in Java have conducted experiments in budding rubber through the last decade and the articles by Dr. Heusser and Dr. Cramer and many others appearing in *Archief Voor de Rubber-cultuur* in the past three years trace the progress they have made. By budding from high yielders the average yield has been increased considerably as the comparative figures\* for seedlings, selected seed and budded rubber given below show:—

Plantation of heavy yielders 498 lb. per acre

Plantation of selected seed 543 lb. per acre

Plantation of budded rubber 660 lb. per acre.

The Director of Agriculture, Ceylon, concisely sums up, in the last volume of the *Tropical Agriculturist*, the position of budded rubber in Malaya, Java and Sumatra, useful reading to all embarking on a fair budding programme in Ceylon. Budding of rubber gives, in addition to high yield, a better balanced and more healthy foliated tree, remarkably striking uniformity in the plantation, and a cylindrical shaped tree.

#### Cotton.

Dr. Harland† in a very recent article, after briefly narrating the history of several experiments from 1909 in grafting and budding cotton, mentions a simple method of rapid propagation of herbaceous plants. This method was described by Blakeslee and Farnham and was called "bottle grafting." Mr. E. E. Cheeseman successfully applied this method at Dr. Harland's suggestion and now, in 2 years' time, several hundred plants have been grafted. Sea Island, Egyptian and Up-land cottons have been grafted on seedlings of the more vigorous perennials. The technique, with a good clear illustration of the graft, is described by Dr. Harland.

#### Cacao. ‡

Dr. Harland observed that the cacao in River Estate, Trinidad, branched in two different ways: the fan-form, the branches having no definite termination, and the choupon form, the branches terminating in five parts, each of which behaves like a fan. He noted that the budded cacao was invariably fan-form. When pruned back, to determine the consistency of these habits, 57 of 59 fans gave rise to fans again, and 24 out of 40 choupions gave rise to choupions.
