STATISTICS IN RURAL ECONOMY

STATISTICS were originally considered to be figures relating to the resources of the State with regard to its man power, and were at first almost solely concerned with the numbering of human heads. However useful such figures may be in levying a poll-tax they by themselves are of little more value. As statesmanship developed the application of information to demonstrate the condition of the people became more and more of use until in recent times figures relating to all factors bearing upon the human population of a country have generally become recognised as a fundamental necessity in forming plans for social progress. Statistics and statistical method have become a science. A modern application of statistical method is the collection of figures of individual behaviour and the formulating and applying them to guide us in the planning of crop and animal experiments.

There is an important point that requires careful realisation and that is the clear distinction between the mere mathematics of the statistician and the deduction to be drawn from the figures, by the application of clear reasoning to them, of lines along which progress can be attained. There is a danger in statistical method becoming too mechanical when applied to living things.

123

The prevailing conditions in Ceylon at the moment as the result of pestilence and drought have brought home to us how lacking we are in statistical information that could supply us at once with much that we want to know concerning the life of the countryside. From an agricultural point of view the first but by no means the last set of figures we require is that relating to the production of our village crops apart from tea, rubber, and coconuts. We have in fact no system of crop reporting. It affords interesting reflexion to read a recent publication issued by the National Agricultural Research Bureau, Ministry of Industries, Nanking, China. A map of China is therein given showing the location of 4,095 Crop Reporters who forward figures to the Directorate of Statistics for the purpose of answering two questions.

  1. 1. What production of crops are we likely to have, or do we have, already in a given year as compared with other years?
  2. 2. What changes have taken place in the country that will affect the nation's rural economic situation for the present and for the future.

There is indication of a beginning in China along lines that might be of value in Ceylon.

THERE is an important group of diseases in cattle occurring in many parts of the world in which transmission is by ticks much in the same way as Malaria in man is transmitted by mosquitoes.

Of this group at least three occur in Ceylon and as they are of considerable economic importance it is advisable that cattle owners should be familiar with them.

The three representatives of the group which are known to occur in Ceylon are all caused by protozoal organisms which are found in the blood stream parasitising the red blood corpuscles, in this respect they can be considered as analogous with the malarial parasites of man.

The diseases caused by these three parasites are characterised by fever, anaemia and loss of condition, but they have each certain individual features which will be mentioned later.

All three diseases have the following features in common. Cattle of the Native and Indian breeds have a very marked resistance and it is seldom that serious symptoms are observed in such cattle; calves and young cattle are much less severely affected than adults; recovery from infection confers a resistance to reinfection.

Cattle of European or Australian breeds are much more susceptible than Native or Indian cattle and suffer seriously when affected.

The three diseases are extremely common in most parts of Ceylon, indeed it would probably be safe to assume that all cattle which are allowed to roam at large are infected. That is not to say that they will be showing symptoms of illness but they are harbouring the parasites in their blood and are capable of infecting ticks which may suck blood from them and so through the ticks can infect other cattle.

125

That this is so can readily be proved by taking a small quantity of blood from an animal, to all appearances healthy, which has been living in an area where it is exposed to ticks and injecting it into a susceptible animal which will develop the disease after the usual period of incubation.

In an area where these diseases are enzootic, and this applies to the greater part of Ceylon certainly to practically the whole of the low-country, symptoms of illness sufficiently severe to attract the attention of the owner are seldom or never seen in the locally bred cattle. If however cattle from an area where these diseases do not occur are introduced into an enzootic area and exposed to the bites of infected ticks very severe symptoms are soon produced.

A cattle owner in an area where these diseases are enzootic who maintains his herd by rearing his own calves and never introduces into his herd cattle from an area free from these diseases may be unaware of the existence of these parasites in his cattle.

It will thus be seen that so long as a cattle breeder in an enzootic area, as for example in the low-country, is content to breed his own stock especially if he keeps only cattle of the Native or Indian breeds, these diseases are of comparatively little importance.

On the other hand when he wishes to introduce outside cattle especially cattle of European or Australian breeds they at once become of very great importance. These diseases are the chief obstacle to the use of cattle of European or Australian breeds for the purpose of improving the breed of cattle in the low-country. The failure which so commonly follows the introduction of such cattle into the low-country and which owners usually attribute to the climate, the heat, or lack of suitable grass is in most cases due to tick-borne disease.

In Ceylon these facts are brought most strikingly to notice in connection with the trade in milch cows brought from up-country to the dairies in and around Colombo. These cows are nearly all of European or Australian breed and are bred on tea estates at high elevations. On many of the tea estates there is no place where the cattle can be grazed so they are kept in the cattle sheds from the day they are born and hand fed. As a result many of these cattle grow up to maturity without having

126

ever contracted any of the tick-borne diseases, in consequence many of them are highly susceptible when they are taken to Colombo as adult cows in milk.

On their journey to Colombo they may pick up infected ticks from the grass on the roadsides on their way to the railway station or it is possible that they may pick up ticks in the cattle trucks. On arrival in Colombo they may be taken to a dairy actually in the town where they will be kept in the cattle shed and not allowed outside. In such dairies, if they have escaped infection on the journey, up-country cattle of European or Australian breeds may do very well.

On the other hand they may be taken to a dairy on the outskirts of Colombo. Such dairies are usually on coconut estates or small coconut properties. In such dairies it is the usual practice to tie the cattle outside under the coconut palms for the greater part of the day. Here they are exposed to the attacks of ticks which have fed on infected cattle and soon contract infection.

The subsequent course of events will depend on several factors namely the degree of resistance which the cattle may or may not possess, the species of parasite with which they became infected, the extent of infection and the method of treatment adopted. Here before going further it will be advisable to mention the three common parasites found in Ceylon with a short description of their characteristics and the types of disease they may produce.

First and perhaps the most important is Piroplasma bigeminum a large pear-shaped organism found in the red corpuscles of the blood. It causes a disease which is often referred to as "Red-water" on account of the red colour of the urine which occurs in many cases. The period of incubation, that is the time between the entrance of the parasite into the body and the development of symptoms may be as short as seven days but may be anything up to one month.

In the early stages there are no striking symptoms. The appetite may not be so good as usual and the milk yield slightly decreased. There is a rise of temperature but this may easily be overlooked unless the thermometer is used. The animal does not look very ill and the owner usually thinks that the slight decrease in milk yield and slight loss of appetite is due to change

127

of climate, change of food, etc., and takes little notice. In mild cases these symptoms may continue for some time, the appetite becomes more capricious, the milk yield continues to decrease and there is a loss of condition. The urine may or may not be red in colour.

Such cases may end in recovery after a variable time without any special treatment but there is a marked loss of condition which is recovered very slowly and the milk yield is very much decreased. Such cows although they recover are often difficult to get in calf and may become quite unprofitable to their owners. In severe cases the early symptoms are soon succeeded by serious illness. The breathing is considerably accelerated, the appetite is completely lost, there is usually severe constipation, the urine becomes red or almost black in colour. Such severe cases usually end in death unless treated quickly.

The second parasite common in Ceylon is Anaplasma marginale, this is a small round parasite like a dot found inside the red corpuscles usually close to the edge. The incubation period is considerably longer than in the case of Piroplasma bigeminum and may be as long as one to two months.

As in the case of Piroplasma bigeminum the illness commences with a rise of temperature without any very marked symptoms and is easily overlooked unless the thermometer is used. Red colouration of the urine is never produced by this parasite. The striking features are rapidly developed anaemia and jaundice, constipation with yellow coloured fæces although in some rare cases there is diarrhoea. In most cases the affected cattle show an intense desire to eat sand or earth and if taken outside the shed will immediately lick and swallow great quantities of any dry sand or dusty earth which may be within reach.

There is a very rapid loss of condition and marked decrease in the milk yield. The coat becomes dry and staring and the skin 'hidebound'. This disease is not by any means so common as "Red-water" but is if anything more severe and more likely to end fatally.

The third parasite is known as Theileria mutans, a small rod or ring-shaped parasite found in the red corpuscles. This is a very common parasite in Ceylon cattle and as a rule is much less severe in its effects than the two already described. As a

128

rule it does not cause death. It is characterised by an intermittent fever of long duration with anaemia, loss of condition, and decrease in milk yield. It does not cause red discolouration of the urine.

There is reason to believe that this parasite is responsible for much of the unthriftiness, slow rate of growth and poor development of many young cattle of European or Australian breed in the low-country. While it is true that in the vast majority of cases this parasite does not cause serious illness or death, occasionally very severe and fatal cases occur especially in imported cattle. These occasional severe cases resemble in many respects the deadly East Coast Fever of Africa which is caused by a closely related parasite.

Control of Tick-borne Diseases.—The best method of control is by destruction of the ticks which are the transmitting agents. The best agent for tick destruction is an arsenical solution such as Cooper's Cattle Dip. Where large numbers of cattle are to be dealt with the most economical as well as the most efficient method of using such a solution is by means of a swim dip. Full particulars as regards the structure of these can be obtained from the Veterinary Department.

Where the number of cattle to be treated is not big enough to justify the cost of building a swim bath useful control can be obtained by spraying the solution on to the cattle by means of a spray similar to those used by gardeners for spraying insecticides on to plants. Spraying or dipping to be of use must be done regularly at fixed intervals of a week.

Oily dressings such as mixtures of coconut oil, margosa oil, and kerosene oil are useful for hand dressing of badly infested cattle but are not so effective as the arsenical dip. Where the herd consists entirely of cattle of the local or of Indian breeds the injury caused by these diseases will not be very serious but even such cattle will benefit by being kept free of ticks.

Treatment of Cattle Affected with Tick-borne Diseases.—In these diseases it is particularly important if the best results are to be obtained that treatment should commence at an early stage of the disease. Satisfactory results cannot be obtained if treatment is not started until the disease is advanced and the animal seriously ill. It is a common experience to be called to attend

129

to cases in the dairies around Colombo at such a late stage of the disease that little can be done. The importance of early recognition of the disease cannot be too strongly stressed. The majority of the cases which one is called upon to treat are in cows recently brought from up-country to the Colombo dairies.

Owners of such dairies can save themselves considerable losses if they will observe the following system. The system is in use at the Government Dairy, Colombo, and has effected a great reduction in the loss of newly purchased up-country cows following their arrival at the dairy.

The system may be summarised as follows:

  1. (1) Immediately the cows arrive at the dairy from the train examine them carefully for young seed ticks. If these are found have them destroyed either by hand picking, dressing with Cooper's Dip or with an oily dressing.
  2. (2) Keep the cows in the shed, do not tie them out on grass under coconut trees. See that the shed is thoroughly clean and free from ticks.
  3. (3) From the day the cows arrive until at least a month afterwards take the temperature morning and evening and note it on a sheet of paper. Any rise of temperature over 102.5^{\circ} F. should be regarded with suspicion. The cow will not look ill and the temptation to attribute the rise in temperature to some other causes should be resisted.
  4. (4) When the rise in temperature occurs and before the cow looks ill or refuses her food make two or three blood films and send to the Veterinary Department office for examination. Any cattle owner can have the method of making blood films explained and glass slides supplied if he will call at the Veterinary Department office.
  5. (5) While awaiting the result of examination of the slides keep the cow comfortable, give a good bed of straw and if the nights are chilly cover the cow with a gunny bag. If there is any tendency to constipation give the following: Epsom salts \frac{1}{2} lb., Glauber salts \frac{1}{2} lb., green ginger juice \frac{1}{2} a teacupful, water 2 bottles, or perhaps better than the Epsom and Glauber salts is 1 lb. of common table salt plus the green ginger juice and water.

Should examination of the blood films show that Piroplasma bigeminum is present an intravenous injection of a drug known as Trypan blue should be given at once. This can be done by the Veterinary Officer. If given early the results are extremely satisfactory and a quick return to normal health can be looked for with confidence.

If the examination shows that Anaplasma marginale is the cause of the trouble the prognosis is not so good as in the case of Piroplasma bigeminum. Many drugs have been tried. Trypan blue is of no value for this parasite. The best results so far have been obtained by injecting Novarsenobillon intravenously and this should be done at once. The bowels must be kept open and in this disease common table salt appears to be better than Epsom or Glauber salts. Careful nursing and stimulants such as arrack and ginger if signs of collapse are shown.

In the case of Theileria mutans infection no specific drug has been discovered as yet. Novarsenobillon appears to be of some value. Nutritious feeding and careful nursing are very important.

In all these diseases there is considerable destruction of the red cells of the blood and tonics are of value in the convalescent stages such for example as Sulphate of Iron in half drachm doses each day, powdered Nux Vomica half drachm to one drachm per day. Water ad lib. should be given in all these diseases. If the temperature is very high Sodium Salicylate is useful and this may be given two or three times a day in heaped teaspoonful doses.

The first month or two after an up-country cow is brought to the low-country is a very critical period. Taking the temperature morning and evening and acting immediately a rise occurs will save much loss. Owners cannot be too careful at this time, the purchase and regular use of a thermometer will be found to be a very good investment.

THE PERIOD OF EXPANSION, 1900-1923

THE work of Lawes and Gilbert stimulated interest in agricultural experiments in many parts of the world. In England, after the death of the Harpenden pioneers, the classical work at Rothamsted continued, but no important development resulted until the second decade of the century when more land became available through the help of the Development Commission in 1911. Agricultural colleges and farm institutes entered the experimental field, but with few exceptions, such as at Cockle Park, their efforts were not conceived on the same scale or with the same precision as at Rothamsted. The war, though it provided new problems in crop production, depleted the personnel of agricultural investigators, and thus militated against progress.

In the United States of America activity was more remarkable. Experiment Stations sprang up in a great many states, and much time and energy was devoted to field experiments. The incentive of the Rothamsted work is undeniable, but the American Stations embarked on a wider sphere of experimentation than had been customary in England. Every conceivable type of cultural and varietal experiment is to be found in the early records of the American Stations, but nowhere is there the same evidence of continuity as at Rothamsted. It was inevitable that in a country that was new in the agricultural sense, where there were many pressing problems, the satisfactory solution of these problems could not keep pace with the continual expansion of agricultural effort. The urgent demand for specific information led to the institution of short time experiments, the results of which were frequently contradictory. The volume of this work can be judged from the fact that, in the 'eighties of

132

last century, the Experimental Station Record was started, to give abstract reports of the published results from the many stations already in existence.

By the beginning of the present century it was beginning to be realised that the promise of clear-cut guidance from the multifarious field experiments was not being fulfilled, and this gave rise to dissatisfaction with field experimental methods. American commentators of that time are commendably candid about the state of affairs. Piper and Stevenson (15) in a paper on the standardisation of field experiments, in 1909, suggest that though in the past the large sources of error in field experiments had been recognised, the difficulty of such experiments had exercised a retrogressive influence on those whose work it was to carry them out. In short, experimentalists had become unnecessarily careless in their technique. They had reached an impasse, for whilst they admitted the existence of formidable obstacles to accurate experimentation, they were undecided as to what steps to take to mend matters. This was the state of affairs when in 1907 the Journal of the American Society of Agronomy commenced publication, and led the way in the study of more refined methods of experiment.

In the immediately succeeding years, whilst agronomists were casting about for a solution to their difficulties, a number of workers in widely scattered centres were considering a new method of approach, the use of the theory of probability in the interpretation of divergent results in agricultural trials. To whom priority should be given is not clear. At first an approximate methods was used, and Egorov (16) in Russia employed the mean deviation of plot yield as a measure of heterogeneity. He studied reduction in error as plot sizes increased from one to 120 units of approximately 50 square feet (one square sazhen). His results were published in 1909. Wood and Stratton (17) in 1910 gave the first explanatory statement of field errors in England, and this was followed by Hall and Russell (18) in 1911, by Mercer and Hall (19) in the same year, and by continental workers later. Kostecki (20), Fröhlich (21), Frischauf (22), Lehn (23), Gregoire (24), and in Japan, Miyake (25), had all examined the method of least squares in relationship to agricultural experiments, particularly in variety trials, between 1912 and 1916. For the moment the history of these early attempts to apply statistical method is all that concerns us, but Student's work whilst amongst the earliest, had an aspect of modernity that makes it the link between the earlier and the later development in method; and because of its great importance its consideration will occupy a separate section later.

Wood and Stratton, (loc. cit.) set out to examine the general magnitude of crop experimental errors and their relation to size. By combining small plots on a uniformity trial of mangolds, they concluded that 1/80 acre represented an optimum size; and that for ranges larger than that there was no correlation between size and error. From 200 pairs of similarly treated plots drawn from crops of wheat, barley, oats, mangolds, swedes, potatoes and seed-hay they assessed the average probable error per plot as 5 per cent. a value which agreed with that for optimum plot size from the detailed mangold experiment. In the light of subsequent work they undoubtedly underestimated the plot variability, for their comparisons were based upon a larger number of plots than it is usual to find in a single agricultural trial. This however affected the permanent value of their contribution to the subject but little, for they had focussed attention on plot errors, and they had laid the foundation of replication as the remedy.

Mercer and Hall re-emphasized the necessity for careful choice of land and further explored useful plot sizes. They were not so fortunate as Wood and Stratton in their diminution of error with increasing size, and they recommended 1/40 acre as the standard optimum size. They too were optimistic in their estimate of ultimate error values, suggesting that a fivefold replication would give errors below two per cent. on land judged to be suitable for experimental purposes. In conjunction with Wood and Stratton they were responsible for the replacement of large or single plots, by small replicated units.

THE CHECK PLOT METHOD

At this time the main contribution from the American Stations was the development of the check plot. There was much activity in varietal work, and the changes that could be rung on the varietal theme were more extensive than with

135

manurial treatments. Faced with a continually expanding list of varieties in cereals, and discontented with the old single plot methods, the experiment stations tried to combine control of heterogeneity with economy of plots by replication of standard check plots. The general principle was to arrange that at intervals of three, four or five plots, a repetition of a standard variety should occur to which all comparisons should be referred. This method of multiple controls was first used in Sweden by Holtsmark and Larsen (26) with check plots every third plot. They used the three nearest controls as the basis for their comparisons and corrected this average by reference to the average of all controls. Leidner (27) and his colleagues also favoured multiple control plots, though they concluded, after trial of this and other methods, that the ideal method had yet to be found. There is evidence of a considerable variety of methods of interpretation. In some cases attention was restricted to the two checks lying on either side of the specific variety. The assumption was made that fertility gradient between check plots was uniform. A simple formula based on this hypothesis allowed the potential fertility of the intermediate plots to be calculated, and this hypothetical plot value was used as a correction.

In other cases the correction for fertility was made more general by taking into account variation over the whole area. For any particular variety the correction was the difference between the nearest check yield and the average check yield. The sign was positive or negative according as the yield of variety was below or above the average check yield (28).

The disadvantages of these methods far outweighed their advantages. In the first place, comparisons could only be made through the appropriate check variety instead of directly. Further, the yields were entirely hypothetical; they represented nothing real. Most serious of all is the objection that after correcting, one has no criterion of the errors of correction. It is far from satisfactory to try and reduce one source of error by introducing yet another, even though the expectation is that the second error will normally be smaller.

Gradually the concept of replication of all varieties made its way into the technique. Pritchard (29) combined the system of alternate checks with a replication of other varieties as high as tenfold. Such a plan besides being wasteful of space takes

126

no account of the fact that the mean check yield from which all corrections and comparisons derive will be estimated with far greater accuracy than the rest of the experimental yields.

When statistical formulae were applied to results from the check plot method, Kiesselbach (30) plainly showed its deficiencies. He investigated the question of optimum spacing of checks using checks alternately, every second, and every third plot.

He found that there was much variation in his calculated errors, and that checks every third plot gave lower errors than when closer spacing was adopted. He concluded that these anomalous results invalidated the method, and made a strong plea for replication as the mainstay of experimental accuracy.

THE ROD ROW METHOD

The multiplicity of experimental plots led to an exploration of further means of saving time and labour in the collection of data. Experiments, chiefly associated with the name of Arny, were made to test the efficacy of sampling small areas on standard plots, instead of harvesting whole plot areas. The result was a development of the rod row method in all its various forms (Arny and Garber, (31) Arny and Steinmetz (32), Hayes and Arny (33)).

Arny and Garber took regularly arranged rows, one rod in length, within each plot. They had nine in all, and considered them in groups of 9, 5 and 4. They assessed the coefficient of variability of the data resulting from these groupings, and concluded that the nine row sample provided a sufficiently accurate estimate of yield. Later, using square yard samples, Arny and Steinmetz compared the error of yield calculated from a few combined units with that of the main 1/10 acre plots. Although they found it greater for the square yard aggregates, they presumed that by increasing the number of units in the aggregates they could ultimately obtain errors smaller than those from the whole plots. "Student" (34) pointed out the grossly fallacious reasoning employed in this argument, for, reduced to its logical conclusion, it meant that when all but a fraction of the plot was used the errors would be many times less than using the plot itself. Arny and Steinmetz had assumed that increase of size of plot would reduce error in the proportion of 1/\sqrt{n} where n was the number of aggregated units, i.e., that increase in size was as effective as increase in replication. BORDER EFFECTS

Although check plot and rod row methods contained inherently grave defects it would be untrue to suggest that their development contributed little to the advance of experimental technique. They threw into high relief the problems of soil heterogeneity, plot shape and plot size, and if for a time no satisfactory synthesis could be built up from the manifold analytical data they produced, still they played an important part in preparing the way. One important contribution was the study of border effects. The general acceptance of small plots made the unrepresentative character of the outside rows a serious matter. With varietal grain trials, overshadowing of short

138

strawed crops by long ones proved to be a source of interference, and the greater root range at the edge of uncropped paths sometimes gave certain varieties special advantages. Arny and Hayes (38) found that by discarding two border rows the rank of their varietal yields was in some cases changed significantly. Arny (39) adopted the system of cropping the alleys, without however including those rows in yield measurements. Where winter wheat was cropped with alleys of spring wheat, the effect was satisfactory, but not when oats or barley was used as the dividing crop. The only satisfactory way of avoiding border effect whether from uncropped alleys, or different rooting capacity of alley crop and main crops was to discard outside rows. In many experiments this meant planting as many guard rows as yield rows. Arny (40) recommended sacrificing three rows.

The trouble with border effects was accentuated by the trend of opinion regarding plot shape. Long narrow plots were convenient for management, but gave a disproportionate perimeter for a given area. They were also favourably looked upon from the point of view of error reduction, though there was some conflict of evidence. Love (41) Spragg (42) and Summerby (43) in their researches into technique supported long plots. Day (44) reached the heart of the problem when he brought in the consideration of the direction of the fertility slope. He preferred a square plot to a long one having its greatest dimension in the direction of least soil variation, and only recommended the strip plot where the trial plots lay side by side in the direction of least fertility gradient. Summerby's (loc. cit.) decision that length decreased error more than increase in width is placed under suspicion because he omitted any reference to the fertility slopes he was dealing with. Later with the advantage of a better statistical technique Christidis (45) showed that theoretical consideration in favour of long plots was fully borne out by practical examples, but he did not consider the complications of border effects.

There is much repetition in the American work but its cumulative effect was to emphasise the importance of replication of small plots. This brought in its wake other sources of error not recognised at first but subsequently brought to light and remedied. The fact of soil heterogeneity was readily admitted, and overshadowed every other consideration, but though Mercer

139

and Hall had pointed the way, the technique that had grown out of their work in the second decade of the century was satisfactory neither to the statistician nor the experimentalist.

THE MODERN STATISTICAL PERIOD

The modern statistical period does not fall neatly into a clearly defined section of time. In sketching out the chronological sequence of events, some of these have only a temporary significance, whilst others, not at the time fully appreciated, fall into a truer perspective later. This has been so in the realm of experimental technique, so that in studying the modern period it is more advantageous to regard the subject from the point of view of the unfolding or fruition of a method, without undue adherence to the actual sequence of events.

STATISTICAL METHODS RELATED TO DESIGN OF EXPERIMENTS

What distinguishes this period from its forerunner is the fact that, previously, statistical conceptions had been applied to field experiments as an accessory. Statistical methods had been elaborated outside the sphere of agricultural science. When use was made of them it was as a last stage in the development of the experimental plan. It might be true to say that no experiment was complete without them, and that every agronomist was expected to make use of them, but the statistical procedure was something apart, a veneer that gave a polish, not something that penetrated to the very core.

The new viewpoint puts statistical procedure in an entirely different place. From the very first stages of an experimental project, the statistical method has to be borne in mind; the mathematical process must fit the data that are to be gathered, and the experimental design must be in accordance with the statistical procedure.

There is an element of danger in this development inasmuch as it can easily lead to statistical method becoming an end in itself. Statistics is an ancillary weapon, but under the new auspices it has become a weapon that has a rightful place in a permanent armoury.

The modern viewpoint then demands three things; a valid statistical procedure, a design that conforms to this procedure; and a criterion of significance of results as a basis of interpretation. It is remarkable that at the time of Mercer and Hall's

140

fundamental researches these three desiderata were in the main fulfilled by Student.

STUDENT'S METHOD

In 1908 Student had taken the first step towards solving the problem of the application of the theory of probability to small samples (46). What in effect he did was to investigate the standard deviation of a population of which he had only a small sample. The true standard deviation of the population was unknown, but the experimental data provided an estimate of it. "Student" studied the distribution of this estimated standard deviation, and showed that it agreed with the true distribution for those statistics generally used in comparison of means, and Fisher (47) later extended its application to cover the higher statistical derivatives.

Student thus provided a valid statistical procedure and an appropriate criterion of significance (Student's z). In an appendix to Mercer and Hall's paper (loc. cit.), he elaborated a field experimental design that was appropriate to his processes.

The classical example of Student's design is an experiment to test the yield capacities of two varieties of wheat. He postulated that these should be sown in the experimental field in alternate strips, allowing an equal number for each variety. Before harvesting, each of these strips was bisected longitudinally, thus doubling the original number of plots. The two outer half strips were discarded, so that from n original plots he produced n-1 comparisons for the estimate of yield differences, in place of the \frac{n}{2} that were available from the original considerations in favour of long plots was fully borne out by practical plots. Labelling the varieties A and B the sequence of the half strips became A B B A A B B A. Now considering two adjacent half strips as a unit, this arrangement provided that if there were a steady fertility gradient across the strips neither variety would be favoured. The mean difference between A & B is calculable from the sum of the differences between A and B in each unit separately, and the only effect that systematic cross fertility gradient has on mean differences, is what is encountered within the narrow confines of the double half strip unit.

In calculating the errors of the experiment, the differences between varieties A and B for each unit were tabulated and the standard error of the mean difference evaluated from these

141

data. The striking accomplishment of this design was that mean differences, from which fertility or positional advantages had been largely eliminated, were interpreted with reference to an error from which the disturbing influence of these same positional factors had been consciously removed also. This was the first occasion on which the correlation between nearly adjacent plots was turned to advantage by using it to overcome the fertility gradient that had been such a handicap to the American workers.

Advantage was taken of Student's arrangement for comparing two varieties by Beaven (48) who in his half-drill strip method ingeniously arranged that the drilling of the two varieties in the required order should be done by the same drill. The seed hopper was divided into two compartments served by the same number of coulters, one division for each variety. The sowing of the plots thus became a simple continuous operation, since on turning the drill the order of the varieties was reversed. Beaven used this plan in 1920 and it was adopted as a standard procedure by the National Institute of Agricultural Botany in 1923. In the same year it found in America an advocate in Love (49) who remarks that "this method has been little used in interpreting experimental work. Probably it has been overlooked".

It is convenient to mention here that when Student's work received due recognition, the statistical processes used in his original exposition were frequently divorced from the niceties of his design. The statistical calculation became known as "Student's method" which is somewhat of a misnomer. With popularity came misconception, examples of which will be discussed later.

THE ANALYSIS OF VARIANCE

For a single comparison the "method" is simplicity itself, but in applying it to trials involving a relatively large number of comparisons the complexity is vastly increased. Student (34) extended his investigations to a trial of eight varieties replicated twentyfold in a regular chess board arrangement, and, in order to derive an error common to the whole experiment, he estimated the error of the mean difference for every combination of possible pairs, 28 in all. In seeking to simplify the arithmetical calculation he divided the variation encountered within the experiment

142

into a number of parts due respectively to differences in variety, differences in position of his replicated groups of varieties on variable blocks of land, and finally casual variation or error.

At the same time, and independently, Fisher had seen the possibilities of subdividing variation in the same way. The simplicity of Fisher's formula and working have resulted in his method superseding Student's, so that consideration can be confined to the former's statement of the problem.

The Analysis of Variance, to give it the name that Fisher suggested, postulates that if a number of data are arranged in a number of classes containing an equal number of observations in each class, then the total variance about the general mean is divisible into two parts; that which is represented by sums of squares of deviations of individuals in the groups from their own group mean, and that represented by the deviations of the group means from the general mean.

If there are n^1 classes and each has in it k individuals then from the point of view of sums of squares of deviations the following identity holds good (50).

The advantages of this method are many and varied. First in order of importance from the point of view of field experiments is the control of local fertility differences. The arrangement of chess board experiments into blocks each containing a member of all varieties or treatments to be tested, makes ipso facto a distinction between the degree of soil variation to which varieties in the same block or in different blocks is subject. Consequently, by this method which was at one time commonly used, mean differences, into which block variation does not enter, are judged by reference to an error into which block variation does enter. By taking precautions to plan the experimental layout and the statistical procedure in concert, this anomaly can be avoided. The resultant gain in precision when gross block variance is eliminated before making the estimate of error has been demonstrated by Eden and Fisher (53). In their experiments, examples were obtained of a more than fourfold increase in precision.

The analysis of variance, used with adequately designed experiments, provides another advantage. When systematic variance due to treatment and position have been eliminated, the remaining variance contributions between parallel plots can be pooled irrespective of the treatment or block from which they ultimately derive. This gives a greater number of degrees of freedom for error estimation, and hence increased accuracy; and it provides one error figure relevant to any treatment or varietal comparison included in the experimental design.

MISAPPLICATIONS OF STUDENT'S METHOD

In America particularly, there grew up a tendency to believe that Student's method was applicable to all types of data and that the interpretation of field experiments could be based entirely upon examination in that way. This rigid adherence to the method still holds in some modern experiments, and, with the parallel process of the analysis of variance at our disposal, it is possible to point out and resolve some of the difficulties that have cropped up.

Salmon's (57) criticism of the method as used in America is acute and generally reasonable, though his papers on the whole give the impression that he deprecates statistical analyses in general. Arguing from single plots devoted to two different treatments over a period of years, he was the first to suggest that "Student's method" often gave an interpretation in conflict with common-sense. Thus a series of A-B differences might all be positive, and yet because of their marked variability, the

147

variance of the difference might be high enough to discount the reality of the mean difference. The trouble in such cases is that the variance so estimated may be due either to random causes or to differential response of treatments in a number of different years. In the absence of a more complete analysis, to which in any case a single plot experiment does not lend itself, the method must often give negative results, not because it is faulty, but because it is not designed to answer the detailed question that has sometimes been asked of it. In a replicated experiment it would be possible to compare this differential variance (or interaction) between season and treatment with casual errors. In many cases the result is negative because, admitting that seasonal differences in response may be likely, the tenure of the experiment is not long enough to decide with accuracy whether, on the whole, one treatment turns out better than another. This is a difficulty that the original Rothamsted experiments with their long duration completely avoided. Salmon also pointed out that a significant result obtained by this method could be validly accounted for on the assumption that the tract of land relegated to one of the treatments was intrinsically better than that allotted to the other. Again in the absence of replications the matter is left in doubt.

Salmon dealing with experiments of the single plot per treatment type could give no formal demonstration of his contention, but the point is well illustrated by data from the Dutch East Indies on both rubber and tea experiments. Barclay and Grantham (58) have recently shown that, for rubber, monthly yield differences between two treatments when used as a basis for calculating error by "Student's method" give lower error figures than when the error is derived from an analysis of replicates. They rightly say that the fact that an error figure assessed by a method which gives a lower value than an alternative method, is not necessarily an indication of its validity; but they do not show how the two error figures are related. The misuse of statistical interpretation that has just been cited arose from a failure to distinguish between what may be called time or seasonal errors, and those due to soil heterogeneity. USE OF KIKUYU GRASS IN COMBATING SOIL EROSION*

IN the great anti-soil erosion campaign, the construction of dams, to serve as drinking places for stock, is an extremely important measure, since it strikes at the very root of the evil, viz., the tramping out of the veld. The efficacy of a dam, of course, depends, amongst other things, upon how long it will be able to store sufficient water to serve as a drinking place, and in this connection silt plays an important part, especially in those areas of the country which are subject to erosion and where the need for a greater number of drinking places is most felt. In such areas flood waters usually carry a high percentage of silt, as a result of which the silting up of dams becomes a serious problem. Yet on the other hand, it is desirable to have dongas silted up. That it is possible to make the dongas silt up at comparatively small cost, without getting the same result in the dams, has been demonstrated in a convincing manner by Mr. W. C. Kritzinger, of Glen Roy, P. O. Commadagga, whose farm is situated in the Karroo area, Somerset East district, where the mean annual rainfall is 15 inches. Soil erosion is prevalent in that area, and the water carries a high percentage of silt.

For years past there has been a sloop, 10 feet deep and 25 feet wide, in the valley of Glen Roy, and Mr. Kritzinger decided to construct a dam across it in order to store water for irrigation purposes. The dam was built in the form of a wide U, the wall being 300 yards long and 23 feet high. It was soon realized that unless steps were taken to check silting up of the dam, it would take only a few years before it would be completely silted up. Mr. Kritzinger then set to work as follows: On the up-stream side, a few yards from the spot where the sloop enters the dam, he erected a barrier of wire-netting across the sloop. The barrier consisted of heavy wooden posts, planted about 18 in. deep and anchored to large stones some 2 feet deep, as terminals, with standards 5 feet apart and also thoroughly anchored in between; 6 feet of the wire-netting was placed on the up-stream side of the bed of the sloop, and firmly weighted down with stones. Where the sloop was larger more wire was placed on the bed, and it was more firmly weighted down. As soon as this barrier caught silt, which happened after the first rain, Kikuyu grass was planted there, and another barrier was erected in the same way about 50 yards higher up the sloop. Kikuyu grass was also planted here after the first silt deposit. In this way Mr. Kritzinger worked further and further up the valley, and his results exceeded all expectations.

Although an average annual rainfall of 15 inches is too small under ordinary circumstances for the successful establishment of Kikuyu grass, the conditions in this scheme were very favourable for the grass, since it was

PRACTICAL DUCK-KEEPING*

IN Queensland, ducks are chiefly kept for table purposes, although quite a number of small flocks are kept for egg-production. There are very few specialised duck farms; the usual practice is to keep a flock of ducks as a farm sideline. The market for table birds is usually kept fairly well supplied from existing sources, high values are therefore not regular. Reasonably high prices are, however, obtainable when the demand is firming for the Christmas trade. This fact indicates the necessity for the table. The keeping of ducks for egg-production is not practised extensively in this State; this is possibly due to the unpopularity of the duck egg, making it somewhat difficult to market. It is all a question of taste, for a duck egg is equal to a hen egg in food value, and provided the birds are fed on good wholesome food and kept under strict sanitary conditions, it would be fairly difficult to distinguish any difference in general quality. Ducks are more prolific layers, have a longer profitable life, are more easily reared and are freer from disease than other poultry.

The foremost breed is the Muscovy; this bird is essentially a table bird, and may be found all over the State. The Muscovy is distinct from all other breeds of ducks and will always remain distinct, for if this breed is crossed with any other breed of ducks the progeny will be mule ducks.

HOUSING

The mild climatic conditions in Queensland obviate the necessity for the construction of elaborate or costly houses for the accommodation of ducks. That does not mean that ducks can be herded profitably in any class of a house. Houses should be built similar in design to ordinary poultry houses, a lean-to building facing north or north-east, open-fronted, with a ventilation space at the top of the back wall. Buildings so constructed will afford the ducks most protection against prevailing winds and rains whilst at the same time the sun's rays penetrate into the house.

Construction.—The building need not be deeper than 5 feet, and the roof could be 6 feet high at the front and 5 feet high at the back, and a ventilation space of 3 inches at the top of the back wall would be satisfactory. In estimating the size of the building, allow 2 square feet of floor space for each duck; thus a building 10 feet long and 5 feet deep will accommodate twenty-five ducks. The best materials for the construction of duck-houses is sawn hardwood and galvanised corrugated iron. Some persons may desire to make use of bush saplings so as to have cheaply constructed buildings; this may be done, but it is essential to have an iron roof. It may be thought that as ducks usually camp out in the open it is unnecessary to have an iron roof, but this is absolutely essential for one of the most important factors in the housing of ducks is a dry floor.

155

Floors.—It is essential for the floor of the house to be dry at all times, a damp or wet floor in a duck house may cause many deaths among the flock, while practically the whole flock will receive a check in growth or production. To ensure dry floors, build up the floor at least 4 inches above the level of the surrounding land; also excavate drains on the highest side of the house, so as to carry away storm water. Concrete floors are best, but an earth floor that has been tamped down fairly hard will be satisfactory. To facilitate cleaning, cover the floor with coarse sand or a litter of hay, grass or straw. The litter will act as a bedding for the ducks. Nests should be provided. These may be placed on the floor against the walls.

BREEDING

It will be found most profitable to adopt the same breeding season for light-breed ducks as generally adopted for other poultry—namely, June to September. Ducks hatched during these months will commence laying when egg values are high, and continue for about twelve months before moulting. Heavy breeds hatched during June, July and mid-August, will be more profitable, as they can be marketed in prime condition for the Christmas trade. The breeding of heavy breeds may be continued throughout the year, provided that a constant supply of cheap suitable foodstuffs is available.

Selection and Mating.—Care must be exercised in the selection of breeding stock. Special attention must be given to type and size. A careful study of the description of the breed is necessary, so as to be able to select birds that are reasonably true to type. Ducks have a tendency to deteriorate very rapidly in size; therefore, it is essential to maintain size of body when selecting breeding birds. In this regard, it is good policy to weigh the birds before placing them in the breeding pen. Defective ducks should not be used for breeding purposes. In mating, the number of females to mate with each male varies with the age of the male, size of run, whether the birds have access to a swimming pool, and the breed. On an average, mate between six and eight females with each light-breed male, and from four to six females with each heavy-breed male. The number of females may be increased if the male is young and very vigorous. Ducks may be safely bred from until they are three or four years old.

MANAGEMENT

Ducks should be kept apart from fowls, as they are greedy feeders and often prevent the fowls from obtaining sufficient food. Their way of feeding is also slightly different. Apart from these factors, ducks make the drinking water unsuitable for poultry. A swimming pool is not a necessity, but where ducks have access to a pool, they keep in better health, their plumage is cleaner, and they are more free from external parasites. In addition, a higher degree of fertility results if breeding birds have access to a swimming pool. As the duck usually lays in the night or early morning, it is necessary to confine them to the run or house until about 9 a.m., otherwise many eggs may be laid in the pool.

156

Ducks must have a constant supply of clean, cool, fresh water, and when confined during the night water must be supplied. The water vessel should be deep enough for the duck to submerge its head in the water.

Ducks are naturally clean in their habits, but if confined in a small enclosure not properly drained, filthy conditions will result. Therefore, strict sanitation should be practised.

When kept in large numbers, ducks, particularly Indian Runners, are very excitable and easily frightened, and if frightened they are very liable to go into a partial moult.

INCUBATION

It is the usual practice not to set the first hatch of eggs laid by a duck, these being often infertile; also, if fertile, weak ducklings usually result from such eggs.

The period of incubation is 28 days for all breeds with the exception of Muscovy eggs. These take 35 days to hatch. The incubation of duck eggs is best done with ducks. If broody hens are used, it will be necessary to sprinkle the eggs with water regularly. Also sprinkle water on the ground close to the nest, for when the hen comes off she will dust bath, and her feathers will be moistened when she returns to the nest. The duck, however, will moisten her feathers sufficiently before returning to the nest. With artificial incubation, the temperature should be about 1 degree lower than that for hen eggs—namely 102 degrees. After setting, the eggs should not be disturbed for 48 hours. After this period, they should be turned twice daily, and cooled daily. Each time the eggs are turned, before being returned to the machine, they should be sprinkled with warm water. This sprinkling is essential because the eggs require a lot of moisture. Test, and remove all infertile eggs. Do not open the machine after the ducklings commence chipping until the hatch is complete. Ducklings take longer to break out of the shell than chickens.

REARING

Ducklings are very hardy, and easy to rear, therefore rearing may be done by artificial methods. Any type of a simple brooder that will permit of water being kept within access of the ducks will prove satisfactory. For instance, a frame with four legs about 6 inches high to which is tacked a piece of hessian from which flannels hang to within an inch of the ground will give results. First place ample straw on the floor, put down the brooder; the ducklings should be kept under the brooder the first day without food or water. To confine them, use inch netting close up all around the brooder. By adopting this practice they will know where to go when feeling cold. The following night they may be allowed 8 or 10 inches around the brooder, and in this space place water vessels. After about a week, it will be necessary to confine them to the brooder. After about three weeks the brooder may be removed, providing that ample straw is placed in the shed. One important point must not be overlooked, and that is ground draughts must be prevented. Every day the straw should be forked up and, if necessary, replaced with clean, dry

157

straw. Ducklings must not be crowded; best results will be obtained by rearing ducklings in small units. When about four weeks old they may be placed out in houses for they do not then require much attention apart from plenty of food and water. Ducklings should be protected from the hot sun until they are well feathered on the head and neck; this is more important with Indian Runners than other breeds. Therefore, the rearing pens should have a number of shade trees growing in them; if not, artificial shade should be provided.

FEEDING

Ducklings require no food for 48 hours after hatching. During this period they could be supplied with water, coarse sand, and charcoal or wood ashes. A mash that will give good results if fed from the first meal until they are about four weeks old is prepared by mixing together pollard, 10 lb.; maizemeal, 8 lb.; dried buttermilk, 2 lb.; bonemeal, \frac{1}{2} lb.; and fine salt, 2 oz. If these ingredients are mixed together the amount for each meal may be moistened as required. If available, 3 lb. of curds would replace the dried buttermilk, thus cheapening the ration. Skim milk is excellent for ducklings; it can be used to moisten the mash, but do not give it in the form of a drink. If there is ample milk available, allow it to curd and strain off the whey, then feed the curds. Imitate nature as far as possible by giving several small meals daily to young ducklings. A little and often is a good motto to adopt. After four weeks of age, they may be fed on a similar ration to the mature ducks. When mature it is only necessary to give them three meals daily, supplying as much food as the ducklings can consume in about half an hour. Be sure they have a big evening meal.

A ration that will give excellent results for the feeding of mature ducks is comprised of the following ingredients: Pollard, 55 lb.; bran, 25 lb.; maizemeal, 10 lb.; meatmeal, 10 lb., bonemeal, 1 lb.; and fine salt, 1 lb.; to which may be added 25 per cent. of cooked vegetables or chaffed green stuff. The salt should be mixed in the liquid first, so as to ensure a thorough incorporation in the mixture. At least two meals should be given daily, but with mature birds a small meal of whole maize may be fed in addition to the mash.

For the fattening of ducks consideration must be given to the availability of cheap foodstuffs, which are often obtainable in the form of potatoes, pumpkins, and other vegetables; these should be boiled and may be added to the mash upwards to 40 per cent. of the bulk. Chaffed green stuff should be included, but do not use much green stuff when making use of a large proportion to other cheap foodstuffs, otherwise the mash may be too bulky.

Always keep a supply of shell grit and coarse sand in receptacles before the birds.

WATER

Water is one of the biggest factors in successful duck-keeping; they must always have access to ample, clean, cool fresh drinking water. The water vessels or pool should be sufficiently deep to permit the ducks to

158

submerge their heads. The water vessel should be kept under a shade tree or protected from the sun by providing artificial shade. In rearing ducklings it is a good plan to put a number of stones in the water vessels; this prevents the ducklings swimming and wasting the water.

Water vessels should be constructed so the ducklings can get out easily in the event of their swimming in the vessels, otherwise they may drown through cramp. This cramping is more likely to occur during cold weather.

COMMON TROUBLES

As stated previously, ducklings are hardy and easily reared, but losses will occur if they are neglected. The most common troubles are chills and staggers.

Chills.—Symptoms—Watery eyes and nostrils. Cause—Wet or damp sleeping quarters.

Remedy.—Keeping the floors dry is the most important point. The drinking water may be slightly coloured with permanganate of potash, and changed several times daily.

Staggers.—Symptoms—Ducklings stagger about and fall on their backs before dying. Cause—Lack of water. When water is supplied after there has been a shortage, the ducklings gorge themselves, bringing about this condition.

Remedy.—Keep a constant supply of drinking water before the ducklings.

ANIMAL HUSBANDRY IN CEYLON

The Chairman called upon Mr. Crawford to read his paper on the subject of animal husbandry in Ceylon.

Mr. Crawford said that there was, without doubt, a general tendency in Ceylon to consider animal husbandry as of minor importance in the agricultural economy of the Island. In a large degree this was probably due to attention being focussed on the four great major crops of Ceylon, namely, tea, rubber, coconut and paddy. There was a tendency to lose sight of the fact that animals do play a part in the production of even the major products. He would endeavour to show that livestock, even under present conditions, were of importance to all agriculturists, no matter in what crop they were chiefly interested. He said he would also attempt to show that it was possible, and with advantage, for livestock to play a more important part. First of all he desired to give them some idea of the economic importance of livestock and their products at the present time, but one immediately encountered the initial difficulty of the absence of reliable statistics. In the case of major agricultural products it was easy to obtain figures, the areas devoted to their cultivation were tabulated, market prices were quoted daily, estimates of production made from time to time and export figures frequently published. In the case of livestock he said such figures were largely lacking, but the following figures would serve the purpose of showing that livestock and their products had a greater value in the aggregate than was generally realised.

The value of hides would be much greater if they were free from brandmarks; he had been informed that they would fetch almost three times their present value were this the case. There were no figures for eggs and poultry sold locally but a slight indication of the value of the table poultry produced could be gathered from the fact that in 1929 the imports were valued at over three lakhs of rupees and although such imports had been prohibited in the past few years, on account of the introduction of disease, the local supply had proved fully capable of meeting the demand without any increase in price. These few figures would help to give some idea of local production, the greater part of which was village produce but in spite of the very considerable local production, it was still necessary to import such livestock products as tinned milk, butter, cheese, eggs and bacon, the total annual value of which was about Rs. 2,050,000. It was satisfactory to note, he said, that there had been a marked decline in the import of eggs during the last few years; from a total of 15,000,000 eggs valued at Rs. 500,000 in 1929, to 7,000,000 valued at just over Rs. 200,000 in 1933. The figures for 1934 would probably be less on account of the increased duty. These figures indicated that local production represented a large annual cash turnover and further that there was still margin for increased local production.

Mr. Crawford then went on to outline the part played by animal husbandry in the agricultural economy of Ceylon and its value in the nutrition of the population. He pointed out that for the purpose of paddy cultivation cattle and buffaloes were a vital necessity. It was surprising to what a small extent cattle manure was used for paddy cultivation; cattle and buffaloes were found in almost all paddy growing areas, yet, with the exception of the Jaffna district the dung was rarely collected for purposes of manuring. There was a simple method of preparing pen-manure in which weeds, hedge loppings, etc. were collected during the day and spread in a layer on the floor of a pit into which cattle were driven for the night. They trampled the vegetable material and it became mixed with their dung and urine, and in this way considerable quantities of valuable manure could be prepared at a low cost. He considered that the general adoption of this practice by paddy cultivators would assist in increasing the yield of paddy, the lowness of which was so frequently deplored in Ceylon.

In the coconut industry cattle played a much more important part than they did in the case of tea and rubber, especially for transport, purposes of cultivation, and manuring, which in the case of coconuts was usual by tethering the animals round the palms. For purposes of utilising the manure and of adding material in bulk, better results were obtainable from the use of pen-manure. He considered that coconut estates would benefit considerably by combining animal husbandry with coconut production to a much greater extent than was done at present. On several estates he had seen poultry farming practised with a considerable measure of success. Poultry manure appeared to be one of the best manures for coconuts, and sheep and goat manure were also of great value, as he had seen on several estates. The present low price of coconuts made it difficult for the planter to purchase artificial manures, so that the possibilities of cattle, goat and poultry manure were worthy of consideration.

In the case of tea estates, livestock now played a small part compared with some years ago, when, prior to the development of motor lorry transport, all the carting was done by bullock carts and most estates had large cattle sheds for housing their bulls. The manure produced was a valuable asset to the estate. Was it not possible, he asked, that cattle manure could be advantageously used more extensively than it was at present? Unless fresh blood were introduced in the near future, the standard of these cattle in the up-country districts would deteriorate greatly, and already dairymen in Colombo were finding it difficult to get cows of the standard they did a few years ago. He said that, as all were aware, the diet of the greater part of the population of Ceylon was largely vegetarian, consisting of cereals, such as rice or kurukkan, vegetables, condiments and a little dried or salted fish. Evidence had accumulated from all parts of the world, of the great importance in the diet, especially of infants and young children, of such foodstuffs as milk, ghee, eggs and meat. In his lecture Dr. Nicholls had indicated that the remedy was the greater use of milk, ghee, eggs, animal fats, etc., but was rather pessimistic as to whether the poorer classes in Ceylon could afford to purchase these foodstuffs. Cattle were numerous in nearly all Ceylon villages, yet it was exceptional to see a cow being milked. A common reason given for this was that the Ceylon cow gave so little milk that it was not worth milking. Mr. Crawford said he could not accept this explanation; it was true that the village cow was too low a milk yielder to be of use to the dairyman, but most of them would give two or three pints if given some extra feeding and many would give up to four, five or six pints which would be a sufficient quantity for most families.

MEANS OF OBTAINING A BETTER PRICE
FOR LOCALLY GROWN PADDY

The Chairman called upon Mudaliyar N. Wickremaratne to speak on the resolution that he wished to propose with regard to locally grown paddy.

Mudaliyar Wickremaratne said that no one would gainsay the fact that food and clothing were the most essential necessaries of the life of a nation.

178

He considered that both these requirements could be met in Ceylon by local production, and that it was a reflection on them that they had to depend on foreign countries for these needs. He wished to discuss the necessity for growing their own supply of paddy, which was the staple food of the people, and for finding means to make paddy cultivation a remunerative industry. The paddy question, he indicated, had been discussed on many occasions and he himself had been connected with it for nearly thirty years. No solution had been arrived at and Ceylon still imported from India too large a proportion of her requirements. Those who remembered the threatened food-shortage in 1919-1920, would realise the seriousness of a similar situation which they might have to face in the near future, particularly when their money-producing industries were suffering from depression. During the 1919-1920 rice crisis even the well-to-do were not able to procure rice. Fortunately the North-Central, North-Western, and Eastern Provinces which had some surplus paddy at the time, came to the rescue to some extent. Today, however, even these Provinces were at the door of famine as the failure of one crop had brought about a serious shortage of seed-paddy and foodstuffs, clearly indicating that the paddy situation was now worse than it previously was.

Referring then to records contained in ancient chronicles of great repute, the Mahawansa, the Rajarawaliya and the Pujewaliya, Mudaliyar Wickremaratne quoted instances of how the ancient rulers of Ceylon had encouraged the production of supplies of rice which were even greater than the requirements of the people. This, he said, was evidence that sufficient rice for their consumption could be grown in Ceylon if it were set about in the right way.

As recently as the 16th, 17th and 18th centuries, when the seaboard of the Island were in the hands of the foreigner, the country had sufficient food to maintain its own people, and during the wars with the Portuguese, the Dutch and the English it was the custom of the country to supply the requirements of the King's Army when they passed through a village. With the advent of these nations an import trade of some produce or other began, to the gradual detriment of local rice production. In the beginning it was the surplus of cinnamon and arecanuts that was exported, but after the British occupation attention was given to crops in use in European countries and the indigenous crops, such as paddy, were neglected. The growing of cinnamon, coffee, citronella, cinchona, cacao, tea, rubber and coconuts was taken up one after the other and all the energy of the country was employed to this end. But the end had now been reached and he could not foresee that any other crop would arise in which the production for export could succeed any of these previous crops. This being the case he considered it was necessary that they should now turn their attention to a crop which was in great demand locally. Paddy would not only supply an essential need, but would also enable them to retain in the country a large sum of money which they could ill-afford to allow to go out of the Island.

Some efforts in this direction had been made by some of the more sympathetic administrators. Sir Henry Ward, he said, was the first Governor who gave his attention to the resuscitation of paddy cultivation by his devoted interest in the restoration of ancient tanks. Later, Sir

179

Henry Blake established the Ceylon Agricultural Society for the advancement of village agriculture, primarily the cultivation of paddy. The Department of Agriculture then came into existence as a result of the activities of the above Society, and its first Director in his new scheme of organisation instituted a branch to give attention to the important question of paddy cultivation and allied products, entrusting this to that experienced officer Mr. C. Drieberg. Unfortunately he left the Island before he could do anything much with his wonderful enthusiasm. He was succeeded by that able and most energetic Director, Mr. F. A. Stockdale, as a result of whose interest in paddy cultivation and village agriculture Sir Hugh Clifford expounded his new land policy at a meeting of the former Board of Agriculture. As an outcome of this, he believed, the energetic and resourceful Minister of Agriculture and Lands was able to launch his land policy and Land Bill, an action that would be a landmark in the history of Ceylon. A further step taken by Mr. Stockdale had been the appointment of a number of paddy Sub-Committees under the Board of Agriculture to make investigations into the question of paddy cultivation. These Committees made very useful reports but in the meantime Mr. Stockdale left the Island. His successors placed these reports before the Board of Agriculture which made a series of recommendations to Government which appointed a Commission. This Commission never made a report so that the efforts made in this matter ceased to produce any conclusive results. The reports of the Sub-Committees contained numerous valuable suggestions and it was a pity they were not given the more careful attention that they should have received.

Continuing, Mudaliyar Wickremaratne indicated that the paddy industry today had sunk to the lowest level as regards its market value, so much so that no agriculturist except the poor goiya looked at it with any interest or sympathy. The question was often asked, whether it paid, but if it did not, he said, it must be made to pay. He was of the opinion that paddy cultivation was a necessity and not a matter of rupees and cents. It was time that the planter and the Government joined the goiya in making a supreme effort to grow a sufficient rice supply to meet their requirements. The planter did not take to paddy cultivation as it did not give a return for his outlay; the goiya could not produce a surplus as it did not give him a living wage. To overcome this extraordinary situation, he said, they had to look to the Government, as it was the Government alone who could help them in their dire need. A benevolent and sympathetic Government should look ahead and avoid calamities and his suggestion was that it should take steps to put forward a special effort to encourage paddy cultivation. While everything possible was being done today as regards restoring irrigation works, improving methods of cultivation, and the improvement of seed selection, Government should subsidise paddy cultivation for a number of years, at least until they could grow a substantial part of their own requirements and take measures against foreign competition. What he ventured to suggest was that a minimum uniform price per bushel be fixed and so control the price of rice in the Island as a whole. The necessary expenditure could be met from the duties now levied on imported rice or out of the general revenue.

The control of rice might keep the price at a level which would not allow the estates, which were large buyers of rice, to suffer. In support of his suggestion he quoted from evidence given by the Earl of Crawford and Mr. F. D. In concluding, he requested the Director of Agriculture to consider whether the Divisional Agricultural Officers and their Agricultural Instructors could be employed to take a survey of every cultivable paddy land in their respective areas and to systematically bring such lands under cultivation; also to take steps to establish Central Depôts for storing surplus paddy in every area so as to protect the country from seasonal slumps.

Mudaliyar Wickremaratne then proposed the following resolution with the suggestion that it be transmitted to the Minister for Agriculture and Lands :

"That in order to increase the production of paddy for the national security of the people of this Island and in order to induce the general agricultural communities to take to paddy cultivation, a guaranteed uniform minimum price for paddy should be fixed by Government."

The Chairman in reply to a query by Mr. Rolf Smerdon, agreed that the suggestion put forward by Mudaliyar Wickremaratne appeared to be covered by the motion of Mr. Subramaniam that was now before the State Council.

Mr. C. Arulambalam, in seconding the resolution, stated that during the 1919-1920 period when the price of paddy went up to six rupees, large tracts of jungle land were taken up for paddy cultivation in the Punakari district, but when the price of paddy fell the land was abandoned. He considered that a minimum price was the only way of dealing with the situation.

Mr. Wace de Niese observed that had the ancients mentioned by Mudaliyar Wickremaratne known of tea, rubber and coconuts they would have abandoned paddy cultivation for the better dividends yielded by those crops. The Ministry were doing all they could to help the industry but paddy cultivation had never paid and that was the reason why the Sinhalese worked on tea and rubber estates. A minimum price, he said, would have to be more than the cultivator got today. He thought that as it was the Ministry was spending far too much. Before the country could produce all its requirements of rice, hospitals should be opened and the malaria question tackled.

Mudaliyar Wickremaratne said that if muttusamba rice could be sold at five rupees per bushel he did not see why a better kind of village grown rice should be supplied at two rupees per bushel. That was why he considered that Government should fix a minimum price and control the supply of rice. There was a large surplus and he did not see why the

DEPARTMENTAL NOTES

FIELD DAY AT LABUDUWA FARM, GALLE

G. HARBORD. DIP. AGRIC. (WYE)

DIVISIONAL AGRICULTURAL OFFICER, SOUTHERN, GALLE

A'field day' was organised at the Central Agricultural Station, Labuduwa, Galle on Monday, January 21st, 1935, which drew large numbers of interested agriculturists to the Farm throughout the day.

In addition to large contingents of the peasantry, the visitors included representatives of the Divisional Agricultural Associations of the six Pattus of the Galle District, and many Chairmen and members of Village Committees besides members of Co-operative Societies.

Quite a number of minor headmen from all Pattus, and the Chief Headmen of Talpe Pattu also attended.

PADDY

The harvesting of the Maha crop of Suduheenati pure-line paddy was being carried out—and visitors were thus able to watch the performance of various labour-saving devices.

(a) A Japanese hand-threshing machine. The general impression was that this ingenious machine could not compare in efficiency and output with the local method of threshing—the man-treading process.

(b) A Winnowing Machine.—The visitors were much impressed with the speed in operation and the superior sample of paddy turned out by this machine which has an output of 100 bushels per day.

It was explained that a similar machine was on loan at Babarenda South (Wellaboda Pattu, Matara) and was being successfully worked on a co-operative basis.

(c) A Malay Rice hulling machine. Great interest was shown in this machine.

The general opinion was that, both in performance and output, it showed great superiority over the local pounding methods, and many visitors expressed a desire to procure machines of this type.

(d) Light Mould-board ploughs. (Victory, Meston, Arumugan, Molegode) and also Burmese harrow. Ploughing demonstrations were carried out showing the superiority of the Victory plough for high-land weedy fields; the Arumugam plough for general work on mud lands and also the general utility of the Burmese harrow—provided capable draught cattle are available.

(e) Numerous field-plots of Balamawi selections. It was explained that the final selection was now being carried out of a Pure-line for use in the Weligama tracts of Matara District.

DAIRY

The herd of 26 animals of the Montgomery breed (the best Indian milking breed) was inspected, and the visitors saw for themselves the feeding methods which were practised—including the provision of regulated concentrates, green fodder, and ample free grazing.

They also gained an insight into the system adopted for the production of pure clean milk.

It was explained that bull calves were being raised for sale to local dairy owners for stud purposes.

COFFEE

Considerable interest was shown by many visitors in the plantation where four varieties of the Robusta type are being cultivated under Leucaena glauca, as the most suitable form of shade.

TEA

The two and four year-old plantations grown on the contour system of planting and provided with ample shade trees (grevillea, gliricidia and albizzia) were critically examined; favourable comment was made as regards the development of bushes resulting from the pruning system etc.

FRUIT SECTION

The plantation of Pineapple (Kew), Sapodilla (grafted), Mangosteen, Orange (Washington Navel), and miscellaneous fruit trees, was visited.

Methods of controlling plant pests and diseases by the use of spraying machines was demonstrated.

PLANT SUPPLY NURSERIES

The nurseries of coffee, fruit plants, kapok, tea and shade trees, established to provide planting material for issue to general agriculturists and peasant proprietors, were inspected.

FODDER GRASS PLOTS

The areas reserved for the production of green fodder for stall-feeding to the dairy herd, interested the visitors, the majority of whom were cattle owners. They were able to compare crops of Guinea grass, Napier grass and Mauritius grass being raised in soil conditions best suited for their growth.

The wonderful response of these crops to applications of farmyard manure was stressed.

COMPOST PITS

(Size of pit: 8×8×8 ft.) The pits were visited and the contents examined.

The value of conserving all forms of vegetable refuse for the manufacture of compost—a cheap, but beneficial, form of manure—was clearly demonstrated.

In the northern half of Ceylon, February rainfall was generally below average, except along the north-west coast and in the Jaffna Peninsula. South of the line joining Puttalam and Batticaloa, excesses and deficits were irregularly distributed, and were usually small. Excesses up to 5 inches were reported from several stations in the north and north-west of the hills, and in the districts to the south and south-east of Puttalam. The south and east of the island, including the southern and eastern slopes of the hills, were generally in deficit.

February averages are small in most parts of the Island, and slight or moderate excess does not usually imply a heavy rainfall. No station reported a monthly total of 10 inches of rain, and only a few districts, chiefly in the south-western low-country adjoining the hills, and on the north-eastern hill slopes, reported over 5 inches for the month. In the northern half of the Island, and the eastern half of the Southern Province, the districts south of Kurunegala, and the Uva plateau, rainfall totals were generally under 2 inches, though very few stations reported no rain at all.

Four stations reported daily falls of 5 inches or over, the highest being 6.30 inches, at Eheliyagoda, on the 17th-18th.

For the first half of the month there was very little rain, the usual northerly gradients and north-easterly winds being experienced. On the 14th there was wide-spread light or moderate rain, while for the next two days the rainfall was comparatively light, and mainly in or near the hills. On the 17th and 18th there were many heavy falls of rain, chiefly in the south-west, as the result of local afternoon or evening thunderstorms. On the 19th the weather moderated, and for the next three days was dry. On the 23rd the weather seemed more unsettled, and there was light or moderate rain in the north and east. On the 24th the rain was wide-spread over the Island, but generally moderate, while the weather then cleared, and very little further rain was reported till the end of the month.

Mean temperatures and humidities, as a rule, showed no marked deviations from normal. Clouding was above average. The barometer was generally a little deficit, while wind strength was above normal at most stations. The direction of the wind was generally NE to NNE.
