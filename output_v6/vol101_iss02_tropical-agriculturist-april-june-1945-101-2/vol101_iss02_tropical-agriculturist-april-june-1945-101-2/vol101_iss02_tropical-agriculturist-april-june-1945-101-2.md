CITRUS CULTIVATION

---

**B**EHIND the stock-scion experiment for citrus that is described elsewhere in this periodical lies the story not merely of the progress of a series of trials but the disturbing failure of a much more fundamental one.

The desirable qualities of citrus fruit led to the expansion of its cultivation from its original home in the tropics even towards temperate regions beyond the frost line. In those unusual surroundings, it acquired a certain mellowness of its desirable qualities which enhanced its popularity and gave an additional impulse to its cultivation. Not least among the circumstances that led to the improvement of its qualities in those temperate regions were the care and attention paid to its culture and selection. Later, the recognition of its high nutritive value, following on the discovery of vitamins, was another contributing factor.

In Ceylon, orange, mandarin and lime trees had led a neglected and precarious existence in backyards, in competition with other trees; and not a fraction of the care that these valuable trees were rightly entitled to was devoted to their development. In the late twenties, however, Ceylon, like some other tropical countries, became suddenly alive to the possibilities of citrus cultivation as an economic crop. Immediately, grafted varieties of diverse kinds were imported from all parts of the world—from California, Florida, Palestine, Australia and South Africa, and planted indiscriminately with little thought of scientific management. Somehow, the idea seems to have existed that, as the tropics were the home of the citrus, any varieties that did well in other climes would do even better in their original home. This was the first experiment—the experiment that failed. Fortunately, very few imported these varieties on a large scale, so that, ultimately, the net loss was distributed among a large number of fruit growers. The results, however, were very disappointing. Many of the imported varieties produced fruit with undesirable qualities, and even the bearing life of most of them did not extend to more than eight years. It took some years, of course, before fruit growers realized, to their dismay, that the importation of foreign varieties did not provide a short cut to success; and before they recognized that the problems of selection, propagation and cultivation had to be worked out *ab initio*, for each horticultural zone in the country. The recognition of this was the beginning of the second experiment—the experiment that is

little by little proving to be a success. Already, the Vavuniya and Bibile areas have come to be regarded as the most suitable for citrus cultivation and the possibilities of North Matale, Middeniya and Embilipitiya are under observation. Selections from local parent trees have given a very useful orange and a mandarin, and it is more than likely that they will be recognized as two strains of outstanding performance in the near future. Cultural practices—different ones for different areas—are gradually getting established. The article now under consideration takes us to the point to which progress has been made in methods of vegetative propagation.

61ORIGINAL ARTICLES.STOCK-SCION INFLUENCE IN CITRUS

A. V. RICHARDS, M.Sc. (Calif.), B.Sc. (Lond.), Dip. Agric. (Cantab.), A.I.C.T.A. (Trinidad),

HORTICULTURAL OFFICER

IN most countries where citrus is grown on an orchard scale the commercial method of propagation is by budding the desired scion variety on a compatible stock which can be easily raised from seed. The seedling stocks are grown in nursery beds and budded almost exclusively by the inverted T method of budding.

Budgrafts propagated vegetatively by this method on genetically uniform stocks will nearly always come true to type and bear earlier than seedling trees of comparable age. They tend to be uniform in growth and are easy to train in the orchard. They will produce fruit of uniform quality, and if they are the progeny of buds selected from inherently high yielding trees they will tend to give uniformly high yields. Their comparatively low spreading habit of growth facilitates spraying, pruning, thinning and other cultural operations in the orchard.

Seedling trees on the other hand tend to grow tall and erect and with few exceptions cannot be depended upon to breed true to type owing to possible cross pollination with other citrus varieties. Plants raised on their own roots as layers and gootees are often planted in village gardens, but the method of raising them is slow and cumbersome.

In the case of a budgraft the scion has the further advantage of being on a root stock which is probably more resistant than the scion itself on its own roots to pests and diseases and to unfavourable soil and weather conditions brought about by drought, frost, excessive rainfall &c. In a compatible stock-scion combination the stock will tend to influence the growth of the scion, its yield and fruit quality, while the scion will tend to have a similar reciprocal effect on the root system of the stock (Hodgson *et al.* 1937).

With most deciduous fruit trees a stock-scion combination which is a failure in one place is generally so in most other places regardless of environmental influence, indicating thereby that the failure is mainly due to physiological and structural differences between the stock and scion. But in the case of citrus the problem of stock-scion incompatibility is more complicated, since a stock-scion combination which does well in one place may be a complete failure in another owing to differences in environment. A striking example of this is the behaviour of the sour orange which makes a good stock for sweet orange on a commercial scale in California, Florida and Palestine but is a failure as a stock for sweet orange in South Africa, Java, and Peshawar, although when left unbudded it makes vigorous growth and crops well [Webber (1926), Toxopeus (1936), Brown (1920)]. The position is well described by Professor V. A. Blackman (1937) in a statement that

5------------------------------------------------

62

“ the success of a graft like that of a marriage may depend not only on the inherent qualities of the two individuals but also on the conditions, favourable or unfavourable, to which they are exposed during their partnership ”.

There are in Ceylon wide regional variations in soil and climate which make it necessary to carry out stock-scion trials with local and imported varieties in order to develop a suitable stock-scion combination for each region. Prior to the war citrus grafts were imported in large numbers mainly from Australia and South Africa and planted in various parts of the Island, but it soon became evident from the performance of these trees that many of the stock-scion combinations were not suited to local conditions. It was felt that some of these varieties were likely to do well if they were budded on local acclimatized stocks.

The experiments which form the subject of this paper were laid down under different soil and climatic conditions in the wet zone at Peradeniya in November, 1936, in the semi-dry zone at Nalanda in February, 1938, and under irrigation in the dry zone at Hingurakgoda in November, 1941, with a view to test the suitability of different stock-scion combinations in regard to their (a) general habit of growth and vigour, (b) yield and quality of fruit, (c) resistance to pests and diseases.

In the grapefruit stock-scion experiment at Peradeniya where the average annual rainfall is 93.5 inches there are four different stocks—Rough lemon (*C. jambhiri* Lush), Sour orange (*C. Aurantium* L), Pummelo (*C. Maxima* Merr.) and sweet orange hybrid (*C. sinensis Hybr.*) budded with four different grapefruit varieties—Walters, Marsh seedless, Triumph and Foster. The soil here is a light loam of low fertility. A preliminary report on this trial has already been published (Richards 1938).

In the grapefruit trial at Hingurakgoda Farm where the average annual rainfall is 78 inches there are two stocks—Rough lemon and sour orange—budded with four varieties, Walters, Marsh seedless, Cecily seedless and Triumph. The experimental area is in a low lying section of the orchard which is subject to water logging during a continuous spell of wet weather. The soil is a medium loam about 3 ft. in depth, overlying partially weathered igneous rock.

Sour orange and rough lemon are used as stocks for three imported orange varieties—Mediterranean sweet, Valencia late and Navalencia, and two local varieties, Vavuniya and Katugastota orange, in the trial at Nalanda experiment, and for Valencia late, Vavuniya, Bibile and Indian sweet orange at Hingurakgoda.

The same two stocks are used for Nagpur Santra, Beauty of Glen Retreat, and Emperor Mandarins in the stock-scion trial at Nalanda where the annual rainfall is 83 inches. The soil here is a medium loam of variable depth overlying magnesium lime stone or dolomite from which it is derived. It contains in sections a high proportion of iron nodules.

#### THE ROOTSTOCKS

*Rough Lemon* (*C. jambhiri* Lush.). The Rough lemon grows well on light sandy soils under conditions of low rainfall, and is used for that purpose on a commercial scale in parts of Australia, Florida, India and S. Africa where according to Marloth (1938) it is the only stock used. In Ceylon this variety

6------------------------------------------------

63

is found in home gardens in the Jaffna Peninsula where it is known in Tamil as *Narathai*, but it occurs only rarely in other districts where its origin can often be traced to imported grafts in which the scion had failed.

The ordinary tubercled lime or *Kudaluthehi* (*C. hystrix*) and *Nataran* a variety of citron (*C. medica*), both of which are slow growing and unsuitable as stocks, are often mistaken for rough lemon because of the rough appearance of the fruit, but their foliage is strikingly different from that of the rough lemon.

The rough lemon seedling has a deeply penetrating taproot and fibrous laterals which are well adapted to forage in the fertile top soil. It can stand drought conditions better than most other stocks. It is inherently a vigorous grower, and is invigorating in its effect on the scion especially in the early years. Fruits produced on this stock during the first few years are generally coarse and not up to standard in quality.

The variety is more resistant to collar rot or gummosis than most other stocks with the exception of sour orange which is highly resistant. It is very susceptible to citrus scab and to a lesser extent to citrus canker.

*Sour Orange* (*C. Aurantium* L.). The sour orange was until recently used almost exclusively as a stock for citrus in California. It is a popular stock for use on wet and heavy soils in Florida, Texas, Arizona, Palestine and the Southern states of Australia, since it is highly resistant to foot rot. According to Hume (1930) about 75 per cent. of the world's commercial output of citrus fruit is from trees grown on this stock.

Many strains of sour orange are known to exist of which Bitter Seville is the strain commonly used in California. Baker (1938) has reported the existence in Trinidad of certain poor strains of sour orange which, though resistant to foot rot, are susceptible to other forms of root disease. The Ceylon sour orange or *Ambuldodan* grows well in village gardens in the wet zone and produces a fruit which is valued for its medicinal properties.

The sour orange seedling is not so vigorous in growth as the rough lemon and is reputed to be semi-dwarfing in its effect on the scion. It has a strong root system with a tap root which when severed in transplanting produces several deeply penetrating roots from near the cut end.

*Sweet Orange Hybrid* (*C. Sinensis* hybr.). The sweet orange is more susceptible to gummosis and other root diseases than sour orange and for that reason it has not been used extensively as a root stock. With improved irrigation and cultural practices the danger of loss through gum diseases has diminished and the stock has in recent years come into favour in California and Australia since trees worked on it grow more vigorously than those on sour orange and give better yield of fruit of really good quality from the start. The root system is well provided with strong fibrous roots though it is somewhat shallow compared to that of the sour orange.

The variety used in this experiment is a hybrid type which was selected for its vigour and prolific bearing habit. These hybrid types go under different local names such as *Haldodan*, *Sideran* &c., and are probably produced as a result of cross pollination with other citrus varieties, such as pummelo and sour orange. The fruits are coarse and larger than the common sweet orange. They are sub-acid in flavour.

In the early stages the seedlings have numerous prominent spines and are not unlike sweet orange seedlings in appearance, but their influence on the scion is probably different from that of the true sweet orange.

*Pummelo* (*C. Maxima* Merr.). The pummelo or *jambola* is a large tree which grows well in home gardens in the wet zone. Several types are known to occur, some having round fruits, others pear shaped. Some are pink fleshed, while others have flesh of ordinary lemon colour. Being a monoembryonic variety the seedlings show considerable variability. They are highly susceptible to attack by scale insects and citrus canker in the nursery stage. The variety is not used as a stock on a commercial scale anywhere in the world.

#### DESIGN OF THE EXPERIMENTS

The Randomized split plot design has been used in all the experiments, and in each subplot there are three plants. Wishart and Sanders (1936) have shown that with the split plot lay-out all the comparisons cannot be made with the same degree of precision. In the experiment at Peradeniya, a greater degree of precision was required for the scion treatments whose effects might be smaller than for the general comparison of the stocks in which greater differences were anticipated, and accordingly the scion treatments were allotted at random to the subplots and the stock treatments to the plots. There are five blocks in the experiment with four plots in each block for the four stocks, and four subplots in each plot for the four scion varieties. The total number of plants in the experiment is 240, which is exclusive of 80 plants in the border row all round the experimental area.

In the grapefruit trial at Hingurakgoda there are six blocks with four plots in each block and two subplots in each plot. Since a greater degree of precision was required in the comparison of the stock treatments each of the two stocks was allotted at random to the subplots in each plot and the scion treatments were randomized over the plots. There is a total of 144 plants exclusive of the border row plants.

Similarly in the orange stock-scion trial at Hingurakgoda there are six blocks with four plots in each block for the four scion varieties and three subplots in each plot for the three root stocks—rough lemon, sour orange and sweet orange. Owing to incidence of canker in the nursery plants on sweet orange stock could not be got ready in time for planting and the records are not complete for publication in this paper.

In the orange trial at Nalanda there are four blocks with five plots in each block for the five scion variety treatments, and two subplots in each plot for the two stock treatments, the total number of plants in the experiment being 120 in addition to those in the border rows.

Five replications are provided in the mandarin trial at Nalanda. The scion varieties are randomized over the plots, and the stocks are allotted to the subplots in each plot, the total number of plants being 120.

#### PLANTING MATERIAL

The seeds for the experiment were collected from known parent trees and planted in seed beds. In the grapefruit trial at Peradeniya the seedling stocks were transplanted into the experimental area and budded *in situ* after they were well established. In the other trials budgrafts raised in the nursery were used. In selecting the planting materials all variant types and bench rooted plants were rigidly culled out. Budwood was taken from a selected parent tree of each variety.

### DISCUSSION

Though grown under different conditions of soil and climate all the citrus varieties have made healthy growth on rough lemon which is shown to be not only vigorous in itself but invigorating in its effect on the scion. No symptoms of chlorosis or die-back were observed on young plants budded on this stock, and nearly all of them have now come into bearing.

Lal Singh and Sham Singh (1942, 1944) report that rough lemon has given the best results as a stock for Blood Red Orange in the citrus root stocks trials in the Punjab. In the earlier trials they found that it induced vigorous vegetative growth in young trees of Malta orange, Santra mandarin and grapefruit budded on it.

10------------------------------------------------

67

Similar observations on the vigour of budgrafts of sweet orange and acid lime on rough lemon stock in the root stock trials at Kodur, S. India, are recorded by Naik (1941). Nandi and Bhattacharya (1943) have found rough lemon to be an invigorating stock for local mandarin in Assam.

The sour orange appears to have a variable effect on the citrus varieties budded on it. There is considerable variability even among the budgrafts of the same variety, some being healthy, others are stunted and chlorotic. Most of the Triumph and Foster grapefruit plants were stunted on it and displayed marked scion overgrowth above the budunion. The initial growth of Marsh seedless and Walters was satisfactory, but subsequently gradual decline set in and many of them died back.

The growth of the imported sweet orange varieties was equally poor on sour orange, and many of them were stunted and chlorotic. The two local varieties, especially vavuniya orange, made good initial growth without developing symptoms of chlorosis and die-back, but their rate of growth showed a tendency to slow down after the second year.

The Nagpur Santra mandarin made steady growth on sour orange stock in the early years, but the growth of the two imported Australian varieties was not satisfactory. It remains to be seen whether the Nagpur Santra mandarin plants will continue to grow well on this stock.

The failure of sour orange as a stock for sweet orange has been reported from Java by Toxopeus (1936) who considers that the cause of the failure may not lie in the stock itself, but in some toxic reaction of the scion upon the stock. He found that the scion variety grew normally for 2 to 3 months but eventually declined and withered within 8 to 12 months. No reason is known for the striking failure of sour orange as a stock for sweet orange in S. Africa.

Lal Singh and Sham Singh (1944) report the failure of Blood Red orange through delayed incompatibility with *Kharna Khatta* which is presumably a variety of sour orange. But they find that the local Malta and Santra mandarin grow well on it. This is contrary to the behaviour of the local mandarin in Assam which, according to Nandi and Bhattacharya (1943), is incompatible with Seville or true sour orange. Both Hume (1930) and Swingle (1909) report that Satsuma mandarin is a failure on sour orange in America.

In recent years there has been a marked decline in vigour of lemon trees budded on sour orange in California, and a condition known as "Quick Decline" resulting in the death of Naval and Valencia orange trees on this stock has also been reported by Fawcett (1945).

The sweet orange hybrid has not made a good stock for grapefruit varieties. Many plants of Triumph and Foster, which appear to be inherently weak scions, were stunted and chlorotic on this stock. The other varieties developed symptoms of delayed incompatibility. It is proposed to carry out trials with the common sweet orange as stock.

On pummelo all the grapefruit varieties except Walters developed symptoms of both chlorosis and mottled leaf in the early stages and were stunted in growth. Walters, which appears to be a vigorous scion variety, grew well at the start, but symptoms of delayed incompatibility began to appear after about two years and most of the plants turned chlorotic and died back. Yellowing of foliage followed by die-back has also been noticed

on Valencia late and Mediterranean sweet orange plants budded on this stock.

It was observed by Lee (1921) in the Philippines that most of the commercial varieties of citrus budded on pummelo were attacked by mottled leaf, but trees upon mandarin orange and calamondin stocks made healthy growth. Lal Singh and Sham Singh (1942) have also reported the appearance of mottled leaf on grapefruit trees budded on pummelo stock. Chlorotic symptoms have also been observed on local mandarin plants budded on pummelo in Assam by Nandi and Bhattacharya (1943).

It is clear from the available evidence that pummelo is least suited for use as a stock for citrus. It makes vigorous and healthy growth when left unbudded, and the failure of the scion is probably due to its inability to obtain its requirements of nutrients through the pummelo stock. Apparently when a vigorous scion is grown on it under favourable conditions the appearance of symptoms of incompatibility is delayed.

Whatever the reason may be for the failure of certain stock-scion combinations, it is evident from these trials that rough lemon is an invigorating stock which is compatible with all varieties of grapefruit, sweet orange and mandarin under study, and that it should be used for this purpose in commercial production until more is known of the performance of sour orange and the common sweet orange as root stocks for citrus under local conditions.

SUMMARY

All four varieties of grapefruit (*C. paradisii* Macf.) at Peradeniya—Walters, Marsh Seedless, Triumph and Foster—made healthy growth on rough lemon (*C. jambhiri* Lush.), an invigorating stock.

On sour orange (*C. Aurantium* L.) and hybrid sweet orange (*C. sinensis* hybr.) their growth was variable with a tendency for most plants to be stunted and chlorotic. Scion overgrowth was noticeable in many on sour orange, a semi-dwarfing stock.

All the varieties failed on pummelo (*C. maxima* Merr.). Triumph and Foster were the first to become stunted and chlorotic while Walters the most invigorating scion was the last.

All four varieties—Walters, Cecily Seedless, Marsh Seedless and Triumph—at Hingurakgoda showed similar behaviour as at Peradeniya on rough lemon and sour orange.

The seven varieties of sweet orange—Vavuniya, Bibile, India, Valencia Late, Mediterranean Sweet and Washington Naval, at Nalanda and Hingurakgoda were compatible with rough lemon, but on sour orange the growth of the imported varieties was variable, many being stunted and chlorotic. The local varieties, especially Vavuniya, made healthy growth although the plants were comparatively small.

All three mandarin varieties (*C. nobilis* Var. *deliciosa*)—Nagpur Santra, Emperor and Beauty of Glen Retreat at Nalanda were compatible with rough lemon, but on sour orange Nagpur Santra made healthy growth while Beauty of Glen Retreat and to some extent Emperor were failures.

## STUDIES IN THE PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND ASSAY OF COMMERCIAL PAPAIN

C. CHARAVANAPAVAN, M.Sc. (Lond.), D.I.C., A.R.I.C.,  
RESEARCH ASSISTANT IN FOOD TECHNOLOGY

### SUMMARY

**S**TUDIES of a fundamental and practical nature on the latex of the papaya fruit have shown that :—

1. (1) Papaya latex coagulates rapidly on stirring in a flat dish as a result of physico-chemical changes brought about by atmospheric oxidation.
2. (2) The oxidation of papaya latex is promoted by oxidizing enzymes belonging to the class called peroxidases.
3. (3) Common salt is quite suitable as a coagulating and protective agent for papaya latex.
4. (4) Commercial papain of good quality can be prepared from papaya latex by sun-drying or oven-drying for 6 to 8 hours between 50° C and 55° C or by drying to the consistency of a fairly hard paste of composition about 20 per cent. added common salt, 45 per cent. papaya latex solids and 35 per cent. moisture.
5. (5) Papaya latex should be dried to a moisture content below 10 per cent. unless common salt is used as a protective agent. Samples having a moisture content above 10 per cent. lose their activity very rapidly and undergo a change in colour from pale cream to dark brown within a period of about one year.
6. (6) A simple modification of the gelatine viscosity method can be used for comparing the proteolytic activities of samples of commercial papain.
7. (7) Good quality commercial papain (not treated with common salt) should have a pale cream colour, a pungent but not unpleasant odour, a moisture content not exceeding 8 per cent., a minimum nitrogen content of 11.5 per cent. on a moisture free basis, and a proteolytic activity not less than 70 per cent. of a freshly prepared sample.

### INTRODUCTION

Commercial papain is prepared by sun-drying, oven-drying or vacuum-drying the latex extracted by lancing the mature green fruit of the papaya tree (*Carica papaya*).

16------------------------------------------------

73

The cultivation of the papaya tree, the method of lancing the fruits and extracting the latex and drying it, have been described in a previous number of this journal (1). This data has been very valuable in many respects. However, as a result of recent advances in our knowledge of the properties of the latex, further investigation appeared desirable in order to improve the local methods of production of commercial papain.

Commercial papain is widely used as a raw material in industry for pharmaceutical preparations, for clearing fruit juices and fermented liquors, for tenderizing meat and for preshrinking and improving the handle of wool (2).

Papaya latex is known to contain at least two proteolytic enzymes called papain and chymopapain (2). These enzymes lose their activity as a result of atmospheric oxidation promoted by certain substances present in the latex. The nature of these substances will be discussed at a later stage in this paper.

The inactivation of the proteolytic enzymes in commercial papain can be reversed by means of certain reducing agents like hydrogen sulphide, hydrogen cyanide, sulphides and sulphites. After prolonged exposure to oxidizing influences, however, the material is no longer capable of reactivation (3). Therefore, commercial papain does not keep indefinitely and it is necessary that its proteolytic activity should be determined, in order to ascertain its quality.

#### METHODS OF ASSAY

The methods of determining the proteolytic activity of commercial papain described in a previous number of this journal (1), have been abandoned by modern workers as they were found to be cumbersome and not accurate enough. The tentative casein digestion method of the A.O.A.C. (4), was tried out and found to involve the use of as much as 200 cc of 95 per cent. ethyl alcohol for every titration. Moreover, the method itself was rather cumbersome, though accurate enough for ordinary purposes. Eventually the modified milk clotting method of Balls and Hoover (5), was used with satisfactory results.

It was brought to the writer's notice that a simple and quick method of determining the proteolytic activities of samples of commercial papain would be useful to the producer. The gelatine viscosity method (6), was therefore modified and a simple apparatus in the form of a capillary-pipette of about 25 cc. capacity devised. The pipette was 45 cm. long and had a capillary tube 15 cm. long of 1 mm. bore, attached to the end. The time of flow of a 5 per cent. aqueous solution of gelatine at room temperature, at about 30°C, from a point marked at the top stem of the pipette to a point marked at the bottom stem should be about sixty seconds. The procedure adopted was as follows:—

A 0·1 per cent. aqueous solution of the commercial papain was prepared by grinding the product to a paste with water and then diluting it. 1 cc. of this solution was then added to 30 cc. of a 5 per cent. aqueous solution of gelatine prepared by warming the gelatine gently with water until it was completely dissolved and cooling it to room temperature. The two solutions were well mixed and allowed to remain at room temperature for 15 minutes. The time taken for the gelatine solution to flow from one point in the capillary-pipette to the other as described above was noted by means of a stop-watch before

17------------------------------------------------

74

and after treatment with the commercial papain solution. The time of flow ( $t$ ) in seconds is given by the formula :—

$$t = K \frac{v}{d}, \text{ where } (v) \text{ is the viscosity coefficient, } (K) \text{ a constant}$$

for the particular pipette and ( $d$ ) the density of the gelatine solution. Now, if  $t_1$  and  $t_2$  are the times of flow before and after treatment with the commercial papain solution, respectively, then the proteolytic activity ( $A$ ) is given by the

relationship :—  $A = \frac{t_1 - t_2}{t_1}$ . The change in density due to the addition

of 1 cc. of the commercial papain solution to 30 cc. of the gelatine solution was negligible.

It should be noted that by this method, it is only possible to state whether a particular sample is inferior or superior to another, as there is no simple relationship between activity and enzyme concentration (6).

### EXPERIMENTAL

The investigations on the properties of papaya latex and its behaviour when exposed to air indicated that the coagulation of the latex was a physico-chemical change brought about by atmospheric oxidation. This was accompanied by a fall in pH from 6.2 to 5.8 and was probably due to the formation of acidic substances.

The fresh latex had a moisture content of about 80 per cent. It took about 4 to 6 hours to coagulate on standing ; but rapid coagulation occurred in about 10 minutes when it was poured into a flat dish to form a thin layer, and then stirred vigorously.

Papaya latex was observed to keep fresh for about 4 hours without appreciable deterioration in activity. On keeping for longer periods, however, decomposition set in with the development of an offensive odour and accompanied by a rise in pH to 7.2 in about 24 hours. This decomposed product gradually darkened to a brown colour on drying. This darkening was probably due to the oxidation of a chromogenic substance present in the latex, as in the case of apples (7). The darkening of papaya latex was further enhanced by the use of iron utensils. Iron appears to catalyse the above reaction. The presence of oxidizing enzymes belonging to the class called peroxidases in the latex, was demonstrated by the benzidine colour reaction, in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (8). These enzymes are thermostable and promote oxidative changes which reduce the proteolytic activity of papaya latex during drying and storage (5).

It was observed by Balls *et al.* (5), that common salt had a remarkable coagulating effect on the latex. The writer observed that the fresh latex required about 3 per cent. by weight of common salt to coagulate it. Moreover, common salt was reported to have a protective effect on the proteolytic enzymes in the latex, provided the treated latex was not completely dried. A paste prepared by partially drying the latex treated with common salt,

18------------------------------------------------

75

to give a final composition of 35 per cent. papaya latex solids, 20 per cent. common salt and 45 per cent. moisture, was found to have undergone no appreciable loss of activity on exposure to air at about 30°C. for 14 days. It did not lose activity to any appreciable extent for as long as 50 days and less than 10 per cent. in 190 days, when kept in airtight vessels. This method was investigated by the writer and it was confirmed that a minimum concentration of 20 per cent. common salt in the final product was necessary to produce a paste of satisfactory consistency. A sample of paste was prepared by adding 10 per cent. of common salt by weight to the latex and drying the mixture in the sun to a stage when the material became thick and plastic. This product was found to contain 20 per cent. common salt, 45 per cent. papaya latex solids and 35 per cent. moisture.

Papaya latex after coagulation can be converted into thin uniform worms by passing it through a colander, string-hopper squeezer or potato-squeezer, having tiny holes. These utensils could be made of aluminium, brass or stainless-steel. If iron is used, it should be heavily tinned. The material was distributed uniformly in thin layers on drying trays covered with muslin cloth. The material was then dried in the sun or in a well ventilated drier between 50°C. and 55°C., for about 6 to 8 hours, to give a pale cream product with a moisture content below 8 per cent. The dried product should be quite crisp and crumble easily to a powder when crushed. Products not treated with common salt and having a moisture content above 10 per cent. were found to deteriorate very rapidly even on storage in airtight bottles. They lost proteolytic activity almost completely with the development of an offensive odour and changed in colour from pale cream to dark brown within an year. It was observed that good quality commercial papain (not treated with common salt) generally had a moisture content of 5 per cent. to 8 per cent. and a nitrogen content of 11.5 per cent. to 11.8 per cent. on a moisture-free basis. It had a pale cream colour and a pungent but not unpleasant odour. Commercial papain in the form of a half dried paste containing 20 per cent. common salt was observed to have a pale cream colour and to retain the pleasant odour of fresh papaya latex for a longer period than the former.

#### METHODS OF PRODUCTION

The above investigations indicate that commercial papain of good quality can be produced under local conditions by the following methods:—

1. (1) The latex is extracted by lancing the mature green fruits lightly on the surface by means of a piece of glass with a sharp edge, a stainless steel knife or a bamboo splinter with a sharp edge. This operation is done in the mornings before 10 A.M.
2. (2) The latex is collected in glass or porcelain vessels. Coconut-shells or the leaf-sheaths of arecanut palms could also be used. The latex is pooled and then poured into flat enamel or earthen-ware vessels and stirred vigorously by means of a wooden spoon till it coagulates.
3. (3) The coagulated latex is immediately passed through a colander, string-hopper squeezer or a potato-squeezer, made of aluminium, brass or stainless-steel. If iron is used, it should be

19------------------------------------------------

76

heavily tinned. The material is exuded as thin uniform worms and is evenly distributed in thin layers on drying trays covered with muslin cloth.

1. (4) The material is dried in the sun or in a well ventilated drier at 50°C. to 55°C., till it becomes crisp. The product takes about 6 to 8 hours to dry to a moisture content below 8 per cent.
2. (5) If producers wish to use common salt as a protective agent, a half dried paste is prepared by adding 10 per cent. by weight of common salt to the latex. The subsequent process is as described in para. 3. The material is dried in the sun or in the drier at 50°C. to 55°C., until it becomes thick and plastic. The product takes about 2 to 3 hours to dry to this consistency.
3. (6) Commercial papain should be packed immediately after drying in tins free from rust and sealed airtight. It is preferable to evacuate the air from the tins and seal them to ensure good keeping quality. The tins should be kept in a cool place preferably under cold storage.
4. (7) The product, soon after packing in tins, should be transported with as little delay as possible to the central clearing house.

## RATE OF NUTRIENT SUPPLY IN TWO MAJOR SOIL TYPES OF THE ISLAND

D. E. V. KOCH, B.Sc. Hons., Ph. D. (London), D.I.C., F.R.I.C.,  
ASSISTANT CHEMIST, DEPARTMENT OF AGRICULTURE, PERADENIYA.

### SUMMARY

ONE of the most important factors conditioning soil fertility is that which takes cognizance of soil nutrients. This study is concerned with those aspects of a soil which may help in ascertaining its inherent fertility commonly associated with its colloids. It is not the quantity factor but the intensity factor, or rate of nutrient supply in soluble form or pseudo-solution, that has been made the special subject of this investigation.

The two soils selected for this purpose are entirely different in character. One sampled at Nalanda is a non-lateritic loam, slightly alkaline, and having a fairly good supply of organic matter and a reserve of alkaline—earth carbonates. The other from Peradeniya, is a lateritic loam, slightly acid in reaction and containing only a fair quantity of organic matter but none of the carbonates.

The conclusions drawn from this study are summarized briefly below :—

#### 1. In the case of exchangeable bases :—

- (a) The nutrient supply to the soil solution or liquid phase of the soil depends not only on the organic status of the soil but also on the rate of nitrification taking place.
- (b) The rate of dissolution of calcium is much higher than that of magnesium.
- (c) The soluble ammonia content of both soils is low.
- (d) Iron and more particularly manganese are brought into solution slowly but the former tends to be dispersed in colloidal form under the conditions of the experiment.

#### 2. In the case of anions :—

- (a) Inorganic phosphate is present only in minute amounts but there is a tendency for this low concentration to be maintained by quick replenishing. Organic phosphate is present in extremely low concentration and its behaviour is similar to that in inorganic form.

21------------------------------------------------

78

- (b) Nitrates are, in the main, responsible for bringing bases into solution.
- (c) Chlorides have hardly any power of being produced in soluble form from unionised conditions.
- (d) The amount of organic matter in solution or pseudo-solution appears to be dependent on the organic status of a soil.

The practical application of the knowledge gained hereby is discussed.

### INTRODUCTION

The soil chemist engaged on advisory work on an extensive scale is obliged to take cognizance of a number of factors conditioning soil fertility and of these the most important is that of soil nutrients, the study of which helps him in advising suitable measures for maintaining and even increasing soil fertility. It is only by means of systematic survey and analyses on a large scale, involving the carrying out of innumerable determinations both qualitatively and quantitatively in a well equipped laboratory, that any fairly comprehensive view of the state of fertility of a soil can be attained. In addition to the chemical analytical figures obtained a vast amount of empirical observations on the effects of soil treatment is made by pot and water cultures, and field experiments, but, it is doubtful whether the knowledge thereby established completely solves the problem. It is well established that soils vary in their inherent potentialities for crop production and also that these differences are attributed to the soil colloids—the clay and the humus.

This paper deals with some of those aspects, which throw light on the inherent fertility of two entirely different soils. It is not the quantity factor but the intensity factor or rate of supply of nutrients in soluble form or pseudo-solution that has been made the special subject of this investigation.

Attempts have been made by various workers to devise simple methods whereby the inherent fertility of a soil may be assessed. In certain cases conductivity measurements on soil suspensions were made (a) immediately and (b) after standing for a period of time. The increase in conductivity, which is brought about mainly by micro-organic activity, has been proposed as measure of soil fertility. Unfortunately such conditions are quite different to normal field conditions and hence the data obtained cannot be properly interpreted. Nevertheless, it has to be emphasized that it is the rate of nutrient supply in a soil or the ionic pressure of its nutrients that is an important factor in estimating fertility. P. Kottgen and L. Jung (1) have shown that "the ionic pressure curves obtained by means of electro-ultra filtration give a better picture of soil fertility than the relative numbers obtained by various extracting substances." Thus, there appears to be some advantage in the determination of water soluble constituents. Such studies are of special application especially when dealing with soils from semi-arid regions where irrigation is practised and for this reason is of special importance in Ceylon.

### Soils

The two soils selected for this purpose are entirely different in character. One sampled at Nalanda is a non-lateritic loam, slightly alkaline, and having a fairly good supply of organic matter (3.3 per cent.) and a reserve of alkaline-earth carbonates (1.52 per cent.) The other from Peradeniya, is a lateritic loam, slightly acid in reaction and containing only a fair quantity of organic matter (1.68 per cent.), but none of carbonates.

### Methods

The following methods of analyses, which have been found to be highly reliable, were employed. pH electrometrically, conductivity by a special apparatus devised by the author, chloride volumetrically, ammonia by the Pregl micro-chemical Kjeldahl method, nitrate-nitrite (nitrogen) by reduction to ammonia using Kahlbaum reduced iron, organic matter by a modification of the Budapest soil-carbon method, calcium as oxalate by isoelectric precipitation, magnesium by micro-chemical titration employing an achromatic indicator, phosphoric acid by the Denige's colorimetric method adapted by W. #### PRACTICAL APPLICATION

It is being increasingly recognized in India (5) that the next stage in improving yield of crops and in decreasing cost of production lies not so much in evolving fresh varieties as in improving soil fertility through efficient soil management and judicious use of manures. Dr. Harold Mann, who spent many years of his life in India, taking part in a discussion at a meeting of the Royal Society of Arts where Sir Bryce Burt read a paper on Agricultural

24------------------------------------------------

81

Progress in India during the decade 1929-39, said that the soil in India is about the poorest in the world and until a method can be found for improving the soil, the improvement of crops will not go much further than it has gone up to the present. That we are no better off than our neighbour, India, in our heritage, the soil, is forcibly brought out in this investigation which has conclusively proved that the rate of dissolution of plant nutrients is slow even in these soils which are above the average fertility level of those of Ceylon. This is not surprising as the soils of the Island are probably the oldest in the world and have been subjected to high temperatures and fairly heavy rainfall as a result of which they have been leached. The base, calcium, has been shown to be preferentially lost. That the waters of Ceylon are rich in calcium silicate has been proved by oceanographic surveys (privately communicated), yet do we systematically lime the soil, if we lime at all, to make good the loss ? We deceive ourselves if we think that we can cheat the soil. Land is opened up and fair yields are obtained for a few years. Then cultivation becomes unprofitable. The loss of nutrients must therefore be reduced. Are we making use of these two facts (1) that transpiration by the plant and (2) the retention of moisture by the organic matter in the upper layers of the soil reduce leaching ?

Is the average Agriculturist aware that a grass ley is beneficial also because it reduces leaching considerably and holds bases, especially calcium ? The importance of maintaining the calcium status of a soil should be realized because the base holding properties of its clay-colloids diminish fairly rapidly as this constituent is leached away. The converse is also fortunately true for alkaline waters containing lime have been observed to bring about a gradual improvement in the capacity of the soil to hold bases (6). Liming should therefore be regularly carried out. No less important than liming is the maintenance of the organic matter status of a soil. When it is continuously cultivated its organic matter is burnt out and the soil falls to a powder and becomes liable to erosion. This must be avoided. Bulky organic manures like green manures, cattle manure and compost should be applied frequently and the land, which should under no circumstances be left bare fallow, rested for a period of time under grass. It has to be added that if crop yields are to be maintained it is necessary to supply those nutrients removed in the crop. However, as different plants vary greatly in the relative amounts of nutrients they absorb even when grown under similar conditions, the exact manorial requirements of each crop should be determined by carrying out field experiments.

This investigation has shown that a sufficiency of lime and organic matter is necessary to get the best effect from added fertilizers. It also clearly brings out that lime promotes the decomposition of organic matter. We find that where there is a surplus of lime as in soils associated with limestone, *e.g.*, those of Jaffna, Dambulla, Nalanda and Tabbowa, the soils are generally poor in nitrogen and organic matter.

Thus for the maintenance of fertility—

1. (1) The loss of nutrients by erosion leaching &c., should be reduced to the barest minimum,
2. (2) The application of lime and bulky organic manures should be systematically carried out and
3. (3) The nutrients removed in the crop should be made good.

## THE NATURAL REGENERATION OF CEYLON FORESTS

C. H. HOLMES, B.Sc. (Lond.), B.A. (Cantab.), B.Sc. (Sp. Hons.) Lond., Diplomas in Forestry (Cantab. and Oxon.)

*DIVISIONAL FOREST OFFICER, SILVICULTURE.*

**N**EITHER the substantive nor qualifying adjective of the term "Natural Regeneration" carries its full literal significance. It is "regeneration" only in a rather special way and "natural" in a restricted sense. Regeneration implying the reproduction of its own kind as applied to Natural Forests is the last thing we as Foresters desire.

The question of Forest Regeneration arises from one or more of three circumstances :

- (a) The forest concerned does not carry any of the timber species required, or
- (b) A sufficient concentration of such species, or
- (c) Economically desirable species as may have existed have been felled.

In Ceylon these reasons are nearly always simultaneously operative. The second condition is specially characteristic and to perpetuate this under any form of regeneration will be no kind of improvement. The pure ecologists will think of regeneration in terms of seral succession and be inclined to view it as a form of short secondary succession back to original or similar type of forest brought about by biologic interference. Actually we want something more and something better than the regeneration of the original type particularly as regards its specific composition.

This cannot be achieved without active and purposeful manipulation over a period of years. Hence the regeneration we intend is not entirely natural and is not much more than that the seed from which it arises occurs naturally ! This process is, therefore, more accurately an artificial conversion of natural forest into more economically valuable and desirable timber bearing forests suitable to and conditioned by the climatic and edaphic factors of the area concerned brought about by the selective reproduction of the constituent vegetation by natural means. Of the various means of reproduction from which natural regeneration may arise that from seeds is the most important. It is rather a whole complex of factors the nett result of the actions and reactions of which ultimately determine the effective contribution it makes. Amongst the unit factors in its make up must be considered :— the time, periodicity or abundance of the seed fall of a species; the germination capacity of the seed, its agents of dispersal, its predators; and lastly, the growth, vigour, pests, diseases and silvicultural requirements of the resultant seedlings.

From the stand point of Natural Regeneration, it is most appropriate, therefore, that organized Silvicultural Research in Ceylon should have

28------------------------------------------------

85

commenced with the study of the Flowering and Fruiting of our principal timber species. This work falls into two phases :—

- (a) The study of Flowering and Fruiting “en masse” over large areas by the collection and collation of observations carried out on a large number of trees of selected species by numerous widely distributed observers over a period of years, and
- (b) Observations on individual, numbered and properly identified, trees by single observers in different localities.

The first part of this investigation has been concluded and results published on 31 important species. The work on the second stage of the investigation has been going on for the past three years and monthly observations are being maintained in a large number of phenological observation plots. These give a fair representation of the principle species comprising our major high forest types with a proportionately larger number of plots for the more extensive forest types concerned.

The seedling is the most critical period in the life of the tree. With the usual extravagance of tropical nature, many millions of seedlings come up every year only a small number of which survive ultimately under even the most favourable conditions. Up to a few years ago preoccupation of those engaged on forest work has been almost entirely with the adult exploitable trees. It is indeed a far cry from the seedling of a few days or months to the adult sawable tree of near a century. It is not surprising, therefore, that few of even the preferred timber species were generally identifiable as seedlings. The juvenile forms of certain of our trees—e.g., *Tolol* (*TRICHADENIA ZEYLANICA*), *Welang* (*PTEROSPERMUM CANESCENS*), *Bedi-del* (*ARTOCARPUS NOBILIS*), *Palu* (*MANILKARA HEXANDRA*) &c., are so very different from what they look like as big trees. Seedlings of others like *Lunumidella* (*MELIA COMPOSITA*), *Satin* (*CHOLOROXYLON SWIETENIA*), *Suriyamara* (*ALBIZZIA ODORATISSIMA*) resemble certain common herbaceous weeds. With the inauguration of the Silvicultural Research Branch of the Forest Department, experimental nurseries and gardens were opened up, representative of the principal climatic and forest zones of the Island, where seed germination characteristics and the growth, behaviour, silvicultural requirements, pests and diseases of the seedlings of our principal timber and other forest trees could be more closely and effectively studied than under the vicissitudes of forest conditions. Information on a large number of important species is steadily accumulating from fundamental routine investigations conducted at these places that would in due course assist and make possible the efficient and scientific handling of regeneration of such species under field conditions.

In coppice regeneration two principle types have to be recognized :—

- (a) True coppice from the stools of large exploitable trees felled.
- (b) Shoots from young saplings and seedlings that have been cut down.

Both contribute to natural regeneration in different measure and specially in the case of the former varies according to the particular species concerned. The following species are known to coppice exceedingly well :—

*Pepaliya*, (*Aporosa latifolia*). *Welipenna* (*ANISOPHYLLEA CINNAMOMOIDES*), *Mora* (*NEPHELIUM LONGANA* and *GARDNERI*), *Dikwenna* (*PITYRANTHE VERRUCOSA*), *Madan* (*SYZYGIUM CUMINI*), *Guru-kina* (*CALOPHYLLUM*

29------------------------------------------------

86

CALABA), *Kuma* (GLENIA ZEYLANICA and LEPISANTHES TETRAPHYLLO) others like *Suriyamara*, *Kabalmara* (ALBIZZIA LEBBEK), *Bulu* (TERMINALIA BELERICA), *Gammalu* (PTEROCARPUS MARSUPIUM), *Milla* (VITEX PINNATA) coppice fairly well whilst others still, e.g., *Satin*, *Palu*, *Hedawaka* (CHAETOCARPUS SPP.) *Hora* (DIPTEROCARPUS ZEYLANICUS), *Bu-hora* (DIPTEROCARPUS HISPIDUS) &c., coppice indifferently or not at all.

Next to coppice, root-suckering is the most important other form of vegetative reproduction. In certain species it is so dependable that it may be employed to the total exclusion of seedlings. Close young stands of *Katu-imbul* (GOSSAMPINUS MALABARICA) *Kohomba* (AZADIRACHTA INDICA), and CASUARINA EQUISETIFOLIA for example may be raised by the simple expedient of soil wounding or surface trenching at intervals. The profuse regeneration of *Satin* and sometimes *Suriyamara* to be seen in newly cultivated 'chenas' has been found to arise principally from root-suckers. Root-suckers of *Welang* have been observed to spring up in profusion along shallow intercepted trenches delimiting experimental plots in the Northern Division. Other species that have been observed to propagate themselves freely from root-suckers are:—*Diospyros melanoxylon*, *Dikwenna*, *Wa* (CASSIA SIAMEA and TIMORENSIS), *Kabal Mara*, *Ehela* (CASSIA FISTULA), and *Dunu-madala* (STEREOSPERMUM CHELENIOIDES).

Other forms of propagation, e.g., through layering of lower branches and sprouting from detached vegetative parts are of limited occurrence. Of indigenous tree species the only case of natural layering observed has been of *Then-Thukki* (DIMORPHOCALYX GLABELLUS). I once came across a branch of this tree remaining perfectly green and vigorously alive though it had been cut and completely severed from the stem more than a year previously. On examination it was found that the twigs which had been just touching the ground had rooted and the translocation streams were apparently of the opposite directions to normal! In the case of certain woody shrubs and herbaceous weeds prolific natural regeneration by layering and detached vegetative parts, e.g., *Kekiriwara* (SCHUMACHERIA CASTANAEFOLIA) *Schigostima hirsuta* and *Lanka palu* (MIKANIA SCANDENS) to mention a few—give no end of trouble to the Silviculturist.

Seeds being the principle means of propagation the presence of parent seed trees is of course the essential requirement for natural regeneration. This does not, however, always and for all species mean that parent seed trees must of necessity be in or even near the site to be regenerated. The necessary minimum proximity of parent seed trees is dependant solely on the dispersal potential of the seeds of species desired. Species with small seed carried by wind or aerial animal agency such as by birds, bats or flying foxes may effectively seed areas miles away from the parent seed trees. *Satin*, *Ranai* (ALSEODAPHNE SEMECARPIFOLIA), *Mora*, *Kolon* (ADINA CORDIFOLIA), *Etamba* (MANGIFERA ZEYLANICA), *Milla*, *Del* and the *Dambu* (SYZYGIUM SPP.) belong to this class of high seed dispersal potential and do not require to be actually on the site. On the other hand large seeded species like *Hal*, (VATERIA COPALLIFERA) species with dehiscent fruits like the *Hedawakas* (CHAETOCARPUS SPP.) or with helicopter mechanisms for their dispersal like *Hora*, the *Duns* (DOONAS or SHOREAS)—or those species the seeds of which are spread by the ground fauna of the forests—e.g., *Palu*, *Ehela*, *Mi*, *Etahirilla* (TURPINIA MALABARICA) the *Kinas* (CALOPHYLLUM SPP.) are not carried far and require to be on the spot. Fortunately for us the dispersal potential of a large number of our preferred timber species is such that it is possible other factors being favourable, to obtain a good proportion of them in natural regeneration particularly in the Wet Evergreen High Forest Type without much concern over the presence or absence of parent seed trees—over small operational areas. Whilst flowering and fruiting has been found to be annual

functions in the case of most of our Timber trees species it has been known that bumper seed-years occur periodically. It is when such seed years coincide with regeneration operations that the best results with particular species, e.g., *Hora* are obtained. Animal depredators of seed may yet upset anticipations. A bumper seed year of *Hora* may be whittled down to poor resultant regeneration by swarms of parakeets and green woodpeckers that feed on the fruits.

Quality, germinative capacity and retention of viability of seeds are other factors that determine the contribution they make. Seed of *Palu*, *Kekuna* (CANARIUM ZEYLANICUM), *Hedawaka*, *Kohomba*, *Ranai* and *Satin*, do not retain viability of seed for long and unless favourable weather conditions follow seed fall the regeneration of such species in that year are poor. The general lack of *Palu* regeneration in the Dry Mixed Evergreen High Forests may in part be due to unseasonal 'seed'—fall.

Weather conditions generally are important particularly before setting of seed and after seed-fall—fair weather without excessive drought for the former period and good sustained rain seen after seed-fall. Naturally rainfall tends to be limiting more in the Dry Zone and hardly at all in the rest of the Island. In this Zone as well as in the Hills, for other reasons, seedlings of certain tree species exhibit a peculiar annual die-back of their leafy shoots year after year whilst the roots continue to develop underground till they are able to sustain the last shoot permanently. This means that such seedlings, e.g., of *Satin*, *Milla* and *Chaddavakku* (WALSURA PISCIDIA) or *Kina* (CALOPHYLLUM WALKERI) and *Dambu* (SYZYGIUM SPP.) of Dry Mixed Evergreen and Sub-Tropical Montane Forests respectively remain in the recruitment stage for 3 to 4 years or more.

Root competition from the trees of the parent overwood is probably the principle single factor we are concerned with. This more than light is what affects the incidence and establishment of natural regeneration under different regeneration operations though they appear to have more to do with the variation and control of light. This competition is more for soil moisture less for soil nutrients and, as is to be expected, is more important in Dry Zone forests. The use of *AZADIRACHTA INDICA*, one of the more readily dependable species for planting in these localities as an indicator species was spoilt due to stripping of the leaves and breaking of the plants by monkeys. The results, however, demonstrate quite clearly that the most limiting of the factors influencing the growth of young seedlings in the Mixed Evergreen High Forests is undoubtedly the intense competition for the limited soil moisture offered by the established root systems of the parent overwood. It is interesting and important to note also that a controlled surface burn of the slash obtained from clearing the undergrowth assists the vigour and growth of young seedlings in this type of forest. Such slash burning is unnecessary in Wet Evergreen High Forest type and contra-indicated for the reason that when such burning is feasible it is likely to cause damage by bringing about the death of the characterically thin barked trees of these forests. These results have been confirmed by more recent experiments at the centres mentioned.

Associated with and inseparable from the factor of root competition from the trees of the overwood is the matter of light incidence. Other factors being favourable light is hardly ever limiting in any of our forests at least for seed germination and the early life of seedlings. Indeed shade is necessary and even essential for germination of the seed of forest tree species and the well-being of young forest tree seedlings. For practical purposes no playing about with photoelectric meters is really necessary. Use of such instruments can only measure direct overhead light satisfactorily whereas it is the oblique light that is more important where light as a factor happens to count at all. The canopy manipulation affects the underground conditions more than those above ground and that the former effect is more important from the point of view of natural regeneration must be borne in mind. For these reasons the expression of canopy density as a fraction of the sky-space shut out serves amply for working purposes and as it admits of fairly accurate interpretation in the field has been used in the investigations on which this paper is based.

Soil conditions of the site are not important to the same extent as in the artificial regeneration. Natural regeneration can be only a conversion of a forest to a greater concentration of a selected number of species which are already on the site and these amenable to the conditions therein and are to that extent not of the operator's choice. In the case of small seeded species like *Kolon*, *Rukkatana* (*ALSTONIA SCHOLARIS*), *Bakmi* (*NAUCLEA ORIENTALIS*), *Helamba* (*MITRAGYNA PARVIFOLIA* and *TUBULOSA*), a fairly clean floor not closely covered with litter is necessary for successful germination.

Making conditions favourable to the development of the desired Timber Tree species necessarily invites also other undesirable species which are generally more prolific and much faster growing. Unless early and relatively frequent attention is given to cleanings profuse regeneration that might initially have reminded one of overstocked nursery—beds may disappear completely within a couple of years. The normal shrub associates of such regeneration are easily controlled and do not give much trouble. Most are useful and must even be specially retained to protect the soil and nurse the desired species. Others, however, are veritable scourges—e.g., *Lanka-palu* (*MIKANIA SCANDENS*), *SCHIZOSTIGMA HIRSUTA*, *Wal-kolon* (*EUPATORIUM*

The incidence of certain weed species help to give a rough indication of the extent of the opening up of the overwood in natural regeneration operations. FEEDING STUFFS AND FODDERS

Since the publication in *The Tropical Agriculturist* of May, 1940, of Chemical Note 17, which dealt with the analyses of local manures, green manures, fodders and feedingstuffs, and composting materials (1), several samples of these classes of agricultural products and waste materials have been examined in the Chemical Laboratory, Peradeniya, for advisory purposes. In this Note the analyses of the more characteristic of these samples are recorded and discussed as they are of general interest and utility. Most of the analyses were carried out by Messrs. S. Kandiah and D. G. Panditsekera, and a few by Dr. D. E. V. Koch, Assistant Chemist. The samples discussed in this paper number 40 and include: 15 manures or waste products which may be used as such, 9 green manures, composts and composting materials, 12 feedingstuffs, and 4 pasture grasses and fodders. The results are shown in *Tables I to IV*.

MANURES

The data of *Table I* relate to manures.

*Elephant Dung.*—The nitrogen and phosphoric acid contents of the fresh sample are low compared with those of cattle manure, but the well-decomposed sample compares favourably with the latter in these respects. The old sample has a high ash content (due to admixture with earth and foreign material), but its potash and lime contents are remarkably high. If this sample is typical of others, well-decomposed elephant dung should be a very valuable bulky organic manure.

*Hare Dung.*—The manurial constituents of this material are low considering its low moisture content.

*Bat Guano.*—Previous analyses (1) of bat guano from other sources in Ceylon have shown nitrogen contents varying from 6.1 to 12.8 per cent, phosphoric acid from 1.1 to 4.1 per cent, and potash from 1.0 to 2.4 per cent. This sample is, therefore, of average quality as regards manurial value. It is reported that the quantity of bat guano available in the Nitre Cave district of Ceylon is not large, but this is a matter for further investigation. The caves, are, however, not easily accessible and transport costs of the manure will be high. But considering the high cost of manures at present, it would be well worth exploiting this useful material wherever it is obtainable.

*Fish Sweepings and Fish Scales.*—The former is a waste product of the dry fish trade which finds a ready sale. Its manurial value is slightly lower than that of fish manure. A waste material which is, however, not utilized to the extent it might be in Ceylon is fish scales. The sample examined contained over 6 per cent. of nitrogen, 4.5 per cent. of phosphoric acid and 22 per cent. of lime. It is thus superior to fish manure in manurial value and its use,

after grinding is, therefore, strongly advocated, particularly on acid soils because of its high lime content.

*Maldiv Fish Dust.*—The sample examined contained in addition to the dust, pieces of the dried fish in varying sizes up to 1 by  $\frac{1}{4}$  inch. In manurial value it is superior to fish manure. As its fat content is low (2.93 per cent.), it would be a useful material for application to local crops provided it is ground down before use.

*Margosa and Mee Poonac.*—Margosa poonac has a nitrogen content of over 5 per cent. Its fat content is, however, fairly high (9.0 per cent.), and it would, therefore, decompose in the soil relatively slowly. Where available, it could advantageously be used for manuring highland crops. Mee poonac is only of low manurial value. Mahua or mowrah cake obtained from the seed of the related species *Bassia latifolia* nitrifies very slowly owing to its high saponin content. As mee poonac contains saponins, it is likely to behave similarly.

*Tea Sweepings.*—The sample examined contained just under 3 per cent. of nitrogen, and about 1.8 per cent. potash. It should be useful for composting with other waste materials or for application to highland crops.

*Barnacles and Ship's Bottom Scrapings.*—These samples were sent by the Harbour Engineer, Colombo. Barnacles, as would be expected from their nature, consist mainly of calcium carbonate (84 per cent.). Ground down fine, they would be a useful ameliorant for acid soils. The sample contains about 0.5 per cent. of nitrogen. On receipt, the sample of ship's bottom scrapings was in a high state of decomposition. Air-dried, it was a dark grey material with nitrogen and phosphoric acid contents of about 1.5 per cent. and 1 per cent. respectively. This material is best utilized for composting with vegetable wastes.

*Potash Manures.*—The crude sample is a dark grey and the concentrated product a white granular powder. It was reported to be prepared from local materials, presumably potash husks. The former has a potash content of about 24 per cent. and the latter about 38 per cent. They should make useful fertilizers for most crops, but as they are mainly in the form of chlorides, they would be unsuitable for use on tobacco.

*Citronella Ash.*—An analysis of a sample made in 1928 (2) showed the following figures: potash 7 per cent., lime 3.3 per cent., and phosphoric acid 1.4 per cent. The present sample is appreciably richer in potash. Citronella ash has been found beneficial for paddy on sandy soils and alkaline clay loams.

#### GREEN MANURES, COMPOSTS AND COMPOSTING MATERIALS

The analyses of these materials are shown in *Table II*. The tender stem and leaf material of the two leguminous green manures *Mimosa bracteatinga* and *Albizia sumatrana* is rich in nitrogen, but that of the latter is the richer of the two. The phosphoric acid content of *Albizia sumatrana* is also higher than that of the former.

*Salvinia auriculata*, which has recently become a troublesome weed in our water courses is, like its more dangerous ally the water hyacinth (*Eichornia crassipes*), richest in potash of the common plant nutrients, but *Salvinia* has only just over half the potash content of the water hyacinth.

Samples of water hyacinth in Bengal showed much higher potash contents. *Salvinia* is slightly superior to water hyacinth in nitrogen content, but both are poor in this constituent. The former has much more lime and appreciably more phosphoric acid than water hyacinth. *Salvinia* is best used for composting with other waste materials preferably in a partially-dried condition. A raw material which is uncommon in this Island, but which is occasionally to be found in upcountry districts is *papyrus*. The sample examined had nitrogen and ash contents, on a dry matter basis, similar to those of citronella grass. *Papyrus* could, therefore, be advantageously used for composting.

#### FEEDINGSTUFFS

Table III furnishes the analyses of the characteristic feedingstuffs examined.

*Gingelly Poonac.*—A locally grown sample of the poonac prepared in a hydraulic press was compared with one prepared in an expeller press. The analytical data show higher figures for fat, fibre and ash in the former. The lime content of gingelly poonac is high and this is one reason for its high nutrient value.

*Dhal "Dust" and "Chuni".*—These are by-products in the preparation of cured "dhals" from pulses. The former is a locally-prepared sample and the latter one imported from India. They mainly consist of the husks with part of the adhering kernel of the pulse. The samples are of similar nutritive value, but the "chuni" from India is somewhat richer in protein and has a lower fibre content. They are both very useful feedingstuffs.

*Kurakkan Straw and Chaff.*—The chaff was obtained as a waste product in the threshing of kurakkan earheads in a tea roller and sifter. It has a similar composition to that of kurakkan straw, but has a lower fibre content than the latter. The straw is richer than rice straw in proteins, but is otherwise of much the same composition as the latter. Its calcium content is slightly lower than that of rice straw.

*Tamarind Seed.*—This was a sample of the roasted and shelled seed from India. It had a low fibre and high protein and fat contents. In analytical composition it is superior to the cereals as a feedingstuff. Experience is, however, lacking locally in regard to the use of this material

as a feed for farm animals, though it is reported to be utilized for this purpose in India.

*Maize "Cake".*—This material was received from the Controller of Animal Foods as "maize cake" and consisted of the skins or hulls of the maize kernel. The sample has fair protein and fat contents. While its fibre content is high, compared with other coarse feeding stuffs, *e.g.*, straws, it is much lower in this constituent. It could be used for feeding purposes as a coarse fodder.

*Fish Meal and Netheli.*—A sample of sprats (*netheli* fish) from the local market showed a very high protein and high lime and phosphoric acid contents. Sprats are a very useful poultry feed. The two samples of fish meal examined were prepared at the Ambepussa Farm from local fish, a variety of *sudayo*, S. One was prepared by removing the skin, bones, head &c. of the fish, drying in the sun and grinding in a mill. The other was dried whole and ground. Both samples are of high nutritive value, but the one with bones &c. is, as would be expected, richer in lime and phosphoric acid than the other. Both samples, however, suffered from the disadvantage that they contained very high percentages of salt. They were, therefore, unsuitable for use as feedingstuffs unless the salt contents were reduced to within the maximum of 4 per cent. permissible. Investigation in this laboratory showed that by soaking the meal in water for about half an hour and decanting or pressing off the excess water, the salt content could be reduced to within the safe limits. In the case of the whole fish, the material had to be steeped in water overnight and then washed and dried.

#### PASTURE GRASSES AND FODDERS

In *Table IV* the analyses of 4 samples of pasture grasses and fodders are presented. Two of these were carpet grasses (*Axonopus* spp.) from Peradeniya, the broad leaf variety (*A. compressus*) being more widely prevalent than the narrow leaf (*A. affinis*). One was a sample of Wimmera rye grass (*Lolium subulatum*, an imported Australian annual) at the flowering stage from Bopatalawa Farm. The soil on this farm is an acid peaty loam and burnt lime at the rate of 1—2 tons' acre is generally applied prior to the establishment of pasture. This sample was from a limed area. The last was a sample of maize fodder from an upcountry district.

It will be seen that of these samples, Wimmera rye grass contains appreciably less protein than the other two. It has also more fibre than the broad leaf carpet grass. In regard to mineral composition, the rye grass is richer in phosphorus than the carpet grass species, especially the narrow leaf one. Its calcium content is higher than that of the broad leaf species of carpet grass and slightly less than that of the narrow leaf species.

Compared with British pasture grasses (3), these samples are poorer in lime, phosphorus and protein than one of good cultivated British pasture. They are similar in composition to poor natural pasture in Britain except in regard to calcium in which constituent two of our species are of similar quality to good natural pasture. The analyses show, however, that these grasses are superior in composition to South African natural pasture grasses (4). Compared with Australian data for Wimmera rye grass, the Bopatalawa sample is lower in protein and lime and slightly higher in phosphates than most Australian samples of the same stage of maturity (5).

### RUBBER RESEARCH SCHEME (CEYLON).

#### MINUTES OF THE SEVENTY-FIFTH MEETING OF THE RUBBER RESEARCH BOARD HELD AT THE SECRETARIAT, COLOMBO, AT 2.30 P.M. ON MONDAY, JUNE 11, 1945.

*Present.*—Mr. L. J. de S. Seneviratne, C.C.S. (in the Chair), Col. C. J. D. Lanktree, C.C.S. (Acting Deputy Financial Secretary), Mr. T. Amarasuriya, M.S.C., Mr. W. N. de Alwis, Mr. W. P. H. Dias, J.P., Mr. A. M. Clement Dias, Mr. F. J. C. de Mel, Mr. R. P. Gaddum, Mr. F. H. Griffith, M.S.C., Mr. R. J. Hartley and Mr. R. C. Kannangara, M.S.C.

Mr. M. W. Philpott, Acting Director, was present by invitation.

An apology for absence was received from Mr. L. P. Gapp.

#### 1. MINUTES

(a) *Confirmation.*—Draft minutes of the meeting held on March 12, 1945, which had been circulated to members, were confirmed and signed by the Chairman.

(b) *Appointment of Research Assistants.*—The financial position in connection with the proposal to appoint 4 research assistants was considered and it was agreed that steps be taken to make the appointments.

#### 2. BOARD

The Chairman reported the following changes in the membership of the Board since the last meeting :—

- (a) Messrs. A. M. Clement Dias and F. J. C. de Mel had been nominated by the Low-Country Products Association for three years from May 19, 1945, in place of Messrs. W. N. Gunawardena and J. L. D. Peiris, whose periods of membership had expired.
- (b) Mr. W. H. Attfield had resigned and Mr. R. P. Gaddum had been nominated in his place by the Ceylon Estates Proprietary Association with effect from May 2, 1945.
- (c) Mr. W. P. H. Dias had been renominated as a representative of small holders for a further period of three years from June 10, 1945.

A vote of appreciation of the services of the outgoing members was passed.

#### 3. EXPERIMENTAL COMMITTEE

(a) *Membership.*—Agreed that Mr. F. J. C. de Mel should act for Mr. R. C. L. Notley as a member of the Committee.

(b) *Recommendation made at meeting held on April 30, 1945 :—*

*Brown Bast K. P.*—The recommendation that a Brown Bast K. P. be appointed at Dartonfield on the approved salary scale was approved.

(c) *Recommendations made at meeting held on June 11, 1945 :—*

*Tenders for buildings and Hedigalla cart road.*—As recommended by the Committee, a sub-committee was appointed to consider the tenders and award suitable contracts.

Arrangements for supervision of construction of the buildings and cart road were decided on.

Dias Pieris & Co., in connection with the decline of crops in some of the Scheme's experimental blocks on that estate. The Agents had recommended the termination of the experiment, but the Director of Research had met them and, as the closing down of the experiment was untimely, had suggested a satisfactory arrangement whereby the Board might consider the payment of an amount, calculated annually, by way of compensation for loss of crops due to experimental treatment. After a full discussion, in which the views of Mr. Stanley Dias, a partner of Messrs. Dias Pieris & Co. were obtained, the Board agreed to this arrangement.

#### 7. GENERAL.

(a) *Memorandum on the Future of the Coconut Research Scheme.*—The unanimous approval of the Board had been obtained by the circulation of papers for the distribution of the Memorandum as a printed document and the estimate for printing of Rs. 375 had also received the Board's sanction. This charge was to be met from the Printing and Advertising Vote.

The Chairman referred to the Method of Providing Funds proposed in the Memorandum, viz., by means of a cess on exported coconut products based on  $12\frac{1}{2}$  cents per 1,000 nuts, supplemented by a Government contribution varying in such a manner as to ensure a total annual income to the Scheme of Rs. 150,000 and suggested that alternative proposals should be put forward in the Memorandum to ensure the whole of the required annual income solely by means of an export cess without having recourse to the present annual Government grant or to an

110

increased and varying grant as proposed in the Memorandum. The Board agreed with this view and instructed the Director of Research to evaluate the amounts of such increased cess and insert the Chairman's suggestion in its appropriate place in the Memorandum.

(b) *Training of Students.*—Circulation Paper No. 352, embodying the correspondence that had passed between the Hon. Minister for Agriculture and Lands, the Chairman and the Director of Research, was discussed and it was resolved that the Chairman should inform the Hon. Minister that the Coconut Research Scheme, was not at present in a position, with the funds at its disposal, to undertake the training of students at its Research Stations, but that arrangements for such training of students could be made if the financial status of the Scheme were improved as proposed in the Memorandum on the future of the Coconut Research Scheme or, earlier, if special financial assistance for the purpose were to be provided by the Government.

(c) *Issue of Publications.*—A letter from the Secretary, Planters' Association, Kandy, forwarding a resolution of the Chilaw Planters' Association, relating to the necessity for giving wider publicity to the Scheme's work, and a reply thereto by the Director of Research were tabled. It was resolved that all suggestions of this sort from interested bodies should be collected and considered in relation to the Memorandum on the Future of Coconut Research Scheme.

#### 8. ANY OTHER BUSINESS.

(a) *Mr. Graham Pandittesekera's resolution regarding the Hot Air Drier at Ratmalgara Estate.*—All available information on the questions raised by Mr. Pandittesekera with regard to the working of the Hot Air Drier at Ratmalgara had been circulated, (C.P.536), and Mr. Pandittesekera was satisfied that the quality of the copra at present produced therein was not satisfactory and much inferior to the copra produced in the "Pearson Patent" Kiln at Bandirippuwa Estate. Mr. Pandittesekera was, however, desirous that every possible effort should be made to improve the design of the Ratmalgara kiln in order to increase its output of which copra or, failing that, to devise another kiln of cheap construction to enable small-holders to produce this superior grade of copra which commanded a very high price before the war. It was agreed that the matter was worthy of further consideration.

Rainfall in May was exceptionally low, particularly in the south-western parts, where many stations which normally record 20 to 30 inches received only about a third of that amount. Highest monthly totals were 21.80 inches at Kokkawita Group, 20.71 inches at Gikiyanakande Estate and 20.31 inches at Pelenda, while a majority of stations in this region received less than 10 inches. In other parts of the Island the monthly totals were generally below two inches, there being no rain at all in the north-western parts and in Jaffna Peninsula.

At several stations in the south-west quarter this month's rainfall was the lowest on record for May. Thus 3.21 inches at Dunedin Estate is the lowest May total for this station for the last 62 years, the previous lowest being 3.80 inches in 1934. Similarly 7.77 inches at Avissawella Estate is the lowest for 48 years, 0.11 inch at Keenagaha Ella Estate, the lowest for 38 years, and 10.93 inches at Carney Estate, the lowest for 31 years.

There was accordingly a marked deficiency in rainfall in the south-western parts, deficits at a large number of stations exceeding 10 inches. The highest deficits were 21.52 inches at Carney Estate, 20.48 inches at Norton Bridge and 20.44 at Watawaa.

The acuteness of the water shortage experienced in May was due to the fact that in certain parts of the Island, particularly in the southern and northern parts of the central hills and in the south-western foot-hills there was a continuous deficiency of rainfall for the last six months, *i.e.*, December, 1944, to May, 1945. Stations thus affected include Maskeliya, Watagoda, Hakgala, Galaha (Kirimetiya estate), Elkaduwa, Dehiwita (Digalla estate), Labugama, and Kalawana (Kokkawita Group).

Rainfall has been a little above normal at a few stations on the lee side in the Province of Uva and in Batticaloa District, the largest excesses being 3.64 inches at Viragoda, 3.00 inches at Ohiya and 2.84 inches at Walton estate.

There were twelve daily falls of over five inches for the month, the highest being 8.76 inches at Gonapenigala estate and 8.40 inches at Dolabodakanda estate on the 4th.

Although a moderate south-westerly pressure gradient was in evidence about the middle of the month, it showed no tendency to develop. The usual heavy rains in the south-west of the Island during May that mark the advent of the south-west monsoon were absent this year.

Temperatures were above average by day and generally about normal by night. The highest recorded shade temperature was 98.8° at Trincomalee on the 17th and 21st, while the lowest air temperature was 40.4° at Nuwara Eliya on the 9th. Day humidities were distributed on either side of average, and night humidities were slightly below normal. Cloud amounts were in deficit. Winds were mainly south-westerly.

Hailstorms were reported from Woodside estate on the 25th and Kabaragalle estate on the 30th.
